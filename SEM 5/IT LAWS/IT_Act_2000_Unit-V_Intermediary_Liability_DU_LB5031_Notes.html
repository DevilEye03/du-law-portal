🎯 THE ONE IDEA THAT CARRIES THIS ENTIRE UNIT

Every platform you use — a search engine, a social network, an e‑commerce marketplace, a blog host, an ISP, a cyber café — is an intermediary: a conduit for third‑party content that it did not create. Section 79 of the IT Act, 2000 grants such entities a conditional safe harbour: immunity from liability for what other people put on their systems, in exchange for (i) a genuinely passive role, and (ii) due diligence — publishing lawful‑content policies and removing unlawful material promptly once it acquires actual knowledge. The safe harbour is lost the moment the entity conspires, abets, aids or induces the unlawful act, or fails to act on actual knowledge.

  • s.2(1)(w) defines "intermediary" — an inclusive list running from telecom and ISPs to search engines, online‑payment and auction sites, online market places and cyber cafés.
  • s.79(1) is the safe harbour; s.79(2) sets its three preconditions; s.79(3) sets its two disqualifiers.
  • "Actual knowledge" was read down in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, (2015) 5 SCC 1, to mean a court order or a notification by the appropriate Government or its agency — a private complaint alone does not forfeit the safe harbour.
  • Due diligence is spelled out in the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, as amended in 2022, 2023, 2025 and 2026 — now including a 3‑hour takedown clock, a 2‑hour clock for non‑consensual intimate imagery, and mandatory labelling and provenance metadata for synthetically generated information (deepfakes).
  • s.72A separately punishes an intermediary (or anyone) who discloses personal information in breach of a lawful contract; s.69A supplies the parallel, tightly safeguarded blocking power.

If you remember one sentence for the exam: Section 79 is an affirmative defence, not a blanket immunity — always run the three‑gate test (passive role → due diligence → no actual knowledge/conspiracy) before concluding that a platform is protected, and remember that "actual knowledge" means a court order or a Government notification, not a private notice.

📍 Five things to keep straight before you start
  • s.79 is an affirmative defence, not an immunity. The Delhi High Court in My Space Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. put it exactly: s.79 "is neither an enforcement provision nor does it list out any penal consequences for non‑compliance. It sets up a scheme where intermediaries have to follow certain minimum standards to avoid liability; it provides for an affirmative defence and not a blanket immunity from liability." The plaintiff must first establish a wrong; only then does the defendant invoke s.79.
  • The Rules are where the real content is. s.79 says "due diligence" and "such other guidelines as the Central Government may prescribe" but does not define them. The 2011 Intermediaries Guidelines Rules did so first; they were superseded by the 2021 Rules, which have since been amended five times (28 Oct 2022, 6 Apr 2023, 22 Oct 2025, 10 Feb 2026 and 30 Mar 2026). Never cite the 2011 Rules as current law.
  • "Actual knowledge" ≠ a private complaint. This is the single most‑tested point in the Unit. After Shreya Singhal, the obligation to remove arises on a court order or a Government/agency notification. But note the important copyright exception in MySpace: in a s.51(a)(ii) Copyright Act claim, a specific, work‑identifying notice from the rights‑holder is enough — no court order needed.
  • Copyright gets its own rule. s.81's proviso preserves Copyright Act and Patents Act rights, and s.79 must be read harmoniously with it. MySpace is the definitive authority: the proviso does not oust the safe harbour, but a catalogue‑wide, prospective injunction is impermissible — the remedy is specific notice‑and‑takedown within 36 hours.
  • Passive versus active is now a live, unsettled question. The Single Judge in Amway v. 1Mg held that an e‑commerce platform providing warehousing, logistics, payment collection and returns was not a passive intermediary. The Division Bench in Amazon Seller Services v. Modicare set that aside: s.79 draws no distinction between passive and active intermediaries. Learn both — the contrast is a guaranteed "critically examine" question.

1Who is an "intermediary"? — the s.2(1)(w) definition

Syllabus item 1  Everything in this Unit turns on a definitional question that is prior to the law of liability: is the entity in the dock an intermediary at all? If it is, s.79 offers a safe harbour. If it is not — if it is a publisher, an originator, or an active participant in the unlawful act — the safe harbour never engages and ordinary civil and criminal liability applies. Google India Pvt. Ltd. v. Vishakha Industries (2019) and Amazon Seller Services v. Modicare (2020) both turned, at bottom, on this prior question.

1.1 The statutory text

s.2(1)(w)

"Intermediary"

"intermediary, with respect to any particular electronic records, means any person who on behalf of another person receives, stores or transmits that record or provides any service with respect to that record and includes telecom service providers, network service providers, internet service providers, web‑hosting service providers, search engines, online‑payment sites, online‑auction sites, online‑market places and cyber cafes."
Read the opening words carefully: "with respect to any particular electronic records" — the definition is record‑specific. An entity can be an intermediary for one record and a publisher for another. That is precisely why the courts hold that intermediary status is a question of fact for trial, not a plea to be decided on pleadings. The list is inclusive ("includes"), not exhaustive — a new species of platform is caught by the functional test even if unlisted.
s.2(1)(na)

"Cyber café"

"cyber café means any facility from where access to the internet is offered by any person in the ordinary course of business to the members of the public."
Why it matters: cyber cafés are the one category in the s.2(1)(w) list that the legislature took the trouble to define separately. It did so because a cyber café sells access, not content — it is a pure conduit, and therefore the paradigm case of a passive intermediary, yet also the classic vector for anonymous misuse. Parliament's answer was the IT (Guidelines for Cyber Café) Rules, 2011.

1.2 The four functional limbs — break the definition into parts

Examiners reward an answer that unpacks s.2(1)(w) rather than reciting it. The definition has a general limb (the functional test) and an illustrative limb (the named list). The general limb has four alternative triggers, any one of which suffices — and a fifth requirement that runs through all of them:

#LimbWhat it capturesIndian illustration
0"on behalf of another person"The third‑party requirement. The intermediary acts for someone else, not for itself. This is the doctrinal heart of the safe harbour: the entity is not the author of the content.A blog host stores a blogger's post; the host is not the blogger.

📜 The critical point about "transmitted", "temporarily stored" and "hosted" — s.79(2)(a)

The My Space Division Bench drew a distinction that students routinely miss: under s.79(2)(a) the words are "transmitted or temporarily stored or hosted". Parliament did not distinguish between kinds of transmission — live streaming and stored data are both covered — but it did leave room for an argument about permanent storage. MySpace urged that permanent storage is simply how the internet works and must fall within the section. The Court accepted that storage is "an essential part of the transmission/hosting process" and that the platform only "provides access to [a] system to third parties enabling transmission of information". Result: a platform that hosts user uploads indefinitely still qualifies as an intermediary — the differentiator is not duration but knowledge and due diligence.

💡 The unlisted platform

Because the list is inclusive, a platform that did not exist in 2000 is still caught if it satisfies the functional test. A cloud storage service stores; a content delivery network transmits and caches; a messaging app transmits; a video‑sharing platform hosts; a dating app provides a service with respect to records; an online gaming intermediary (expressly added by the 2023 amendment to Rule 3) provides a service. The 2021 Rules make the point structurally by naming "social media intermediary", "significant social media intermediary" and "online gaming intermediary" — all sub‑species of the s.2(1)(w) genus.

1.4 Who is not an intermediary — the four disqualifying patterns

Pattern 1 · The originator
  • The person who creates the unlawful content is the author, not a conduit. He is liable under the substantive law (defamation, obscenity, copyright infringement, s.67 IT Act) and gets no shelter from s.79.
  • Example: A‑1 in Google India v. Visaka — the coordinator of the "Ban Asbestos India" Google Group who wrote the articles. He was Accused No. 1; Google India was Accused No. 2. The two occupy entirely different legal positions.
Pattern 2 · The publisher who selects or modifies
  • An entity that initiates the transmission, selects the receiver, or selects or modifies the information in the transmission fails s.79(2)(b) and steps outside the safe harbour.
  • But note the crucial qualification from My Space: automated, machine‑driven format conversion and advertisement insertion, with no human intervention, is not "modification" and does not defeat the safe harbour at the prima facie stage. Only where the software requires a human approval can knowledge be attributed — and that is a trial question.
Pattern 3 · The conspirator, abettor or inducer
  • s.79(3)(a) disqualifies the intermediary that has conspired, abetted, aided or induced the unlawful act — whether by threats, promise or otherwise. This is the provision invoked where a platform's business model is itself built on the unlawful content.
  • Example: In Amazon v. Modicare the Single Judge found that the platforms "offer a comfortable refuge for parties breaching their contracts… This refuge by itself constitutes inducement." The Division Bench disagreed — inducement requires active efforts, not mere knowledge.
Pattern 4 · The wrong group entity
  • The safe harbour belongs to the entity that actually hosts or transmits. A domestic subsidiary is not automatically the intermediary for a global platform operated by its foreign parent.
  • In Google India v. Vishakha the appellant argued it was not the intermediary at all — the intermediary was Google LLC. The Supreme Court rejected the contention that the High Court should have accepted this at the threshold and held it to be a matter for trial. Practical lesson: group structure is not an exit from Indian jurisdiction.

⚠️ The single most common error in this Unit

Students treat "is it an intermediary?" as a question of what the platform calls itself. It is not. It is a question of function, decided record by record, on evidence, usually at trial. The Supreme Court in Google India v. Vishakha expressly refused to decide it at the quashing stage. In an exam, always write: "Whether the appellant is an intermediary for the impugned record is a mixed question of fact and law, to be determined at trial; the High Court erred only in treating it as concluded at the threshold."

1.6 Extraterritorial reach — s.75 and the targeting test

📜 s.75 — Act to apply for offence or contravention committed outside India

s.75(1) extends the Act to any offence or contravention committed outside India by any person, provided the computer, computer system or computer resource involved is located in India. s.75(2) is the crucial proviso for this Unit: it requires the Central Government to notify intermediaries (and a class of intermediaries) for the purpose. In X v. Union of India (2021) the Delhi High Court relied on precisely this structure when it held that the IT Act carries "extraterritorial jurisdiction and overriding application" so long as the computer resource is located in India, and when it reasoned that a search engine can, with comparative ease, implement global de‑indexing (citing Google Inc. v. Equustek Solutions, 2017 SCC 34).

Practical consequence: a foreign platform is not beyond reach merely because it is offshore. The doctrinal filter is the targeting / purposeful availment test developed in Banyan Tree Holding v. A. Murali Krishna Reddy (covered in Unit IX): mere accessibility of a website in India is not enough; there must be a deliberate targeting of the Indian market. MySpace's India‑centric website, its India office and its India‑specific operations were exactly the kind of facts that established that targeting.

2Cyber cafés — s.2(1)(na) and the cyber café regime

Syllabus item 2  The syllabus names cyber cafés separately from intermediaries, and the legislature defined them separately in s.2(1)(na). The reason is structural: a cyber café is the purest conduit in the Act — it sells access, authors nothing, selects no audience — and it is simultaneously the classic vector for anonymous misuse. The regulatory answer is a dedicated set of rules imposing identity, logging and reporting duties.

2.1 The definition and the three elements

s.2(1)(na)

Cyber café — the three elements

"cyber café means any facility from where access to the internet is offered by any person in the ordinary course of business to the members of the public."
(1) A facility — physical premises with computer resources; (2) offering access to the internet — not content, not hosting, but connectivity; (3) in the ordinary course of business to members of the public — a commercial, open‑to‑all operation. Note the contrast with a corporate office LAN (not open to the public), a school computer lab (not "in the ordinary course of business"), and an ISP's own node (already covered as an ISP).
Why separately defined?

The legislative logic

Anonymity plus impersonality. A cyber café user is faceless to the state and to the victim: he pays cash, sits at a shared terminal and leaves. Every other intermediary has some identifying hook — an ISP has a subscriber, a marketplace has a registered seller, a social network has an account. A cyber café has none. The legislature therefore attached identity‑verification, logging and reporting obligations to cyber cafés specifically, on top of the general s.79 due diligence that applies to them as intermediaries.

2.2 The IT (Guidelines for Cyber Café) Rules, 2011

The cyber café regime is not in the 2011 Intermediaries Guidelines Rules. It is a separate notification — the Information Technology (Guidelines for Cyber Café) Rules, 2011, framed under s.87 — and it is the "other order or rule" that the syllabus's cross‑reference to "due diligence" necessarily imports. Its principal obligations are:

🔍 Holistic reading — where the cyber café rules sit in the wider architecture
  • s.2(1)(na) + s.2(1)(w): a cyber café is both a defined category and an intermediary. It therefore owes the full s.79 due diligence — publish terms, inform users not to host unlawful content, act on actual knowledge — in addition to the identity and logging duties.
  • s.67C IT Act (Unit IV): an intermediary must preserve information for 90 days for investigation when called upon. The cyber café log register (one year) is the operational mirror of that statutory duty.
  • Rule 3(1)(d), 2021 Rules: the 36‑hour (now, for court/Government orders, 3‑hour) takedown clock applies to cyber cafés too — but in practice a café cannot "take down" content it did not host, so its compliance burden is disclosure and preservation, not removal.
  • s.72A (§7 below): a cyber café operator who hands over a user's browsing history or personal data otherwise than in breach of a lawful contract escapes s.72A; one who sells or leaks it does not.
  • Privacy counterpoint: the logging regime has been criticised as disproportionate — it records the browsing of all users to catch a few, raising Art. 21 questions post‑Puttaswamy. A good "critically examine" answer flags the trade‑off explicitly.
💡 Real example — how a cyber café prosecution is actually built

In 2011, an obscene image is sent from a cyber café in Kanpur to a journalist's e‑mail. The trail: (1) the recipient's ISP produces the sender's IP address; (2) that IP resolves to a registered cyber café under the 2011 Rules; (3) the café's log register fixes the date, time and identity of the user; (4) the proxy logs and browsing backups corroborate; (5) the monthly report filed with the licensing agency independently confirms the entry; and (6) the electronic record is proved through a s.65B Evidence Act / s.63 BSA certificate. The café itself is liable only if it failed its own duties — identity, logging, or refusal to disclose on lawful demand — because as a pure conduit it is protected by s.79 for the content the user created.

📝 Likely short‑note formulation

"A cyber café is a facility offering internet access to the public in the ordinary course of business (s.2(1)(na)). Being expressly included in the s.2(1)(w) list, it is an intermediary and owes s.79 due diligence; in addition, the IT (Guidelines for Cyber Café) Rules, 2011 require registration with a designated agency, verification of every user's identity (with a photograph where identity cannot be proved), a log register maintained for one year, monthly reports to the licensing agency, and inspection by a police officer not below the rank of inspector. The regime sacrifices a measure of user privacy for traceability, and is to be read with s.67C preservation duties and s.72A's prohibition on disclosure in breach of a lawful contract."

3Exemption from liability — the s.79 safe harbour

Syllabus item 3  Section 79 is the heart of this Unit. It is the provision that makes the modern internet possible in India — no platform could survive if it were answerable, as author, for every word posted by its users — and it is also the provision that is most often misdescribed in answers as "absolute immunity". It is nothing of the kind. It is a conditional, affirmative defence.

3.1 The statutory text — the current (post‑2008) version

📜 CHAPTER XII — INTERMEDIARIES NOT TO BE LIABLE IN CERTAIN CASES

79. Exemption from liability of intermediary in certain cases. — (1) Notwithstanding anything contained in any law for the time being in force but subject to the provisions of sub‑sections (2) and (3), an intermediary shall not be liable for any third party information, data, or communication link made available or hosted by him.

(2) The provisions of sub‑section (1) shall apply if —
  (a) the function of the intermediary is limited to providing access to a communication system over which information made available by third parties is transmitted or temporarily stored or hosted; or
  (b) the intermediary does not —
    (i) initiate the transmission,
    (ii) select the receiver of the transmission, and
    (iii) select or modify the information contained in the transmission;
  (c) the intermediary observes due diligence while discharging his duties under this Act and also observes such other guidelines as the Central Government may prescribe in this behalf.

(3) The provisions of sub‑section (1) shall not apply if —
  (a) the intermediary has conspired or abetted or aided or induced, whether by threats or promise or otherwise, in the commission of the unlawful act; or
  (b) upon receiving actual knowledge, or on being notified by the appropriate Government or its agency that any information, data or communication link residing in or connected to a computer resource controlled by the intermediary is being used to commit the unlawful act, the intermediary fails to expeditiously remove or disable access to that material on that resource without vitiating the evidence in any manner.

Explanation. — For the purposes of this section, the expression "third party information" means any information dealt with by an intermediary in his capacity as an intermediary.

📜 The pre‑2008 text — printed in the DU case material (Google India v. Visaka Industries, AP HC, 2011)

"CHAPTER XII — NETWORK SERVICE PROVIDERS NOT TO BE LIABLE IN CERTAIN CASES
79. Network service providers not to be liable in certain cases: For the removal of doubts, it is hereby declared that no person providing any service as a network service provider shall be liable under this Act, rules or regulations made thereunder for any third party information or data made available by him if he proves that the offence or contravention was committed without his knowledge or that he had exercised all due diligence to prevent the commission of such offence or contravention.
Explanation. — For the purposes of this section, (a) "network service provider" means an intermediary; (b) "third party information" means any information dealt with by a network service provider in his capacity as an intermediary."

Three differences that examiners love: (i) the original protected only against liability "under this Act, rules or regulations made thereunder" — it did not touch liability under the IPC or any other law; (ii) it placed the burden on the intermediary to prove absence of knowledge or exercise of due diligence — a trial question; (iii) it used "network service provider" as the operative term. The 2008 Amendment (in force 27 October 2009) added the non‑obstante clause, thereby extending the shield to liability under any law, and substituted the s.79(2)/(3) architecture. This is exactly why the AP High Court in 2011 could hold Google India liable: the articles dated 31 July 2008 and 21 November 2008, and the complaint of January 2009, all pre‑dated the amendment.

3.2 The three gates of s.79(2) — and how they interact

s.79(2) is drafted as a disjunctive set of alternatives, joined by "or". The Division Bench in Amazon Seller Services v. Modicare (2020) supplied the definitive reading of how the gates interact:

GateRequirementWho must satisfy itThe Modicare reading

"The exemption under Section 79(1) of the IT Act from liability applies when the intermediaries fulfil the criteria laid down in either Section 79(2)(a) or Section 79(2)(b), and Section 79(2)(c) of the IT Act. Where the intermediary merely provides access, it has to comply with Section 79(2)(a), whereas in instances where it provides services in addition to access, it has to comply with Section 79(2)(b) of the IT Act."— S. And on the passive/active question: "In terms of Section 79 of the IT Act, there does not appear to be any distinction between passive and active intermediaries so far as the availability of the safe harbour provisions are concerned."

💡 The My Space illustration of Gate B in operation

The Division Bench applied s.79(2)(b) limb by limb: (i) initiation — MySpace created a "share" option, but "the usage of that feature rests purely in the hands of third party users", so at the prima facie stage it did not initiate transmission; (ii) receiver — "anyone with Internet access can open its website and be a receiver", so it selected no receiver; (iii) selection/modification — the modification was "to the format and not to the content", and even that was "an automatic process without either MySpace's tacit or expressed control or knowledge". Conclusion: MySpace prima facie complied with s.79(2)(b). This three‑step application is the model to reproduce in an exam answer.

3.3 The two disqualifiers of s.79(3)

s.79(3)(a) — the culpable‑participation disqualifier
  • Triggered by conspiracy, abetment, aiding or inducement — "whether by threats or promise or otherwise".
  • This is the provision that catches a platform whose business model is the unlawful content: a site whose revenue depends on piracy, or which actively solicits infringing uploads.
  • It is a mens rea disqualifier — the safe harbour is lost by culpable participation, not by volume or by knowledge alone.
  • In Google India v. Vishakha (SC, 2019) the Court noted that s.79 as substituted "denies benefit of exemption in case where there is conspiracy between intermediary or abetting or aiding or inducing or otherwise in commission of unlawful act", and held that where the complaint alleges connivance, the matter cannot be proceeded with under s.482 Cr.P.C.
s.79(3)(b) — the failure‑to‑act disqualifier
  • Triggered by actual knowledge (or Government/agency notification) plus a failure to act expeditiously, and the removal must be effected "without vitiating the evidence in any manner".
  • Both elements are essential: knowledge without inaction does not forfeit the safe harbour, and neither does inaction without the requisite knowledge.
  • The evidence‑preservation limb is frequently overlooked and is mark‑earning: an intermediary that deletes content and logs in a way that destroys evidence does not comply, even if it acts fast. This pairs with s.67C and with the 90‑day preservation duty in Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules.
  • "Actual knowledge" is read down — see §6 below.

3.5 What the non‑obstante clause does — and what it does not do

✅ What s.79(1) does
  • Post‑2008, it shields the intermediary from liability under "any law for the time being in force" — the IPC/BNS, the Copyright Act, the Trade Marks Act, the IT Act itself — for third‑party information, data or communication links.
  • It creates an affirmative defence that the defendant pleads and must bring itself within.
  • It is subject only to s.79(2) and s.79(3) — those are the only restrictions written into the section.
❌ What s.79(1) does not do
  • It does not protect the intermediary's own content. If the platform itself posts, it is a publisher.
  • It does not create a cause of action. It is a shield, not a sword: "Section 79 is neither an enforcement provision nor does it list out any penal consequences for non‑compliance."
  • It does not survive a failure of due diligence. Non‑compliance with the platform's own published policy takes it outside the safe harbour (Amway v. 1Mg, Single Judge).
  • It does not oust the Copyright Act or Patents Act — s.81's proviso expressly preserves those rights (see §9 below).
⚠️ Burden of proof — a point that decides problem questions

In Amazon v. Modicare the Division Bench held that the plaintiffs had wrongly sought to enforce s.79 positively against the platforms: "Section 79 of the IT Act has been, contrary to the judgment in MySpace Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd., sought to be enforced by the Plaintiffs positively, rather than be deployed as 'affirmative defence'." The correct sequence is: (1) the plaintiff must first show a violation of its rights; (2) only then does the defendant invoke s.79; (3) only then does the court test s.79(2) and (3). Writing this sequence out earns marks, because most answers collapse steps 1 and 2.

📝 The four‑line answer to "Explain the safe harbour under s.79"

(1) s.79(1) — notwithstanding any law, an intermediary is not liable for third‑party information, data or communication links made available or hosted by it. (2) This is conditional: s.79(2)(a) or (b) (passive role) and s.79(2)(c) (due diligence under the prescribed guidelines). (3) It is forfeited under s.79(3)(a) (conspiracy, abetment, aiding, inducement) or s.79(3)(b) (actual knowledge plus failure to expeditiously remove, preserving evidence). (4) "Actual knowledge" means a court order or a Government/agency notification (Shreya Singhal), except in copyright claims where a specific, work‑identifying notice suffices (My Space). s.79 is an affirmative defence, not a blanket immunity.

📜 Rule 3(3) and Rule 3(4), 2011 Rules — the operative duties, as struck down in part by Shreya Singhal

Rule 3(3): "The intermediary shall not knowingly host or publish any information or shall not initiate the transmission, select the receiver of transmission, and select or modify the information contained in the transmission as specified in sub‑rule (2)." The proviso then excludes from "hosting, publishing, editing or storing" two kinds of conduct: (a) temporary, transient or intermediate storage automatically within the computer resource as an intrinsic feature, involving no human editorial control, for onward transmission; and (b) removal of access to information after it comes to the actual knowledge of a person authorised by the intermediary pursuant to any order or direction under the Act.

Rule 3(4): "The intermediary, on whose computer system the information is stored or hosted or published, upon obtaining knowledge by itself or been brought to actual knowledge by an affected person in writing or through email signed with electronic signature about any such information as mentioned in sub‑rule (2) above, shall act within thirty‑six hours and where applicable, work with user or owner of such information to disable such information that is in contravention of sub‑rule (2). Further the intermediary shall preserve such information and associated records for at least ninety days for investigation purposes."
Shreya Singhal read down Rule 3(4): "The knowledge spoken of in the said sub‑rule must only be through the medium of a court order. Subject to this, the Information Technology (Intermediaries Guidelines) Rules, 2011 are valid."

4.6 A practical due‑diligence checklist — what "compliance" actually looks like

✅ What a compliant intermediary does
  • Publishes rules, privacy policy and user agreement prominently, in English or an Eighth‑Schedule language.
  • Its user agreement reproduces the Rule 3(1)(b) prohibited‑content categories.
  • Appoints a Grievance Officer, publishes contact details, acknowledges in 24 hours and resolves in 7 days.
  • Maintains a monitored channel capable of acting on a court order or Government notification within 3 hours.
  • Maintains a 2‑hour workflow for NCII complaints.
  • Preserves removed content and associated records for 90 days (and in accordance with s.67C).
  • Deploys automated detection, labelling and provenance for SGI.
  • Files monthly compliance reports if it is an SSMI (see §5).
❌ What forfeits the safe harbour
  • No published terms, or terms that do not track Rule 3(1)(b).
  • Publishing a policy and then not following it — the Amway v. 1Mg point: non‑compliance with the platform's own policies takes it outside the safe harbour.
  • Failing to act on a court order or Government notification within the prescribed clock.
  • Removing content in a way that vitiates evidence — s.79(3)(b) expressly forbids this.
  • Any conspiracy, abetment, aiding or inducement of the unlawful act.
  • Human editorial selection or modification of the transmitted information (s.79(2)(b)(iii)).
  • Knowingly promoting, hosting or allowing the continued circulation of harmful SGI after becoming aware of it.

5Significant Social Media Intermediaries & Part III — the Digital Media Ethics Code

Syllabus item 5  The 2021 Rules introduced a two‑tier structure: baseline due diligence for every intermediary (Rule 3), and enhanced obligations for Significant Social Media Intermediaries (Rule 4) — a category that exists only because of scale. Part III adds a separate code for digital news publishers, curated‑content platforms and OTT services. This is the material for the classic question: "Critically examine the due diligence obligations of SSMIs."

5.1 What makes an intermediary "significant"

Rule 2(1)(v)

"Significant social media intermediary"

A social media intermediary with more than 50 lakh (5 million) registered users in India, as notified by the Central Government. (A "social media intermediary" is one whose primary or principal function is to enable interaction between two or more users and which allows them to create, upload, share, disseminate, modify or access information using its services.)
The rationale is scale. The obligations in Rule 4 are expensive — compliance officers, traceability infrastructure, monthly reporting. Imposing them on a neighbourhood forum would be disproportionate. The threshold concentrates the burden where the harm is greatest.
Rule 4 — the six obligations

The SSMI compliance architecture

(1) Chief Compliance Officer — an India‑resident senior employee responsible for compliance with the Act and the Rules, and liable for proceedings if the intermediary fails to observe due diligence.
(2) Nodal Contact Person — available 24×7 for coordination with law enforcement, also India‑resident.
(3) Resident Grievance Officer — India‑resident, the point of contact for users; the grievance clock runs against this officer.
(4) Traceability — enable identification of the first originator of information on its platform, on the order of a court or of a competent authority under s.69(3), for specified serious offences.
(5) Voluntary user verification — provide a mechanism for users to obtain a voluntary verification mark.
(6) Monthly compliance report — publish a monthly report of complaints received and action taken.

5.3 The traceability mandate — the most contested obligation in the Rules

⚖️ The debate, in the form an examiner wants

For the mandate: it is the only way to identify the originator of a message that has been forwarded thousands of times — indispensable for investigating rape threats, lynching incitement, child sexual abuse material and terrorist content. Without it, only the last forwarder is ever identified.

Against it: on end‑to‑end encrypted platforms, traceability requires breaking or weakening encryption for everyone, which is a direct Art. 21 privacy intrusion post‑K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) 10 SCC 1, and which the Supreme Court in Shreya Singhal was plainly unwilling to contemplate. The litigation (WhatsApp v. Union of India, Delhi High Court, pending as of 2026) remains unresolved at the Supreme Court level. A first‑class answer states the mandate, the privacy objection, and the unresolved status — and notes that traceability is triggered only by a court order or a s.69(3) competent authority, not by administrative fiat.

5.4 Part III — the Digital Media Ethics Code

Part III applies not to intermediaries generally but to publishers of news and current affairs content, publishers of online curated content, and online curated content providers (OTT platforms). Its grievance mechanism is a three‑tier structure:

📝 Why Part III belongs in a Unit V answer

Part III is frequently omitted by students because it sits in the same Rules as s.79 due diligence but regulates a different class of entity. The syllabus names the "Digital Media Ethics Code" expressly, so an answer on the 2021 Rules must mention it — and must make the distinction: Rule 3 and Rule 4 govern intermediaries who host third‑party content; Part III governs entities that are themselves publishers of news, curated content or OTT programming. The overlap point — a social media platform's own news feed — is exactly where the two regimes meet, and where the deepest free‑speech objections lie.

6"Actual knowledge" — the Shreya Singhal reading‑down

Syllabus item 6  If you learn one doctrinal move in this Unit, learn this one. Section 79(3)(b), read literally, would require an intermediary to take down content the moment anyone complains about it. The Supreme Court held that this would force platforms into the role of unaccountable private censors, and read the provision down.

6.1 The problem the Court identified

🔍 The Court's reasoning, in its own words
"It must first be appreciated that Section 79 is an exemption provision. Being an exemption provision, it is closely related to provisions which provide for offences including Section 69A. We have seen how under Section 69A blocking can take place only by a reasoned order after complying with several procedural safeguards including a hearing to the originator and intermediary… The intermediary applying its own mind to whether information should or should not be blocked is noticeably absent in Section 69A read with 2009 Rules."— Nariman J., Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, W.P. (Crl.) No. 167 of 2012 (24 March 2015)

The Court then gave the practical objection, which is the one to quote: "otherwise it would be very difficult for intermediaries like Google, Facebook etc. to act when millions of requests are made and the intermediary is then to judge as to which of such requests are legitimate and which are not."

6.2 The holding — verbatim

📜 Section 79(3)(b), as read down

"Section 79(3)(b) has to be read down to mean that the intermediary upon receiving actual knowledge that a court order has been passed asking it to expeditiously remove or disable access to certain material must then fail to expeditiously remove or disable access to that material… Also, the Court order and/or the notification by the appropriate Government or its agency must strictly conform to the subject matters laid down in Article 19(2). Unlawful acts beyond what is laid down in Article 19(2) obviously cannot form any part of Section 79. With these two caveats, we refrain from striking down Section 79(3)(b)."

And the corresponding treatment of Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules: "However, Rule 3(4) needs to be read down in the same manner as Section 79(3)(b). The knowledge spoken of in the said sub‑rule must only be through the medium of a court order. Subject to this, the Information Technology (Intermediaries Guidelines) Rules, 2011 are valid."

💡 The two caveats — learn them as a pair
  1. Source caveat: "actual knowledge" arises only from a court order or a notification by the appropriate Government or its agency. A private party's complaint — however detailed, however obviously meritorious — does not by itself trigger the duty to remove or forfeit the safe harbour.
  2. Subject‑matter caveat: the court order or Government notification must relate to the eight subject matters of Article 19(2) — sovereignty and integrity of India, defence of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence, and (later additions) the integrity of India. "Unlawful acts" beyond these cannot form part of s.79.

6.4 The copyright exception — how My Space qualified Shreya Singhal

📜 The express qualification

"The scope was widened in Shreya Singhal Vs UOI (2015) 5 SCC 1, where actual knowledge was held to mean a Court order in cases relatable to Article 19 of the Constitution of India… However, this was in respect of restrictions under Article 19(2) of the Constitution of India.… In the case of copyright laws it is sufficient that MySpace receives specific knowledge of the infringing works in the format provided for in its website from the content owner without the necessity of a court order."

Why the distinction is principled, not arbitrary: Shreya Singhal's reading‑down was driven by a constitutional concern — that intermediaries should not become private censors adjudicating contested free‑speech claims on a private complaint. A copyright takedown notice raises no Article 19(1)(a) question: copyright is not a fundamental right, and the rights‑holder is not asking the platform to judge opinion but to locate a file. Requiring a court order for every pirated upload would make enforcement impossible at platform scale and would not advance any constitutional value. This is exactly the kind of reasoning an examiner rewards.

⚠️ The residual problem — the victims' delay

The reading‑down protects speech but creates a practical gap: a victim of revenge pornography, harassment or defamation must now obtain a court order before the intermediary is legally obliged to act — a process that can take weeks or months, during which the harm compounds and multiplies through re‑posting. The 2021 Rules partially address this through voluntary rapid‑response incentives (a grievance officer, a 24‑hour acknowledgement) and the 2026 Amendment through compressed clocks — but the core doctrinal gap identified in Shreya Singhal, that private notice alone cannot trigger the loss of safe harbour, remains constitutionally necessary and practically unresolved. X v. Union of India (2021) is the leading judicial response to precisely this problem — see §16 below.

📝 The three‑sentence answer to "What is 'actual knowledge' under s.79(3)(b)?"

(1) Literally read, s.79(3)(b) would require takedown on any complaint, making intermediaries private censors; the Supreme Court therefore read it down in Shreya Singhal so that "actual knowledge" means a court order or a notification by the appropriate Government or its agency, and Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules was read down in the same manner. (2) Two caveats attach: the order or notification must strictly conform to the Article 19(2) subject matters, and "unlawful acts" beyond Article 19(2) cannot form part of s.79. (3) In copyright claims, however, My Space holds that a specific, work‑and‑URL‑identifying notice from the rights‑holder is sufficient without any court order, because the constitutional rationale for the reading‑down does not apply.

7The companion provisions — ss.69A, 72A, 67C, 75 and 81

Syllabus item 7  The syllabus's "due diligence" cross‑reference pulls in the 2021 Rules; its "exemption from liability" cross‑reference pulls in s.79's neighbours. Five provisions are so closely interlocked with intermediary liability that no answer is complete without them. Learn them as a single apparatus.

7.1 s.72A — disclosure of information in breach of lawful contract

s.72A

Punishment for disclosure of information in breach of lawful contract

"Save as otherwise provided in this Act or any other law for the time being in force, any person including an intermediary who, while providing services under the terms of lawful contract, has secured access to any material containing personal information about another person, with the intent to cause or knowing that he is likely to cause wrongful loss or wrongful gain, discloses, without the consent of the person concerned, or in breach of a lawful contract, such material to any other person, shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years, or with fine which may extend to five lakh rupees, or with both."
Why it is in the syllabus: it is the only Chapter XI provision that names intermediaries expressly, and it is the criminal counterpart of the intermediary's data‑handling duties. It is the provision that bites when a platform leaks or sells what it holds.
Elements

The five ingredients of a s.72A charge

(1) A person — expressly including an intermediary.
(2) Providing services under the terms of a lawful contract — the source of the access must be contractual.
(3) Secured access to material containing personal information about another person.
(4) Mens rea — intent to cause, or knowledge of likelihood of causing, wrongful loss or wrongful gain.
(5) Disclosure — without consent, or in breach of the lawful contract.

💡 Real examples
  • A violation of s.72A: a marketplace employee sells a customer database containing names, addresses and phone numbers to a telemarketing firm for a commission. The access was contractual, the material contained personal information, the disclosure was without consent and for wrongful gain.
  • Not a violation: the same intermediary discloses the customer's shipping address to the seller who must fulfil the order — that is disclosure with consent, or at least not in breach of the contract.
  • Not a violation (but note s.67C): an intermediary hands over user information to a law‑enforcement agency on lawful demand under s.67C / Rule 3(1)(d) / the Second Amendment Rules, 2026 — the opening words of s.72A ("save as otherwise provided") and the "lawful contract" framing preserve this route.

7.2 s.69A — the parallel blocking power, and the 2009 Blocking Rules

📜 s.69A — Power to issue directions for blocking for public access of any information through any computer resource

Where the Central Government or a specially authorised officer is satisfied that it is necessary or expedient so to do, in the interest of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the defence of India, the security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States or public order, or for preventing incitement to the commission of any cognizable offence relating to the above, it may — subject to sub‑section (2), for reasons to be recorded in writing — by order direct any agency of the Government or intermediary to block for public access any information generated, transmitted, received, stored or hosted in any computer resource. Non‑compliance by an intermediary is punishable under s.69A(3) with imprisonment up to seven years and fine.

⚖️ Why s.69A survived and s.66A did not — the comparison to write out

Shreya Singhal put it in one sentence: "Section 69A unlike Section 66A is a narrowly drawn provision with several safeguards." The safeguards are: (i) blocking only where the Central Government is satisfied it is necessary; (ii) that necessity relatable only to Article 19(2) subjects; (iii) reasons recorded in writing, so that they can be assailed under Art. 226; (iv) a hearing before a Committee, which hears not only the intermediary but also the originator if identified; (v) a review committee with power to unblock; and (vi) confidentiality. The Court expressly held that the absence of ss.95–96 Cr.P.C.‑style safeguards did not render the Rules infirm.

8Case brief 1 — Shreya Singhal v. Union of India

Case brief 1 · the anchor case of the Unit

Shreya Singhal and Ors. v. Union of India and Ors.

Supreme Court of India Writ Petition (Criminal) No. 167 of 2012 Decided 24 March 2015 (2015) 5 SCC 1 R.F. Nariman J. (for himself and Chelameswar J.)

01 Citation and provenance

Primary source: the full judgment is printed in the DU case material (CMITLAW‑2026) at pp. 132–179. It appears in the syllabus twice — once under Unit V (Intermediary Liability) and once under Unit VI (Cyber Security, s.66A). For Unit V, only the s.79 limb matters; for Unit VI, the s.66A and s.69A limbs. Learn both, and be explicit about which limb you are discussing.

02 Facts

A batch of writ petitions under Article 32 challenged provisions of the IT Act, 2000. The immediate occasion was a notorious sequence of arrests over Facebook posts: a person was arrested for a post commenting on a bandh observed after the death of a political leader in Mumbai, and another for "liking" and sharing that post. The petitioners included individuals and organisations whose speech had been, or could be, prosecuted under s.66A. The petitions also challenged s.69A (blocking) and the 2009 Blocking Rules, and s.79(3)(b) read with Rules 3(2) and 3(4) of the Intermediaries Guidelines Rules, 2011. A separate but allied challenge was made to s.118(d) of the Kerala Police Act, a state analogue of s.66A.

The impugned s.66A created an offence where any person sends, by means of a computer resource or communication device: (a) any information that is grossly offensive or has menacing character; (b) any information which he knows to be false, but for the purpose of causing annoyance, inconvenience, danger, obstruction, insult, injury, criminal intimidation, enmity, hatred or ill will, persistently; or (c) any electronic mail or electronic mail message for the purpose of causing annoyance or inconvenience or to deceive or mislead the addressee about the origin of such messages — punishable with imprisonment up to three years and fine.

s.79(3)(b), as then worded, provided that the safe harbour would not apply where, "upon receiving actual knowledge, or on being notified by the appropriate Government or its agency", the intermediary failed to expeditiously remove or disable access to the material. Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules required action within 36 hours of the intermediary obtaining knowledge by itself or being brought to actual knowledge by an affected person in writing or through e‑mail signed with electronic signature.

03 Issues

  1. Does s.66A violate Article 19(1)(a), and is it saved by any of the eight subject matters in Article 19(2)?
  2. Is s.69A, read with the 2009 Blocking Rules, constitutionally valid?
  3. Does s.79(3)(b), read with Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules, impose an unconstitutional private‑censorship burden on intermediaries, and is it void for vagueness and overbreadth?
  4. Is s.118(d) of the Kerala Police Act constitutional?
  5. Does the Article 14 challenge — that the internet is treated differently from other media — succeed?

04 Arguments of the parties

Petitioners' arguments
  • s.66A casts the net very wide — "all information" disseminated over the internet — and s.2(1)(v) defines "information" by medium only, with no reference to content. The public's right to know is directly affected.
  • s.66A makes no distinction between discussion, advocacy and incitement. Mere discussion or advocacy of an unpopular view, which may be annoying or grossly offensive to some, is at the heart of Article 19(1)(a).
  • s.66A has no proximate relationship to any Article 19(2) subject: not to public order (the offence is complete on sending a message causing annoyance, with no tendency to disturb the community); not to defamation (injury to reputation is not an ingredient); not to incitement to an offence (nothing need be incited); not to decency or morality (the word "obscene" is conspicuously absent).
  • The language is so vague that neither an accused nor the authorities can tell which side of the line a communication falls on — relying on Winters v. New York, Burstyn v. Wilson, City of Chicago v. Morales, Grayned v. Rockford, Reno v. ACLU.
  • Rule 3(4) requires the intermediary to exercise its own judgment whether information contravenes sub‑rule (2), when intermediaries by definition offer only a neutral platform; and no safeguards such as those in the 2009 Rules are provided. Rule 3(2) is vague, overbroad and unrelated to Article 19(2).
  • s.79(3)(b) likewise makes the intermediary judge "unlawful acts", an expression going way beyond Article 19(2).
Union of India's arguments
  • The internet is different from print and broadcast: global reach; accessible to the illiterate; no pre‑censorship possible; morphing and voice alteration; rumour spread to millions; easy invasion of privacy; harassment, obscenity and communal frenzy at the click of a button; near‑total anonymity; no institutionalised ethical norms.
  • There is therefore an intelligible differentia with rational relation to the object — the Article 14 challenge must fail.
  • The Additional Solicitor General asked the Court to read into s.66A the Article 19(2) subject matters, so as to save it: a "reasonable person" standard; incitement to imminent lawless action (Brandenburg); credible threats; material that stirs public anger; advocacy of violence; fighting words (Chaplinsky); hate speech; satirical or iconoclastic cartoons failing Hustler v. Falwell; glorification of terrorism; infringement of privacy including cyber‑bullying and stalking; obscenity applying Aveek Sarkar; and a context‑and‑background test of obscenity.
  • He also urged that the statute be made workable rather than struck down.

05 Reasoning and decision

Article 14. The Court accepted that the internet's distinctive features justified separate offences for speech over the internet — so there is an intelligible differentia — but held that this did nothing to relax the Court's scrutiny of the curbing of content. The validity of such a law still had to be tested on the ordinary touchstone.

Discussion, advocacy, incitement. The Court identified the three concepts fundamental to the reach of Article 19(1)(a): discussion, advocacy and incitement. "Mere discussion or even advocacy of a particular cause howsoever unpopular is at the heart of Article 19(1)(a). It is only when such discussion or advocacy reaches the level of incitement that Article 19(2) kicks in." The Court quoted Holmes J. in Abrams v. United States on the "market place of ideas" and Brandeis J. in Whitney v. California on the remedy for evil counsels being good ones, and illustrated the difference by Mark Antony's speech in Julius Caesar — advocacy that stops short of incitement.

Public order. Following Ram Manohar Lohia and Arun Ghosh, the test is whether an act leads to a disturbance of the current of life of the community or merely affects an individual. s.66A failed: the recipient is of no importance; no distinction is drawn between mass dissemination and dissemination to one person; no tendency to disrupt public order is required; and the nexus between the message and action is conspicuously absent. The Court's telling example was Arun Ghosh's guest at a hotel "annoying" girls — mere annoyance does not disturb public order. Under the clear‑and‑present‑danger or tendency tests, s.66A would not pass muster.

Defamation. "For something to be defamatory, injury to reputation is a basic ingredient. Section 66A does not concern itself with injury to reputation." Something may be grossly offensive without affecting reputation at all.

Incitement. "Section 66A has no proximate connection with incitement to commit an offence… The mere causing of annoyance, inconvenience, danger etc., or being grossly offensive or having a menacing character are not offences under the Penal Code at all."

Decency or morality. The Court noted the trajectory from Ranjit Udeshi's Hicklin approach through Anand Patwardhan to Aveek Sarkar's contemporary community standards test, and held that what is grossly offensive or annoying under s.66A "need not be obscene at all — in fact the word 'obscene' is conspicuous by its absence".

Vagueness. The Court set out Collins dictionary definitions of "offensive", "menace", "annoy", "inconvenience", "danger", "obstruct", "obstruction" and "insult" to demonstrate that no reasonable standard of guilt was laid down. A penal law void for vagueness fails where it defines no standard of guilt and draws no clearly drawn line.

The ASG's reading‑in request refused. "What the learned Additional Solicitor General is asking us to do is not to read down Section 66A — he is asking for a wholesale substitution of the provision which is obviously not possible." Where the legislature intended to incorporate Article 19(2) matters, it did so — as in s.69A.

s.69A upheld. Narrowly drawn; reasons recorded in writing; a hearing before a Committee that hears both the intermediary and, where identified, the originator; a Review Committee meeting at least once in two months with power to set aside directions and order unblocking; and confidentiality. The absence of ss.95–96 Cr.P.C.‑type safeguards did not render it infirm.

s.79 read down. See §6.2 above for the verbatim holding. The two caveats — the court order or Government notification must strictly conform to Article 19(2), and "unlawful acts" beyond Article 19(2) cannot form any part of s.79 — were attached, and Rule 3(4) was read down correspondingly, so that knowledge must come "only through the medium of a court order".

s.118(d), Kerala Police Act, struck down. Applying A.S. Krishna v. State of Madras, the statute as a whole fell within Entry 2, List II (and, as to the penalty, Entry 1, List III), so it was intra vires; but "causing annoyance in an indecent manner suffers from the same type of vagueness and over breadth, that led to the invalidity of Section 66A", and was therefore unconstitutional under Article 19(1)(a).

Principle evolved — the four holdings, in the Court's own concluding words
  1. "Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 is struck down in its entirety being violative of Article 19(1)(a) and not saved under Article 19(2)."
  2. "Section 69A and the Information Technology (Procedure & Safeguards for Blocking for Access of Information by Public) Rules 2009 are constitutionally valid."
  3. "Section 79 is valid subject to Section 79(3)(b) being read down to mean that an intermediary upon receiving actual knowledge from a court order or on being notified by the appropriate government or its agency that unlawful acts relatable to Article 19(2) are going to be committed then fails to expeditiously remove or disable access to such material. Similarly, the Information Technology 'Intermediary Guidelines' Rules, 2011 are valid subject to Rule 3 sub‑rule (4) being read down in the same manner as indicated in the judgment."
  4. "Section 118(d) of the Kerala Police Act is struck down being violative of Article 19(1)(a) and not saved by Article 19(2)."
💡 Exam takeaways
  • The three‑concept taxonomy is the skeleton of any free‑speech answer: discussion → advocacy → incitement. Only incitement can be restricted. Reproduce it.
  • The proximate‑connection requirement: a restriction must be proximately related to an Article 19(2) subject. "Annoyance", "inconvenience", "danger" and "grossly offensive" are not themselves offences under the Penal Code and cannot supply the connection.
  • Vagueness and overbreadth are independent, free‑standing grounds of invalidity in Indian law after this decision — worth stating expressly.
  • Reading down is not wholesale substitution. If the ASG's twelve‑point substitute text had been accepted, the Court would have legislated.
  • For Unit V, quote the s.79 holding verbatim — it is the single most quotable passage in the Unit, and the two caveats are mark‑earning.
  • Currency note: s.66A was struck down in 2015, yet FIRs continued to be registered under it for years afterwards — a documented enforcement failure that illustrates the gap between formal invalidation and policing practice. Never cite s.66A as an available charge.

9Case brief 2 — My Space Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd.

Case brief 2 · the definitive copyright-intermediary authority

My Space Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd.

Delhi High Court, Division Bench FAO(OS) 540/2011 C.M. APPL. 20174/2011, 13919 & 17996/2015 Decided 23 December 2016 (2017) 236 DLT 478; (2017) 69 PTC 1 S. Ravindra Bhat and Deepa Sharma JJ.

01 Citation and provenance

Primary source: the full Division Bench judgment is printed in the DU case material (CMITLAW‑2026) at pp. 88–125 — 38 pages, the longest judgment in the Unit. It is an interlocutory appeal against an interim injunction, and the Court was careful to say so: it expressly cautioned, following International Confederation of Societies of Authors and Composers v. Aditya Pandey, 2016 SCC Online 967, that elaborate reasoning in interlocutory judgments is "hardly an effective remedy to prevent the imprint of such observations on the human mind that mans the court at different levels". Write this caveat into your answer — it tells the examiner you understand the precedential weight of the decision.

02 Facts

The plaintiff. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. ("SCIL"), also known as T‑Series, is one of India's largest music companies. It claims copyright in more than 100,000 songs in various languages, together with cinematograph films, music videos, sound recordings, underlying musical works and lyrics. It exploits its rights by sale, by licensing (including satellite TV, radio, digital, internet and mobile platforms) and through the TPPL scheme ("T‑Series Public Performance Licences"). Its catalogue, when printed, runs into 10 volumes and about 3,000 pages, with close to 150,000 titles.

The defendant. MySpace operates www.myspace.com, a social‑networking and entertainment website launched in the United States and extended to India in 2007. Indian users are automatically redirected to the India‑centric version. Browsing requires no registration; uploading requires registration under MySpace's Terms of Use Agreement and Privacy Policy, which oblige registrants to respect intellectual property rights and prohibit the uploading of infringing material. MySpace's business model is advertising: it obtains a limited licence from the user to use, modify, delete from, add to, publicly perform, display, reproduce and display user‑generated content ("UGC") so as to convert it into a website‑compatible format. Advertisements are generated by an automated, term‑centric (not content‑centric) process known as "Adwords" — when a user types a search term, "Sponsored Link" advertisements appear beside the "natural results". MySpace claims to derive no revenue from UGC itself.

The safeguards. Being a US company, MySpace had installed a notice and take‑down procedure in compliance with the US Digital Millennium Copyright Act, and had developed three tools at its own cost: (a) the Hash Block Filter, which records the "hash" and creates a "fingerprint" of a removed file so that it cannot be reposted; (b) the Take Down Stay Down tool; and (c) a Rights Management Tool ("RMT") offered free to copyright owners, who register, create digital fingerprints of their works using software by Audible Magic, and supply the fingerprints so that any upload whose first 30 seconds match is automatically blocked.

The notices. On discerning infringing content, SCIL issued a legal notice dated 20 February 2008 with a sample list of infringing webpages, and claimed damages. MySpace replied on 12 March 2008 that the identified content had been taken down and placed in the copyright filter. SCIL alleged that infringing content nevertheless continued to be broadcast. Settlement attempts failed, and SCIL sued.

The interim orders. On 19 December 2008, the Court, while registering the suit, directed that SCIL hand over copies of the albums mentioned in para 47 of the plaint and furnish an updated catalogue, and that MySpace "endeavour to ensure removal of the infringing contents" and file an affidavit of compliance. MySpace provided 9 CDs containing 11 albums plus a catalogue disk; the 11 albums were sent to Audible Magic for fingerprinting, but the catalogue proved technologically difficult to comply with. MySpace's appeal against the second part of the order (FAO(OS) 31/2009) was disposed of by a Division Bench order dated 6 March 2009, which directed that as and when SCIL provided details of specific songs and films uploaded on MySpace's website, MySpace should remove them within one week — and that this would constitute sufficient protection as an interim measure. MySpace then moved under Order XXXIX Rule 4 CPC for vacation of the 19 December 2008 directions.

The impugned judgment. The Single Judge, after an analysis spanning 132 pages, held that "place" in s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act includes both physical and virtual space; that MySpace, having taken a limited licence and modified content, did not qualify for s.79's safe harbour under s.79(2)(a) or (b); that s.79 and the Copyright Act should not be read harmoniously because the proviso to s.81 meant the Copyright Act was a complete code; and that MySpace could not use volume of titles as an excuse to shrug its responsibilities. He granted a prohibitory injunction requiring MySpace to give prior notice to actual owners and to take preventive action before infringement even takes place, restraining MySpace from modifying SCIL's works by adding advertisements or logos or earning profits, and directing removal of future works within one week of notification. He relied on Garware Plastics and Polyester Ltd. v. Telelink, AIR 1989 Bom 331, to reject the difficulty of segregating infringing from non‑infringing material.

03 Issues before the Division Bench

  1. Whether MySpace could be said to have knowledge of infringement so as to attract s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act, 1957 and consequent liability — and, relatedly, whether there was any primary infringement under s.51(a)(i).
  2. Whether the proviso to s.81 of the IT Act overrides the "safe harbour" granted to intermediaries under s.79 of the IT Act.
  3. Whether ss.79 and 81 of the IT Act and s.51 of the Copyright Act admit of a harmonious reading.
  4. What relief was practical and implementable — specifically, whether a catalogue‑wide, prospective injunction covering future works could be granted.

04 Arguments of the parties

MySpace (appellant)
  • Public interest: every time a copyright violation occurs, fixing liability on intermediaries who merely provide an information‑sharing platform is a dangerous trend that threatens the manner of internet functioning. The WIPO Copyright Treaty and WPPT must inform the interpretation of online liability.
  • Section 79: after the 2009 amendment, Parliament cannot be taken to have fixed liability on intermediaries even where they satisfy s.79's conditions. MySpace cannot be held vicariously liable for the acts of others.
  • Impossibility: it is impossible to scan millions of videos or audio files to identify SCIL's works. No prima facie case was made that the catalogue was uploaded at all. An injunction against future works is opposed to s.51(a)(ii), which refers to "the work" — an existing, specific work. Reliance on Time Warner Entertainment v. RPG Netcom, 2007 (34) PTC 668.
  • Workability: the only way to implement the Single Judge's order would be to shut down its India operations or geo‑block the website in India, causing irreparable business loss at a prima facie stage.
  • Section 52(1)(b) and (c): transient and incidental storage in the technical process of electronic transmission is exempt; the 2012 amendment (in force 21 June 2012) expressly took note of intermediaries' challenges, giving absolute immunity under s.52(1)(b) to carriers and ISPs and a limited immunity under s.52(1)(c) to intermediaries without reasonable belief of infringement.
  • Knowledge standard: the safeguard tools were introduced on the basis of general knowledge of infringement, which cannot be the test for secondary liability when the accepted standard worldwide is actual knowledge — the "red flag" test under s.512 DMCA, applied in Viacom International v. YouTube, No. 07 Civ. 2103 (LLS). Reliance also on Religious Technology Center v. Netcom, 907 F. Supp. 1361 (1995); UMG Recordings v. Veoh Networks, 620 F. Supp. 2d 1081; Scarlet Extended SA v. SABAM, ECLI:EU:C:2011:771; Tiffany (NJ) v. eBay, 600 F.3d 93; BUMA and Stemra v. Kazaa (Amsterdam CA); Society of Composers v. Canadian Assn. of Internet Providers, 2004 SCC 45; Performing Right Society v. Cyril Theatrical Syndicate, (1924) 1 K.B. 1; Stephanie Lenz v. Universal Music, 572 F. Supp. 2d 1150; A&M Records v. Napster, 239 F.3d 1004; MGM Studios v. Grokster, 545 US 913; Capitol Records v. Vimeo, 826 F.3d 78; Roadshow Films v. iiNet, [2012] HCA 16.
  • Section 81: it has an overriding effect; the proviso only clarifies that the IT Act does not prevent a copyright owner from exercising Copyright Act rights. If the legislature intended an exception to the s.79 defence, a proviso would have been made within s.79. Its omission means s.79 continues to exempt intermediaries satisfying its conditions.
  • 2011 Rules: an intermediary must respond to a content owner's notice within 36 hours and comply by taking down within one month — so safe harbour exists even for copyright.
Super Cassettes (respondent)
  • Not an ISP but a resource centre accessible through an ISP. Comparing MySpace with a mall owner, SCIL claimed MySpace had both knowledge and control over the content on its website, and was guilty of both direct and contributory infringement.
  • Section 51(a)(i): the moment MySpace communicated SCIL's works to the public without permission, causing loss of revenue, that was a clear violation.
  • Section 51(a)(ii): MySpace generated revenue by creating a website for users to upload unauthorised content, making profit by providing a "space". "Place" includes an intangible space such as the internet. The exception would not be invited because MySpace possessed knowledge and had reasons to believe the content was unauthorised — evident from the fact that all the technological tools were created because MySpace had a reasonable apprehension of copyright violation.
  • The DMCA does not apply; Indian law governs. ss.14 and 51 must be read together to prove primary infringement of the intermediary.
  • Safeguards inadequate: despite them, "rampant violation took place". In the case of fresh releases, where maximum revenue is generated in the first few days, the delay inherent in notice‑and‑takedown causes substantial revenue loss. Reliance on Super Cassettes Industries v. Nirulas Corner House (P) Ltd., 148 (2008) DLT 487; Sega Enterprises v. MAPHIA, 857 F. Supp. 679; Garware Plastics (supra).
  • Section 52(1)(b) and (c): the storage contemplated is transient — cached data and temporary storage facilitating online functions. MySpace permanently stores data to make it available to its audience. Transient storage must be of lawful content duly authorised by the owner.
  • Under s.51(a)(ii), once the copyright owner establishes that the intermediary permitted its space for communicating infringing works, the burden shifts to the intermediary to show ignorance or lack of reasonable grounds to believe. An infringer's lack of knowledge is irrelevant on the language of the provision.
  • Section 52 reinforces the argument that primary liability occurs first when MySpace enables users to post infringing content.

Intervenors. The Centre for Internet and Society (a non‑profit accredited as a permanent observer with WIPO) and Flipkart Internet Pvt. Ltd. were permitted to assist the Court without being impleaded as parties, and made extensive submissions on safe‑harbour provisions, Indian and international judicial propositions, and intermediary liability.

06 Reasoning

The three questions framed. The Court confined itself to three: knowledge under s.51(a)(ii); whether the s.81 proviso overrides the s.79 safe harbour; and harmonious construction. It expressly declined to preclude a full‑dress argument after evidence as to the extent of infringement, the proportion of infringing content to total content, and the proportion of revenue attributable to infringing works.

Knowledge — the actual/general distinction. The Court rejected the Single Judge's test that general awareness suffices: "Simply put, that test overlooks that unlike 'real' space, in a virtual world, where millions of videos are uploaded daily, it is impossible under available technology standards to identify the streaming content, which actually infringes. Knowledge has a definite connotation, i.e. a consciousness or awareness and not mere possibility or suspicion of something likely." Drawing on Copinger and Skone James on Copyright, 16th Edn., it distinguished actual knowledge (including a "Nelsonian" turning of a blind eye — wilful failure to inquire) from constructive knowledge ("reason to believe"), and held that mere suspicion or apprehension is insufficient for either. Applying this to an internet service provider storing millions of videos: to require MySpace to know all owners of all works would be "a possibility not contemplated by the Copyright Act".

Automated modification is not knowledge. "SCIL's argument here that insertion of advertisements or modification of content by MySpace disclosed its knowledge — is unpersuasive… The modification made is to the format of the video or audio file and not to its content. The automated process does not apparently involve MySpace's actual control… if the modification/addition is entirely through an automatic process without any human intervention then knowledge cannot be attributed to MySpace." And more generally: "Knowledge is to be therefore placed pragmatically in the context of someone's awareness (i.e. a human agency); a modification on the technical side by use of software would per se not constitute knowledge. Nevertheless, if the software requires some kind of approval or authorization from a person or authority as opposed to a computer system then knowledge can be attributed. This however has to be seen at the stage of trial."

The specific‑notice requirement. "It is only when MySpace has specific or actual knowledge or when it has reasonable belief, based on information supplied by SCIL and if despite such knowledge or reasonable belief it fails to act can it be held liable for infringement." And: "SCIL is under a duty to specify the works in which it owns copyright. Merely giving the names of all content owned by it without correspondingly stating those, which MySpace is prima facie infringing, is contrary to the established principles of copyright law."

The harm in catalogue‑wide takedowns. The Court identified a grey area: where a genuine licensee uploads, or a licensed performer uploads a remix or combination of two songs, or a work is adapted in the form of another creation — "requiring removal would result in prejudice and injury". An indiscriminate acceptance of SCIL's entire list "would result in grave damage and result in likely multifarious disputes: with up‑loaders, many of whom are original creators in their own right… with distributors, who might hold genuine licences and with others who create versions, remixes or original titles". "The remedy proposed by SCIL and accepted by the single judge in such cases results in snuffing out creativity."

Section 79 — an affirmative defence. "Section 79 is neither an enforcement provision nor does it list out any penal consequences for non‑compliance. It sets up a scheme where intermediaries have to follow certain minimum standards to avoid liability; it provides for an affirmative defence and not a blanket immunity from liability."

Section 81's proviso — read harmoniously. The Court accepted that Parliament's simultaneous addition of the proviso to s.81 and the substitution of s.79 showed an intention not to disturb copyright or patent holders' rights, and that the Parliamentary Standing Committee report (rights under the IT Act "supplementary to and not in derogation of" the Copyright Act) was a relevant, though not conclusive, tool. But it held: "The true intent of Section 79 is to ensure that in terms of globally accepted standards of intermediary liabilities and to further digital trade and economy, an intermediary is granted certain protections… the true intent of Section 79 is to ensure that in terms of globally accepted standards of intermediary liabilities… Section 79 grants a measured privilege to an intermediary. However, that would not mean that the rights guaranteed under the Copyright Act are in any manner curtailed." The provisions are supplementary, not competing; but for the proviso, copyright owners would have been unable to pursue intermediaries at all. Reading them otherwise "would lead to an undesirable situation where intermediaries would be held liable irrespective of their due diligence", discourage investment in the internet sector, and — since a strict regime would simply drive intermediaries to jurisdictions with laxer data protection laws — make content harder to take down.

Section 52(1)(b) and (c). The 2012 amendment (in force 21 June 2012) was held inapplicable to the appeal. In any event, "transient" means temporary or impermanent and "incidental" means subordinate — cached data, cookies and the like. "Hosting and storing data, as observed earlier does not qualify to be referred to as incidental or transient data. The nature of the data being uploaded is of a permanent type." If at trial it were found that MySpace stores temporary information to further its main goal, it could seek the defence.

Comparative law. The Court surveyed Viacom v. YouTube ("if a service provider knows (from notice from the owner, or a red flag) of specific instances of infringement, the provider must promptly remove the infringing material. If not, the burden is on the owner to identify the infringement. General knowledge that infringement is ubiquitous does not impose a duty on the service provider to monitor or search its service for infringement"); UMG v. Veoh (9th Cir. 2013); Perfect10 v. CCBill (mere storage of files with suggestive names is not a red flag; s.512 does not require providers to police content); Religious Technology Center v. Netcom; A&M Records v. Napster ("if a computer system operator learns of specific infringing material available on his system and fails to purge such material… the operator knows of and contributes to direct infringement. Conversely, absent any specific information which identifies infringing activity, a computer system operator cannot be liable for contributory infringement merely because the structure of the system allows for the exchange of copyrighted material"); MGM v. Grokster; Capitol Records v. Vimeo; Roadshow Films v. iiNet; and the OECD's four‑step mechanism — notice and takedown, notice and notice, notice and disconnection (the French "three strikes" model) and filtering.

The chilling consequence. "To attribute knowledge to the intermediary industry would most likely lead to its shutdown… if messenger services like Whatsapp or social media portals like Facebook or Twitter… were held liable for each infringement. The greater evil is where a private organization without authorization would by requirement be allowed to view and police content and remove that content which in its opinion would invite liability, resulting in a gross violation of the fundamental right to privacy."

Disproportionality. Quoting Reno v. ACLU, the Court described the width of the Single Judge's directions as "to burn the house to roast the pig", and framed its task as treading "a delicate balance between the Scylla of over protection (of intellectual property and privileging it in an overbearing manner) and the Charybdis of ineffective or under‑protection, of IP rights: both of which chill and kill creativity, in the final analysis, harmful to society."

Decision — the three conclusions and the substituted relief
  1. "Sections 79 and 81 of the IT Act and Section 51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act have to be read harmoniously. Accordingly, it is held that proviso to Section 81 does not preclude the affirmative defence of safe harbor for an intermediary in case of copyright actions."
  2. "Section 51(a)(ii), in the case of internet intermediaries contemplates actual knowledge and not general awareness. Additionally, to impose liability on an intermediary, conditions under Section 79 of the IT Act have to be fulfilled."
  3. "In case of Internet intermediaries, interim relief has to be specific and must point to the actual content, which is being infringed."

The Single Judge's order was set aside and substituted with: (i) SCIL shall provide an updated catalogue of "specific" works in which it holds copyright, along with the location/URL of such work on MySpace's website, as and when it detects infringement; (ii) on receiving such notice, MySpace shall within 36 hours remove or block access, in accordance with Rule 3(4) of the Intermediaries Guidelines Rules 2011; (iii) MySpace shall keep an account of all content removed, the number of viewings before removal, and the proportionate advertisement revenue earned, to enable calculation of damages at trial. The appeal was allowed in these terms.

💡 Exam takeaways — the five propositions to carry away
  1. Specific knowledge, not general awareness. The rights‑holder bears the onus of identifying the particular works and their locations. A catalogue‑wide list is no notice at all.
  2. Automated, non‑human modification is not knowledge. Format conversion and automated advertisement insertion do not defeat the safe harbour at the prima facie stage; only software requiring human approval can attribute knowledge — a trial question.
  3. s.79 and s.81's proviso are supplementary, not competing. The proviso preserves the copyright owner's substantive rights, exercisable through the s.79 framework via specific takedown notices.
  4. No catalogue‑wide, prospective injunctions. Interim relief must identify the actual infringing content. Remedy: specific notice, 36‑hour takedown, and an account kept for damages.
  5. s.79 is an affirmative defence — pleaded by the defendant after the plaintiff establishes a wrong, not enforced by the plaintiff as a sword.
⚠️ Two limitations to state in a critical answer

(1) Context. The "specific knowledge" standard was laid down in the copyright context, under s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act. It does not automatically transpose to defamation or obscenity claims, which are governed more directly by s.79(3)(b)'s "actual knowledge" as read down in Shreya Singhal. (2) Stage. The Court was deciding an interlocutory appeal, and said so repeatedly. The extent of infringement, the revenue attributable to it, and the degree of human control over the automated processes were all left for trial.

10Case brief 3 — Google India Pvt. Ltd. v. M/s Visaka Industries Ltd.

Case brief 3 · s.79 as a fact-dependent defence

Google India Pvt. Ltd. v. M/s Visaka Industries Ltd.

High Court of Andhra Pradesh Criminal Petition No. 7207 of 2009 Decided 19 April 2011 s.482 Cr.P.C. Samudrala Govindarajulu J.

01 Citation and provenance

Primary source: the full order is printed in the DU case material (CMITLAW‑2026) at pp. 126–128. It is a short but exceptionally dense order, and it is the only case in the syllabus that prints the pre‑2008 text of s.79 alongside the post‑2008 substituted text. That makes it indispensable for a question that turns on the date of the offending conduct. It is also the case that went to the Supreme Court — see Case brief 6 below, where the appeal was decided on 11 December 2019.

02 Facts

The petitioner, A‑2, was Google India Private Limited, represented by its Managing Director (Sales and Operations). The first respondent‑complainant was Visaka Industries Limited, Secunderabad, engaged in manufacturing and selling asbestos cement sheets and allied products, represented by its Deputy Manager (Legal).

It was alleged that A‑1, Gopala Krishna, was the co‑ordinator of a group called "Ban Asbestos India" hosted by A‑2 (a Google Group), and published regular articles in that group. On 21 November 2008 an article was published captioned "Poisoning the system; Hindustan Times", aimed at a single manufacturer of asbestos cement products — the complainant — and naming renowned politicians G. Venkata Swamy and Sonia Gandhi, who had nothing to do with the ownership or management of the complainant company. On 31 July 2008 another article captioned "Visaka Asbestos Industries making gains" was published. Both articles, the complainant alleged, contained defamatory statements and were available in cyberspace to a worldwide audience. The complaint gave details of defamatory remarks in several other articles published by A‑1 in A‑2's group.

A‑2 was charged with offences punishable under ss.120‑B, 500, 501/34 IPC in C.C. No. 679 of 2009 before the XI Additional Chief Metropolitan Magistrate, Secunderabad. The complaint was instituted in January 2009, and the offences were being perpetrated from 31 July 2008 onwards — i.e. long before the amendment to s.79, which took effect from 27 October 2009.

03 Issues

  1. Did s.79 as originally enacted in 2000 exempt a network service provider from liability — much less criminal liability — for offences under other laws, and more particularly under the IPC?
  2. Did the amended s.79 (in force 27 October 2009) apply to the facts of this case, which pre‑dated it?
  3. Could the question whether the offence was committed without the intermediary's knowledge, or whether it had exercised all due diligence, be decided in a petition under s.482 Cr.P.C.?
  4. Can a company — a juristic person with no body that can be damned — be criminally prosecuted and punished?

04 Arguments

Petitioner / A‑2 (Google India)
  • The actions of intermediaries such as Google Inc., which is a service provider providing a platform for end users to upload content, do not amount to publication in law, and consequently the question of holding such intermediaries liable for defamation does not arise.
  • Reliance was placed on s.79 of the IT Act in support of this contention.
Respondent / complainant (Visaka Industries)
  • Notice was issued to the petitioner bringing to its attention the dissemination of defamatory material and the unlawful activity of A‑1 through the medium of A‑2's group.
  • Despite that notice, the petitioner "did not move its little finger" to block the material or stop dissemination.
  • The petitioner is a juristic person which can be prosecuted and, if found guilty, awarded appropriate punishment.

05 Reasoning and decision

On the original s.79. The Court set out the 2000 text and held that it exempted network service providers from liability "under this Act, rules or regulations made thereunder" only. "It did not exempt a network service provider from liability muchless criminal liability for the offences under other laws or more particularly under the Indian Penal Code." Further, the exemption operated only on proof that the offence was committed without knowledge or that the intermediary had exercised all due diligence to prevent it. "Proof in that regard can be let in by way of leading evidence by the accused. Therefore, the said question is a question of fact which this Court may not go into in this petition filed under Section 482 Cr.P.C."

On the amended s.79. The Court set out the post‑2008 text in full and observed: "It is only under the said amendment, non‑obstenti clause was incorporated in Section 79 keeping application of other laws outside the purview in a fact situation covered by the said provision." So that, after the amendment, an intermediary could claim exemption from the application of any other law in respect of third‑party information — provided it satisfied s.79(2). And under amended s.79(3), the exemption could not be claimed where the intermediary conspired, or where, upon receiving actual knowledge or being notified, it failed to expeditiously remove or disable access to the material without vitiating the evidence.

On the facts. "In the case on hand, in spite of the 1st respondent issuing notice bringing the petitioner about dissemination of defamatory material and unlawful activity on the part of A‑1 through the medium of A‑2, the petitioner/A‑2 did not move its little finger to block the said material or to stop dissemination of the unlawful and objectionable material. Therefore, the petitioner/A‑2 cannot claim any exemption either under Section 79 of the Act as it stood originally or Section 79 of the Act after the amendment which took effect from 27.10.2009."

On the date problem. "The present case in the lower Court was instituted in January, 2009 relating to the offences which are being perpetrated from 31.07.2009 onwards i.e., since long prior to the amendment to the said provision."

On corporate criminal liability. "There is no exemption of any criminal liability in respect of a company which is a juristic person and which has no body that can be damned or contemned. In case found guilty, the petitioner company can be awarded with appropriate punishment though not corporal punishment."

Principle evolved
  1. s.79 is a conditional, fact‑dependent defence, not an automatic immunity. Whether the intermediary acted without knowledge or exercised all due diligence is a question of fact requiring evidence, and cannot be resolved at the s.482 Cr.P.C. stage.
  2. Failure to act on notice defeats the exemption under both the pre‑ and post‑2008 versions — anticipating, by four years, the Shreya Singhal "actual knowledge" standard.
  3. A company can be criminally prosecuted; the absence of a body to be punished is answered by appropriate non‑corporal sentencing (fine).
  4. Temporal rule: the applicable version of s.79 is fixed by the date of the offending conduct, not the date of the complaint.
💡 Exam takeaways
  • Use this case to make the pre‑2008 / post‑2008 distinction concrete. It is the cleanest illustration in the syllabus of why the 2008 Amendment mattered: the original s.79 did not touch IPC liability at all.
  • Use it for the corporate criminal liability point — a juristic person can be prosecuted and fined, though not imprisoned.
  • Use it for the stage point: knowledge and due diligence are trial questions, not quashing questions.
  • Note the tension with Shreya Singhal: this Court treated a private notice from the complainant as sufficient to defeat the exemption. After Shreya Singhal, "actual knowledge" requires a court order or Government notification. Flag the tension — and note that the Supreme Court, on appeal, affirmed the dismissal without disturbing the result, holding that pre‑substitution s.79 did not protect an intermediary against an offence under ss.499/500 IPC.

11Case brief 4 — Vyakti Vikas Kendra v. Jitender Bagga

Case brief 4 · the 36-hour takedown, before it was codified

Vyakti Vikas Kendra, Indian Public Charitable Trust v. Jitender Bagga

High Court of Delhi CS(OS) No. 1340/2012 I.A. No. 8877/2012 Decided 9 May 2012 Manmohan Singh J.

01 Citation and provenance

Primary source: the order is printed in the DU case material (CMITLAW‑2026) at pp. 129–131. Note that the printed extract begins at paragraph 5 — the first four paragraphs (the plaint's formal structure and the prayer) are not reproduced. That does not affect the reasoning, which is complete.

02 Facts

The four plaintiffs — Vyakti Vikas Kendra, India Public Charitable Trust (a registered public charitable trust constituted to implement and promote the spiritual, educational, social and developmental activities of The Art of Living in India, founded in 1981 and inspired by His Holiness Sri Sri Ravi Shankar); Mr Gautam Vig (a devout follower); Mrs Bhanumati Narsimhan (Sri Sri Ravi Shankar's sister and an Art of Living teacher, personally a victim); and Mrs Sharmila Murarka (an Art of Living teacher, also personally a victim) — filed a suit claiming ₹5,09,00,000 in damages, permanent and mandatory injunction.

The grievance was that Defendant No. 1, Mr Jitender Bagga, had indiscriminately sent e‑mails and published a large number of blogs on http://www.blogger.com/ — a blog publishing service owned by Defendant No. 2, Google, which allows people to create and publish a "Blog" — containing, according to the plaintiffs, highly vulgar, disgusting and abusive references towards Sri Sri Ravi Shankar and towards various persons associated with the Art of Living. The plaint contained, at para 10, a table of defamatory material and a list of internet URLs, including revolutionprithvi.blogspot.in, revolutionprithvi0.blogspot.in, srisriravishankarisguruorconman.blogspot.in, chairmanrilabetsrioting.blogspot.in, diduspresidentcreateterrorists.blogspot.in and revolutionearthandmore.blogspot.in.

Notice was given to Defendant No. 1, who failed to reply. The plaintiffs filed Defendant No. 2's policy for removal of defamatory content from Blogger, the relevant extract of which read: "After careful review, Blogger may remove content or place a content warning page before viewing content deemed offensive, harmful, or dangerous, such as: Hate against a protected group; Adult or pornographic images; Promotion of dangerous and illegal activity; Content facilitating phishing or account hijacking; Impersonated user identity… Please note that Blogger is a provider of content creation tools, not a mediator of that content. We allow our users to create blogs, but we don't make any claims about the content of these pages. We strongly believe in freedom of expression, even if a blog contains unappealing or distasteful content or presents unpopular viewpoints… Here are some examples of content we will not remove unless provided with a Court order: Personal attacks or alleged defamation; Parody or satire of individuals; Distasteful imagery or language; Political or social commentary."

03 Issues

  1. Is Defendant No. 2 (Google/Blogger) an "intermediary" within s.2(1)(w), and does it owe duties under s.79 and the Intermediaries Guidelines Rules, 2011?
  2. What is the consequence of actual knowledge of unlawful content under s.79(3)(b) read with Rule 3(4)?
  3. Have the plaintiffs made out a prima facie case for an ex‑parte interim order, and where does the balance of convenience lie?

04 Arguments

Plaintiffs
  • Defendant No. 1 has published highly defamatory materials about the plaintiffs, the Art of Living Foundation, its spiritual head and his family members, and has sent numerous e‑mails.
  • If the interim order is not passed, the plaintiffs will suffer irreparable loss and injury, immense loss of reputation, mental trauma, harassment and humiliation.
  • Notice was given to Defendant No. 1, who failed to reply.
  • Defendant No. 2's own published policy itself identifies categories of content it will remove — so it plainly has the capability to act.
  • Defendant No. 2 is an intermediary under s.2(1)(w) and is bound by Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules.
Defendant No. 2 (Google / Blogger)
  • Its own policy states that Blogger is a provider of content creation tools, not a mediator of that content.
  • It strongly believes in freedom of expression, "even if a blog contains unappealing or distasteful content or presents unpopular viewpoints".
  • It will not remove personal attacks or alleged defamation, parody or satire, distasteful imagery or language, or political or social commentary, unless provided with a Court order.
  • Where contact information for the author is listed, it recommends that the complainant work directly with that person to have the content removed or changed.

05 Reasoning and decision

Intermediary status. "Defendant No. 2 is an 'intermediary' within the definition of Section 2(1)(w) and Section 79 of the Information Technology Act, 2000."

The obligation on actual knowledge. "Under Section 79(3)(b) of the IT Act, 2000, defendant No. 2 is under an obligation to remove unlawful content if it receives actual notice from the affected party of any illegal content being circulated/published through its service. He is bound to comply with Information Technology (Intermediaries Guidelines) Rules 2011. Rule 3(3) of the said rules read with Rule 3(2) requires an intermediary to observe due diligence or publish any information that is grossly harmful, defamatory, libellious, disparaging or otherwise unlawful."

The 36‑hour rule. "Rule 3(4) of the said rule provides obligation of an intermediary to remove such defamatory content within 36 hours from receipt of actual knowledge." The Court then quoted Rule 3(4) verbatim, including the requirement that the intermediary "shall act within thirty‑six hours and where applicable, work with user or owner of such information to disable such information that is in contravention of sub rule (2)."

Prima facie case and balance of convenience. "In view of the foregoing facts and reasons mentioned above, it appears that the plaintiffs have been able to make out a strong prima facie case of passing of ex‑parte interim order. The balance of convenience also lies in favour of the plaintiffs and against the defendants. In case, interim order is not passed, the plaintiffs will suffer irreparable loss and injury."

The order passed
  1. Till the next date, Defendant No. 2 is directed to remove all defamatory contents about the plaintiffs posted by Defendant No. 1 on Defendant No. 2's website www.blogger.com, as well as the links mentioned in paragraph 4 of the application, within 36 hours from the date of knowledge of the order passed by the Court.
  2. Defendant No. 1 is restrained from sending any e‑mail (including e‑mails the details of which are given in para 7) or posting any material over the internet which has a direct or indirect reference to the plaintiffs, or the Art of Living Foundation, or any member of the Art of Living Foundation, or His Holiness Sri Sri Ravi Shankar.
  3. Compliance with Order XXXIX, Rule 3 CPC to be made within three days; a copy of the order to be given dasti.
💡 Exam takeaways
  • The 36‑hour takedown standard, applied before it was codified in the 2021 Rules. This is the case to cite for the proposition that the 36‑hour clock has deep judicial roots — later codified as Rule 3(1)(d) of the 2021 Rules and compressed to 3 hours by the 2026 Amendment.
  • A court order is itself a trigger for "actual knowledge". The order operates as the very thing Shreya Singhal later held was required — decided in May 2012, three years before Shreya Singhal. The Cornell notes put it well: the order is "consistent with (and pre‑dating, but anticipating) the Shreya Singhal reading‑down".
  • The intermediary's own policy cuts both ways. Blogger's published policy identified categories of removable content — which established capability — but reserved a court‑order requirement for defamation. A court then supplied the order. Note the doctrinal neatness: the policy's own exception and the Court's reading‑down point the same way.
  • Ex‑parte interim injunctions against intermediaries are an accepted remedy in Indian practice — but note the free‑speech objections later articulated in X v. Union of India and in My Space.
🔍 Holistic reading — the three provisions this case brings together

s.2(1)(w) (status) → s.79(3)(b) (obligation on actual knowledge) → Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules (the 36‑hour clock and 90‑day preservation). Read together with s.500 IPC / s.356 BNS (the substantive defamation), which was Unit III's online‑defamation territory, and with Order XXXIX Rule 3 CPC (the procedural vehicle). A complete answer names all five.

12Case brief 5 — Avinash Bajaj v. State

Case brief 5 · corporate vs. personal liability of officers

Avinash Bajaj v. State (N.C.T. of Delhi)

High Court of Delhi Petition under s.482 Cr.P.C. Decided 29 May 2008 Reported at 116 (2005) DLT 427 (as cited by the SC); 2008 (105) DRJ 721
📌 Provenance — read this first

The full judgment of Avinash Bajaj v. State is not printed in the DU case material. It is listed in the syllabus twice — under Unit IV (obscenity, alongside Suhas Katti) and under Unit V (intermediary liability). This brief is therefore reconstructed from the published order and from the Supreme Court's own account of it in Google India v. Vishakha Industries, 2019 SCC OnLine SC 1587, where the Union of India relied on it as the paradigm of the pre‑amendment position. It is also the case in which the co‑accused, Sharat Babu Digumarti, went to the Supreme Court in Sharat Babu Digumarti v. Government of N.C.T. of Delhi, (2017) 2 SCC 18 — a cross‑reference worth making.

01 Facts

The petitioner, Avnish Bajaj, was the Managing Director of the company that owned the website www.baazee.com at the relevant time — Bazee India Pvt. Ltd. ("BIPL"), later renamed eBay India Pvt. Ltd. ("EIPL"). Baazee.com was an online auction platform on which users could list items for sale.

A user listed an obscene MMS clip — a pornographic video — for sale on the platform. The clip was sold and transmitted. A complaint was lodged; an FIR was registered; and a charge sheet was filed against three persons: the user (the seller), Avnish Bajaj, and an employee, Ravi Raj — together with Sharat Digumarti (Col. No. 4 in the charge sheet). The charges were framed under ss.292 and 294 IPC read with s.67 of the IT Act, 2000.

The investigation, as recorded in the charge sheet, found that "all the said three accused persons knowing fully well and having reasons to believe, have sold/transmitted a pornographic/obscene MMS clip causing lascivious impact on citizens by appealing to their prurient interest for their undue pecuniary gains."

Bajaj filed a petition under s.482 Cr.P.C. seeking to quash the prosecution, which also raised questions concerning the criminal liability of directors.

02 Issues

  1. Did the listing on baazee.com — as distinct from the clip itself — amount to an offence under s.292(1)(a) and 292(2)(d) IPC (selling and advertising an obscene object)?
  2. Was a prima facie case made out under s.294 IPC (obscene songs and acts in a public place)?
  3. Was a prima facie case made out under s.67 IT Act read with s.85 IT Act (liability of persons in charge of a company's business)?
  4. Could the Managing Director be held criminally liable individually, merely by reason of his office?

03 Arguments

Petitioner (Avnish Bajaj)
  • The video clip was not hosted or made available on baazee.com; it was transmitted directly between seller and buyer. BIPL was therefore not responsible for the obscene material itself.
  • The listing itself was not obscene and did not attract the offences under ss.292/294 IPC or s.67 IT Act. At the highest, BIPL was concerned only with the listing.
  • No criminal liability can attach to the petitioner individually merely because he was the Managing Director — especially since BIPL itself was not made an accused.
State (on the charge sheet)
  • The charge sheet recorded that the three accused, "knowing fully well and having reasons to believe", had sold or transmitted a pornographic/obscene MMS clip appealing to prurient interest for undue pecuniary gains.
  • The listing answered the definition of obscenity: it contained words or writing appealing "to the prurient interest", or such as to tend to deprave or corrupt persons likely to read, see or hear it. It contained explicit words that left a person in no doubt that what was sought to be sold was lascivious.
  • The investigation found that the policies and conduct of Baazee.com and its MD were designed to increase sales and maximise profits; that "safeguard of prevailing moral values and prurient interests of any person in particular and the society at large was not a pressing agenda"; and that the policy makers of the company were negligent and failed to exercise due diligence.

04 Reasoning and decision

On the listing. "Prima facie it appears that the listing itself answered the definition of obscenity since it contained words or writing that appealed 'to the prurient interest' or if taken as a whole was 'such as to tend to deprave or corrupt person, who are likely to read, see or hear the matter contained or embodied in it.'"

On s.292(2)(d) — the advertising limb. "The placing of an advertisement on the website informing the viewer that an obscene material or object is available for sale, one click away, is enough to attract the offence under Section 292(2)(d). The advertisement might itself have been inserted by the seller but the website facilitated the sale by carrying the listing which informed the potential buyer that such a video clip that is pornographic can be procured for a price."

On content filters — the intermediary's duty. "Owners or operators of websites that offer space for listings might have to employ content filters if they want to prove that they did not knowingly permit the use of their website for sale of pornographic material. Given the nature of the offence and the 'strict liability' envisaged by Section 292(1) IPC, even if for some reason the filters fail, the presumption that the owner of the website had the knowledge that the product being offered for sale was obscene would get attracted. This of course would be a rebuttable presumption."

On s.294 IPC. "It cannot be said that the website itself did an obscene act or performed any obscene song. The offence under Section 294 is not even attracted prima facie in the facts and circumstances of the present case."

On s.67 read with s.85. The Court found sufficient prima facie material against the petitioner under s.67 read with s.85 IT Act, especially since the company might be summoned later to face trial.

On proportionality. The Court held that any restriction on speech should be founded on the principle of least invasiveness — the restriction should be imposed in a manner and to the extent which is unavoidable in a given situation, and should consider whether the anticipated event would or would not be intrinsically dangerous to the public interest.

Decision — the split outcome
  1. Against the company (BIPL / EIPL): a prima facie case is made out under ss.292(1)(a) and 292(2)(d) IPC and s.67 IT Act. Proceedings continue.
  2. Against the petitioner under s.292 IPC individually: no prima facie case; the proceedings under s.292 IPC as against him are quashed.
  3. Under s.294 IPC: no prima facie case against either the company or the petitioner; charges dropped.
  4. Against the petitioner under s.67 read with s.85 IT Act: a prima facie case is made out; the prosecution continues.
💡 Exam takeaways
  • The corporate / personal distinction. The company answers for the platform's conduct; the officer answers only where his own role is made out. But s.85's due‑diligence proviso did not save the MD here — because the investigation had found negligence in the company's policy‑making, in which he was implicated.
  • Listing ≠ hosting, but listing is enough. The platform did not host the clip; it hosted an advertisement. Carrying that advertisement, "one click away" from the obscene material, was enough. The duty to employ content filters is the earliest judicial articulation of what became the 2021 Rules' automated‑detection obligations.
  • Strict liability and a rebuttable presumption. Under s.292(1), the presumption of knowledge attaches to the website owner once the offence is shown — a very different standard from s.79's "actual knowledge". This contrast is a guaranteed examiner question.
  • The pre‑amendment paradigm. The Union of India relied on this case before the Supreme Court in Google India v. Vishakha Industries as the exemplar of the position before the 2008 Amendment: an intermediary was liable for third‑party content made available by it, absent a statutory exemption.
  • The least‑invasiveness principle is worth quoting — it is the Indian analogue of the proportionality reasoning that later runs through Shreya Singhal and X v. Union of India.
⚠️ A caution on the Cornell summary

The consolidated exam notes describe this case as one where the Court "quashed proceedings against the individual MD personally… while allowing proceedings against the company to continue." That is accurate only as to the s.292 IPC charges. The prosecution of Bajaj under s.67 IT Act read with s.85 continued, and the Court found sufficient prima facie material against him on that count. In an exam, state the split outcome precisely — the nuance is the mark.

13Case brief 6 — Google India Pvt. Ltd. v. Vishakha Industries (Supreme Court)

Case brief 6 · the appellate sequel — and the definitive word on pre-amendment s.79

Google India Pvt. Ltd. v. M/s Vishakha Industries Ltd. and Anr.

Supreme Court of India Criminal Appeal No. 1987 of 2014 Decided 10/11 December 2019 2019 SCC OnLine SC 1587 K.M. Joseph and Ashok Bhushan JJ.

01 Citation and provenance

Primary source: the judgment is not printed in the DU case material; it is cited in the syllabus by citation alone (Google India Pvt. Ltd. v. Vishakha Industries, 2019 SCC OnLine SC 1587). This brief is drawn from the published judgment. Its value is that it is the appellate sequel to Case brief 3 — the same complaint, the same articles, the same Google Group — decided by the Supreme Court eight and a half years later. Read the two together.

02 Facts

The appellant was Google India Private Limited, the second accused in a criminal complaint filed by the first respondent, M/s Vishakha Industries, a public limited company manufacturing and selling asbestos cement sheets with seven plants and more than twenty‑five marketing offices. The first accused was alleged to be the co‑ordinator of "Ban Asbestos India", a group hosted by the appellant, in which articles were regularly published. On 21 November 2008 an article captioned "Poisoning the system: Hindustan Times" was published, targeting renowned politicians who had nothing to do with the ownership or management of the company. On 31 July 2008 an article captioned "Visaka Asbestos Industries making gains" was published. The complaint alleged that the articles were "filled with hatred towards Complainant which is defamatory in nature", that they injured the company's reputation, and that "the service provider like Accused No. 2 has made it easier than ever before to disseminate defamatory statements to the world wide audience without taking any due care and diligence to prevent it". Cause of action was pleaded as arising on 31 July 2008, 21 November 2008, and 10 December 2008 — the date of the notice to withdraw the articles.

The Magistrate summoned the appellant. The appellant's s.482 Cr.P.C. petition was dismissed by the Andhra Pradesh High Court (Case brief 3). The Supreme Court heard the appeal on the papers.

03 Issues

  1. Whether the High Court should have accepted, at the threshold, that the appellant was not the intermediary — the intermediary being Google LLC, its parent.
  2. Whether s.79 as it stood prior to its substitution (i.e. before 27 October 2009) protected an intermediary against an offence under ss.499/500 IPC.
  3. Whether the amended s.79 applied, and whether the appellant could claim exemption under either version.
  4. Whether the Shreya Singhal reading‑down of s.79(3)(b) assisted the appellant.

04 Arguments

Appellant (Google India)
  • It is not the intermediary; the intermediary is Google LLC, and the High Court should have so held (the "Google LLC conditions").
  • It is protected under s.79 "even if the entire allegations are accepted". Once the factum of the electronic record is admitted, s.79 must apply ipso facto and ipso jure.
  • The Act created a separate category of "originator" (s.2(1)(za)) to which s.79's protection was consciously not extended — the appellant is not the originator.
  • Reliance on Shreya Singhal's reading‑down of s.79(3)(b), and on Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer on electronic evidence.
  • The Magistrate had a judicial discretion to proceed without recourse to s.202 Cr.P.C., and no prejudice was pleaded or proved (relying on Shivjee Singh v. Nagendra Tiwari).
Respondent (Vishakha Industries)
  • Google hosts Google Groups. The only question of fact is whether the appellant is in control of the Group, or whether it is controlled by the parent. "The issue is limited as to the role of appellant and its participation in the business of providing Google Groups platform and raising revenues for the same through advertisements, etc. apart from marketing it."
  • "The appellant cannot be allowed to disown its role in Google Groups. The appellant has withheld the actual nature of the activities it is carrying on in India. A party must come to court with clean hands."
  • "The role of appellant is a question of fact. Once it is put on notice, Google cannot be heard to say that it is not aware of the content. If it chooses to perpetuate the content as publisher of defamatory content, consequences under law must follow."
  • "Appellant plays a very active role in controlling advertisement. Google is an active publisher as it provides various tools to users for enabling them to create content online."
  • The appellant's Memorandum of Association itself, prima facie, shows that its main objects include providing internet‑related projects, services and applications. There is "a common economic entity wherein the users are provided digital space purportedly free but are made to part with their personal data as consideration", which data is used to generate targeted advertisement revenues.

The Union of India's submissions (recorded at length, and useful in themselves) included: that the internet is a genuinely democratic medium and a marketplace; that the UN Human Rights Council recognised internet access as a basic human right in June 2016 and that Finland made broadband a legal right in 2010; that the internet works on a "pull" principle unlike the "push" model of television and newspapers; that there is a distinction between publication on the internet and in print media, recognised in Shreya Singhal; that the definition of intermediary itself reveals that it is not an editor or publisher, the role being that of a facilitator or conduit rather than a selector or editor; that the pre‑amendment situation is exemplified by Avnish Bajaj v. State; and that anybody aggrieved can make a takedown request.

05 Reasoning and decision

On the group entity. The Court rejected the contention that the High Court should have acted on the "Google LLC conditions" and found the appellant not to be the intermediary: "We hold that this is a matter for trial." Whether the appellant controls the Google Group, and the extent of its participation in the business of providing the platform and raising revenues, are questions of fact.

On pre‑substitution s.79. The Court entered detailed findings: "In our view, Section 79, before its substitution, exempted the Network Service Provider, which is defined as an intermediary, from liability under the Act, Rules or Regulations made thereunder in regard to any third‑party information or data made available by him provided the Service Provider…" And, decisively: "We hold that Section 79 of the Act, prior to its substitution, did not protect an intermediary in regard to the offence under Section 499/500 of the IPC."

On the earlier version's limits. "The discussion of the High Court regarding Section 79 reveals the following findings: a. The earlier version of Section 79 kept at bay the impact of other laws. After the amendment, Section 79 affords exemption from any other law in respect of the third‑party information subject to sub‑Section (2) of Section 79."

On the pre‑amendment position generally. "Intermediaries stand on a different footing being only facilitators of exchanges of information or sales. Prior to the amendment, the exemption provision under Section 79 did not exist and, therefore, an intermediary would have been liable for any third‑party information or data made available by him as seen in the Bazee (supra). After the amendment, intermediary is not liable under any Act if it satisfied certain requirements as detailed in Section 79."

On what pre‑amendment s.79 did protect. "Section 79, even prior to its substitution, did protect the intermediary from liability for offences under the Act. This was a case where the appellant was sought to be prosecuted under Section 292 of the IPC even though he had been discharged under Section 67 of the Act. The offence was one which properly fell within the scope of Section 67 in so far as the offending matter was contained in an electronic record, as defined."

On the result. "Therefore, it was found that the appellant could not claim any exemption under Section 79 of the Act, prior to its substitution, or Section 79 of the Act, after its substitution, which took effect from 27.10.2009."

On Shreya Singhal. The Court noticed that the High Court did not have the benefit of Shreya Singhal, and that what was considered there was s.79 after substitution. It then set out the Government of India's understanding — that s.79 stands read down so that an intermediary need take down information only upon actual knowledge that a court order has been passed — and quoted the reading‑down passage itself. The reading‑down did not assist the appellant, because the cause of action pre‑dated the substituted provision, and the High Court had found that the appellant had not acted on the notice of 10 December 2008.

On publication. As the WILMap summary of the decision records, the Court held that there may be publication if an intermediary capable of removing content refuses to do so — a proposition of direct relevance to criminal defamation.

Principle evolved — the conclusions in para 155
  1. "We reject the contention of the appellant that the High Court should have acted on the Google LLC conditions and found that the appellant is not the intermediary. We hold that this is a matter for trial."
  2. "We hold that Section 79 of the Act, prior to its substitution, did not protect an intermediary in regard to the offence under Section 499/500 of the IPC."

Together with the reasoning above, the practical consequences are: (a) intermediary status is a trial question, not a quashing question; (b) the applicable version of s.79 is fixed by the date of the cause of action; (c) pre‑substitution s.79 shielded intermediaries only against liability under the IT Act itself, not against the IPC; (d) a refusal to remove content that the intermediary is capable of removing can amount to publication for the purposes of defamation; and (e) group structure does not, by itself, defeat Indian jurisdiction.

💡 Exam takeaways
  • Cite this case for the proposition that s.79 is not an automatic, ipso facto immunity. The appellant's argument that s.79 "must apply ipso facto and ipso jure" once the electronic record is admitted was squarely rejected.
  • Cite it for the "capable of removing but refuses" test — the bridge between intermediary status and the offence of publication in defamation.
  • Cite it for the pre/post‑amendment dichotomy, which is the doctrinal spine of Case brief 3.
  • Note the modern caveat: the findings are of limited forward application because the cause of action arose before the substituted s.79 came into force. Say so — it demonstrates precision.
  • Cross‑refer: Sharat Babu Digumarti v. Government of N.C.T. of Delhi, (2017) 2 SCC 18, the Supreme Court decision in the companion Bazee.com prosecution, and Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer on the electronic evidence on which any such prosecution depends.

14Case brief 7 — Amway India Enterprises v. 1Mg Technologies

Case brief 7 · the passive/active question — the Single Judge's view

Amway India Enterprises Pvt. Ltd. v. 1Mg Technologies Pvt. Ltd. & Anr.

High Court of Delhi (Single Judge) I.A. 11335/2018 in CS(OS) 410/2018 Decided 8 July 2019 (2019) 260 DLT 690; (2019) 79 PTC 425 Prathiba M. Singh J.
📌 Provenance — and read the next section immediately after this one

This judgment is not printed in the DU case material; it is cited in the syllabus by citation and Indian Kanoon link. More importantly, it was set aside on appeal by the Division Bench in Amazon Seller Services Pvt. Ltd. v. Modicare Ltd., 31 January 2020 (Case brief 8). It is nevertheless essential, for two reasons: (1) the Division Bench's reasoning can only be understood as a response to it; and (2) the DU case material's own self‑learning exercise asks you to analyse the statement that "due diligence under section 79 of the information technology act has been narrowed whereas the safe harbour rule is wide" with the hint pointing to the Division Bench decision. So: learn the Single Judge's reasoning, then the reversal.

01 Facts

A cluster of suits (CS(OS) 410, 453, 480, 531, 550/2018 and 75, 91/2019) was filed by Direct Selling Entities — principally Amway India Enterprises Pvt. Ltd., and also Oriflame India Pvt. Ltd. and Modicare Ltd. — against e‑commerce platforms (1Mg, Amazon, Snapdeal, Cloudtail and others). The plaintiffs sold their products exclusively through a network of distributors / direct sellers under contractual codes of ethics, and complained that third‑party sellers were listing and selling their products on the platforms' marketplaces without authorisation, at discounted prices, thereby impairing their marks and inducing breach of contract.

Amway learnt in September 2017 that Amazon was offering "Nutrilite Daily‑120N tablets" for sale without permission. It sent a cease‑and‑desist notice on 22 September 2017 asking Amazon to remove references to Amway products. Amazon replied on 11 October 2017 that it merely provided an online platform for registered sellers, did not monitor or control transactions, and that Amway should proceed against the vendors. Amway rejoined on 6 November 2017 that Amazon was "actively involved" in the sale — helping sellers manage accounts, increase visibility and discoverability, listing products, and publishing print advertisements offering Amway products. On 21 September 2018 Amway's representative purchased Amway products from Amazon's website.

The plaintiffs relied on the Model Framework for Guidelines on Direct Selling dated 26 October 2016 (the "Direct Selling Guidelines" or "DSGs"), in particular Clause 7(6): "Any person who sells or offers for sale, including on an e‑commerce platform/marketplace, any product or service of a Direct Selling Entity must have prior written consent from the respective Direct Selling Entity in order to undertake or solicit such sale or offer."

The platforms' defences, broadly, were: they are intermediaries entitled to safe harbour under s.79; they only facilitate transactions between buyers and sellers; they do not need authorisation from Amway because that is the seller's responsibility; providing facilitation services does not remove intermediary status; and they have no role in sourcing products, the sellers retaining title.

02 Issues

  1. Whether unauthorised sale of DSE products by third parties on online platforms violates the plaintiffs' proprietary rights and operational framework.
  2. Whether e‑commerce platforms can avail of safe harbour under s.79 in the context of active facilitation of unauthorised sales.
  3. Whether the Direct Selling Guidelines, 2016 have binding effect and are enforceable against third parties and intermediaries.
  4. Whether the platforms are guilty of tortious interference with contractual relations.

03 The Single Judge's reasoning on s.79

The learned Single Judge held that the e‑commerce platforms were not merely passive players but "in fact are massive facilitators", inasmuch as they provided warehousing, logistical support, packaging, delivery services, payment services and collection gateways — relying on FDI Press Note No. 2 of 2018 and its definition of e‑commerce. She distinguished My Space Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd., Kent RO Systems Ltd. v. Amit Kotak, 2017 (69) PTC 551 (Del), and Kent RO Systems Pvt. Ltd. v. eBay India Pvt. Ltd., FAO(OS)(COMM) 95/2017 (DB, 1 May 2017), and relied on Christian Louboutin SAS v. Nakul Bajaj, 2018 (76) PTC 508 (Del) and Shreya Singhal.

Her conclusions on the third issue were:

📜 The paragraphs the appeal turned on
"304. The question as to how the platforms are providing the value‑added services and whether they are performing an active role in the same, would have to be finally adjudicated at trial. However, the bare minimum that they would be required to do to avail the exemption under Section 79(2)(c), would be to observe due diligence required under Section 79(2)(c) of the IT Act.
305. Thus, in order for the platforms to continue to enjoy the status of intermediaries, subject to adjudication at trial, the due diligence requirements would have to be met and complied with, as per the Platforms' own policies, and as per the Intermediary Guidelines 2011. Non‑compliance with the Platforms' own policies would take them out of the ambit of the safe harbour."— Prathiba M. Singh J., 8 July 2019

And on tortious interference: "The continued sale of the Plaintiffs' products on the e‑commerce platforms, without the consent of the Plaintiffs, results in inducement of breach of contract, and tortious interference with contractual relationships of the Plaintiffs with their distributors."

04 Principle evolved — and why it matters even after reversal

The four propositions from the Single Judge
  1. Value‑added services can take a platform outside the safe harbour. Warehousing, logistics, packaging, delivery, payment collection and returns handling are not "mere facilitation"; they are "massive facilitation".
  2. Non‑compliance with the platform's own published policy forfeits the safe harbour. Where a platform's exclusion policy prohibits the sale of products by persons who are not "authorised resellers", and it nevertheless lists them, it has failed its own due diligence.
  3. s.79(2)(c) sets a "bare minimum" of due diligence, to be measured against the platform's own policies and the Intermediary Guidelines.
  4. Mere knowledge of a third party's contractual code can amount to inducement where the platform offers "a comfortable refuge" for breach.
⚠️ The reversal — state it in the same breath

The Division Bench in Amazon Seller Services v. Modicare (31 January 2020) set aside the impugned common judgment. It held that the Single Judge "appears to have misinterpreted Section 79 of the IT Act in concluding that is restricted to 'passive' intermediaries"; that "in terms of Section 79 of the IT Act, there does not appear to be any distinction between passive and active intermediaries so far as the availability of the safe harbour provisions are concerned"; that "there is prima facie merit in the contention of the Appellants that the value‑added services provided by them as online market places… do not dilute the safe harbour granted to them under Section 79"; that "the learned Single Judge appears to have erred in distinguishing the decision in Myspace Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd."; and that the Single Judge's findings that the platforms were "massive facilitators" playing "an active role in the sales process" were "too sweeping and definitive a set of findings which have to be properly rendered at the conclusion of the trial".

💡 How to use this case in an exam
  • As the "before" picture in a two‑stage answer. "The Single Judge in Amway v. 1Mg held that a marketplace providing warehousing, logistics, payment and returns services was not a passive intermediary and had to satisfy, at a bare minimum, the due diligence in s.79(2)(c) measured against its own policies. The Division Bench in Amazon v. Modicare set that aside, holding that s.79 draws no passive/active distinction and that value‑added services do not dilute the safe harbour."
  • For the "own policy" proposition, which survives the reversal as a general principle of due diligence even if not as a disqualifier on these facts.
  • For the trade‑mark and exhaustion issues that travel with intermediary liability in the marketplace context — s.30(3)/(4) Trade Marks Act, 1999 and Kapil Wadhwa v. Samsung Electronics on international exhaustion, both discussed by the Division Bench.
  • Do not cite the Single Judge's view as current law without flagging the reversal. That is the single most common error on this case.

15Case brief 8 — Amazon Seller Services Pvt. Ltd. v. Modicare Ltd.

Case brief 8 · the current position — no passive/active distinction

Amazon Seller Services Pvt. Ltd. v. Modicare Ltd. & Ors.

High Court of Delhi, Division Bench FAO(OS) 133, 134, 135, 141, 142 and 157 of 2019 Decided 31 January 2020 AIR OnLine 2020 Del 169 S. Muralidhar and Talwant Singh JJ.

01 Citation and provenance

Primary source: not printed in the DU case material; cited in the syllabus and in the DU case material's own self‑learning exercise (which gives the hint: "Amazon seller Services Private Limited v. Amway India Enterprises Private Limited, Del (DB) decided on Jan 31, 2020"). The appellants were Amazon Seller Services Pvt. Ltd., Cloudtail India Pvt. Ltd. and Snapdeal Pvt. Ltd.; the respondents were Amway, Oriflame and Modicare. The appeals were reserved on 21 December 2019 and pronounced on 31 January 2020.

02 Facts

Six appeals were directed against the same impugned common judgment dated 8 July 2019 (Case brief 7), by which the Single Judge had injuncted the appellants from selling the respondents' goods on their e‑commerce platforms, the respondents claiming to be Direct Selling Entities under the Model Framework for Guidelines on Direct Selling dated 26 October 2016. The appeals raised four issues: trademark infringement and the exhaustion doctrine under the Trade Marks Act, 1999; passing off, misrepresentation and dilution; the applicability of s.79 IT Act; and tortious interference with contractual relations. The appeals also raised a corporate veil question — whether Cloudtail, said to be a wholly owned subsidiary of Prione Business Services Pvt. Ltd. (a joint venture between Catamaran Ventures and Amazon Asia Pacific and Amazon Eurasia), could be treated as one with Amazon.

03 Issues

  1. Whether the sale of the respondents' products on the appellants' platforms infringed their registered trade marks, or whether the exhaustion doctrine under s.30 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 applied.
  2. Whether the conduct constituted passing off, misrepresentation or dilution/tarnishment.
  3. Whether the appellants were entitled to the safe harbour under s.79 of the IT Act — in particular whether s.79 is confined to passive intermediaries.
  4. Whether the appellants were guilty of tortious interference with the contractual relationships between the DSEs and their distributors.

04 The Division Bench's reasoning — the four moves that matter

📜 Move 1 — There is no passive/active distinction in s.79

"In terms of Section 79 of the IT Act, there does not appear to be any distinction between passive and active intermediaries so far as the availability of the safe harbour provisions are concerned. In terms of Section 79 of the IT Act, an intermediary shall not be liable for any third‑party information, data or communication link made available or posted by it, as long as it complies with Sections 79(2) or (3) of the IT Act."

📜 Move 2 — The correct reading of the s.79(2) gates

"The exemption under Section 79(1) of the IT Act from liability applies when the intermediaries fulfil the criteria laid down in either Section 79(2)(a) or Section 79(2)(b), and Section 79(2)(c) of the IT Act. Where the intermediary merely provides access, it has to comply with Section 79(2)(a), whereas in instances where it provides services in addition to access, it has to comply with Section 79(2)(b) of the IT Act." Applied to Amazon, Cloudtail and Snapdeal — "since they provide services in addition to access, they have to show compliance with Section 79(2)(b)": they must show that they (i) do not initiate the transmission, (ii) do not select the receiver, and (iii) do not select or modify the information. The Court's illustration: where a customer clicks the button, chooses the product and the number of units, "as long as this entire transaction is not controlled by Amazon and the choices… are made solely by the customer, such as, say, the decision to purchase three copies of the book, and these choices are not altered by Amazon, the requirements of Section 79(2)(b) of the IT Act would stand fulfilled."

📜 Move 3 — Value‑added services do not dilute the safe harbour, and intermediary status is a trial question

"There is prima facie merit in the contention of the Appellants that the value‑added services provided by them as online market places, as listed out by the learned Single Judge, do not dilute the safe harbour granted to them under Section 79 of the IT Act. Section 2(1)(w) of the IT Act does envisage that such intermediaries could provide value‑added services to third party sellers." — supported by Press Note No. 2 of 2018, para 5.2.15.2.4(vi) ("In marketplace model goods/services made available for sale electronically on website should clearly provide name, address and other contact details of the seller; post sales, delivery of goods to the customers and customer satisfaction will be responsibility of the seller"). And: "Given the disputed questions of fact that emerge from the pleadings in the suit, it is obvious that the issue of whether an entity is an intermediary or not can be decided only after a trial."

📜 Move 4 — s.79 is an affirmative defence, and the burden lies on the plaintiff

"The learned Single Judge appears to have erred in distinguishing the decision in Myspace Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd., where the Division Bench held that Section 79 of the IT Act is not an 'enforceable provision', but merely provides 'affirmative defence' to entities which fulfil the criteria set forth therein." And on burden: "the burden of proof has shifted unfairly onto the Defendants to show that they have complied with the requirements of Section 79 of the IT Act, when in fact the Plaintiffs have to first show that there had been a violation of any of their rights due to the Defendants' activities before the 'affirmative defence' of Section 79 of the IT Act could be sought to be invoked."

On the Shreya Singhal point. The Court recorded Amazon's submission that "the obligation of an intermediary to remove content under Section 79(3)(b) of the IT Act arises only if there is a Court order or a notification from a government agency on the grounds mentioned under Article 19(2) of the Constitution. Amazon points out that it did not receive any such Court order from Amway in support of its allegations. The letter of the FSSAI dated 9th April, 2018 was not a notification by an appropriate government agency and did not conform to the grounds enumerated in Article 19(2) of the Constitution."

On the 2011 Rules and due diligence. "Under Section 79(2)(c) of the IT Act, the obligation of the intermediary is that, in terms of the Intermediary Guidelines, it publishes its policies for the information and convenience of its users. The enforcement of such a policy is another matter." Clause 17 of Amazon's policy prohibited the sale on its platform of "unauthorised" products — but the Court accepted that this could not be interpreted to empower Amway to restrict the sale of its products on Amazon's platform, India following international exhaustion.

On tortious interference. "In the first place, the tort of inducement to breach of contract necessitates that there be a contract in the first place between the online platforms and the DSEs. The mere fact that the online platforms may have knowledge of the Code of Ethics of the DSEs, and the contractual stipulation imposed by such DSEs on their distributors, is insufficient to lay a claim of tortious interference. It was incumbent on the part of the Plaintiffs to demonstrate active efforts on the part of or contracts entered into by the Appellants/Defendants to make a viable case for tort of inducement to breach of contract."

On the corporate veil. "These are matters of evidence. There could not have been a presumption that Cloudtail and Amazon are one and the same entity and that the obligations of the Cloudtail would bind Amazon and vice versa." And, tellingly, on the monopoly objection: "There is merit in the contention of Amazon that by permitting private entities like Amway to restrict downstream distribution of genuine goods, by enforcing contractual stipulations against third parties, the judgment of the learned Single Judge recognizes a monopoly that can be exercised in perpetuity."

Principle evolved — the six propositions
  1. s.79 draws no distinction between passive and active intermediaries. Availability of the safe harbour turns on compliance with s.79(2) and (3), not on the degree of the intermediary's commercial engagement.
  2. The s.79(2) gates are correctly read as: s.79(2)(a) or s.79(2)(b), and s.79(2)(c). Pure conduits satisfy (a); those providing services in addition to access must satisfy (b).
  3. Value‑added services — warehousing, logistics, packaging, payment collection — do not dilute the safe harbour, because s.2(1)(w) itself envisages that intermediaries may provide such services.
  4. s.79 is an affirmative defence, not an enforceable provision; the plaintiff must first establish a violation of its rights, and only then does the burden shift.
  5. Intermediary status is a question of fact for trial, not to be conclusively determined at the interlocutory stage.
  6. Tortious interference requires a contract and active inducement; knowledge of a third party's code of ethics is not enough.
💡 Exam takeaways
  • This is the current law on the passive/active question. Cite it against the Single Judge in Amway v. 1Mg.
  • It is also the authority for the s.79(2) gate structure — quote the "either 79(2)(a) or 79(2)(b), and 79(2)(c)" formulation.
  • It supplies the burden‑of‑proof sequence, which most answers get wrong.
  • It applies Shreya Singhal to a commercial takedown demand — an FSSAI letter is not a "notification by the appropriate Government or its agency" conforming to Article 19(2). A very quotable application.
  • It is the case to use for the "holistic" requirement — it brings together the IT Act, the Trade Marks Act (ss.29, 30(3), 30(4)), the exhaustion doctrine, FDI Press Note 2 of 2018, the Direct Selling Guidelines 2016, and the law of tortious interference.
⚠️ Limitations

The Court was deciding an interlocutory appeal, and it said so repeatedly: the value‑added‑services point was one of "prima facie merit"; intermediary status, the extent of control, and the inducement question were all "matters of evidence" for trial. Also note that Christian Louboutin SAS v. Nakul Bajaj, 2018 (76) PTC 508 (Del), on which the Single Judge relied, had already been disapproved by a Division Bench of the same Court in M/s. Clues Network Pvt. Ltd. v. M/s. L'Oréal, RFA(OS)(COMM) 1/2019 (4 April 2019).

16Case brief 9 — X v. Union of India

Case brief 9 · making takedown orders actually work

X v. Union of India and Ors.

High Court of Delhi Writ Petition (Criminal) No. 1082 of 2020 Decided 20 April 2021 Anup Jairam Bhambhani J. Dr. Paval Duggal as amicus curiae

01 Citation and provenance

Primary source: not printed in the DU case material; cited in the syllabus by citation and number. It is the most recent of the nine prescribed cases and the one that most directly tests the 2021 Rules against the Shreya Singhal framework. Note carefully: this is not the later X Corp. litigation over the Sahyog portal, which concerns s.79(3)(b) as a source of executive blocking power. The 2021 case is about a victim seeking effective directions against intermediaries.

02 Facts

X, an Indian woman, discovered that photographs she had posted on her Facebook and Instagram accounts — with privacy settings activated — had been re‑posted on a pornographic website, www.xhamster.com, by an unknown entity without her consent, together with derogatory captions. The photographs were not themselves obscene or indecent; X's case was that their publication constituted an offence under s.67 IT Act (in conjunction with the captions) and other offences under the IT Act and the IPC.

X filed a complaint with the police and with the National Cyber‑Crime Reporting Portal. Neither authority acted. Within a week of their posting, the photographs had been viewed 15,000 times.

During interim proceedings, the Cyber Prevention Awareness and Detection Unit (CyPAD) of the Delhi Police submitted that, due to technological limitations and impediments, it could not assure the Court that it would be able entirely to remove the photographs from the internet. The Court made an interim order for removal, but X notified the Court that the photographs had been re‑published and re‑posted on other websites, nullifying the effect of the interim order. The Court appointed Dr. Paval Duggal, an advocate specialising in cyber law and cyber crime, as amicus curiae.

03 Issues

  1. When a party seeks the removal of content from the internet, "what directions are required to be passed by a court to make its order implementable and effective; and to which parties are such directions required to be issued?"
  2. "What steps are required to be taken by law enforcement agencies to implement such directions", so that the content does not resurface and the parties "do not succeed in brazenly evading compliance of such orders/directions with impunity?"

04 Arguments

Delhi Police / CyPAD
  • Directions must be issued to intermediaries under s.79 IT Act, the Penal Code and the 2021 Rules "to ensure successful removal of offensive and unlawful content from the world‑wide‑web and to prevent such content being re‑posted, re‑transmitted or republished".
  • Law enforcement does make requests to intermediaries for information or for the removal of content, but the intermediaries do not always cooperate.
  • CyPAD submitted that technological limitations and impediments prevented any assurance of complete removal.
Google LLC and other intermediaries
  • A search engine only indexes information and cannot be categorised as a "publisher"; its function is reactive rather than proactive — it can only remove content on notification by courts or government agencies.
  • It is technologically impossible to remove content when the issue is not the content per se but the context in which it is published. Reliance on My Space Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries, which had warned of the dangers to free speech of intermediaries removing all content when they cannot evaluate context.
  • Any prior restraint or "blanket ban" would infringe the right to freedom of expression and act as a chilling effect. But it had "no opposition to removing access to the offending content" and had processes in place, including a dedicated webform for Indian governmental agencies.
  • "The surest way to ensure that offending content is not accessible" is to remove it from the webpage on which it is hosted — but only the owner of the website can do that.

Other parties. The Ministry of Electronics and IT submitted that a court could make one of three directions — direct the intermediary to remove the content; direct the relevant government agency to have it removed; or grant the petitioner a right to request intermediaries, agencies or the police to seek removal — and highlighted that the Online Cyber‑Crime Reporting Portal had been expanded to cover all cybercrimes. ISPAI (a voluntary association of 82 service providers) explained that its members are "mandated by law to comply with any 'blocking orders' issued by competent government authorities or a court" but "cannot regulate the content of any intermediary", and that because they use encryption mechanisms "it is technically impossible for an internet service provider to block unlawful content". Facebook stressed that X had not sought relief against it, and that it remained willing to cooperate with law enforcement and courts.

05 Reasoning

The interim‑relief problem. The Court stressed that "if the court is not in a position to pass effective and implementable orders and is unable to ensure that such orders are complied with at the interim stage, subsequent adjudication of the matter could well be rendered infructuous".

The legislative framework compared. The Court conducted a thorough analysis of the IT Act, the 2009 Blocking Rules, the 2011 Intermediaries Guidelines Rules and the 2021 Rules. Its key comparative findings: the 2021 Rules "sharpened" the regime; they require the intermediary to notify users that they cannot publish information belonging to another person; they require the intermediary not to host information "violative of decency or morality, upon receiving actual knowledge about such information in the form of a court order or on being notified by the appropriate government or its agency"; they oblige removal within 36 hours of a court order or government notice; they require the provision of information to a governmental agency within 72 hours of a request for investigation, cyber‑security, verification of identity, or prevention, detection, investigation or prosecution of offences; and they oblige the intermediary to nominate a Grievance Officer. In summary, the Central Government had "sharpened the liabilities of intermediaries to deal with unlawful content; had reduced the time period to remove material from 1 month to 24 hours; and had removed the exemption from liability intermediaries enjoyed under section 79 of the IT Act if that intermediary fails to remove unlawful content." The changes were introduced "appreciating the fact that to effectively remove or disable access to unlawful content, it is imperative that action be initiated immediately since any delay in such action can render the same ineffective and futile."

Extraterritoriality and the conditional nature of s.79. The Court noted that the IT Act provides "extraterritorial jurisdiction and overriding application" so long as "the computer, computer system or computer network involved are located within India", and that although s.79(1) exempted intermediaries from certain liability, this was "not unqualified or unconditional and applied only if the intermediary fulfils certain conditions and obligations". It characterised the 2021 Rules as showing that the legislature intended s.79 to impose penal consequences on an intermediary who fails to observe the law — the exemption exists for intermediaries "only to the extent that they obey the Rules" — and referred to Shreya Singhal, which had held that an intermediary loses the s.79(1) exemption if it does not "expeditiously remove or disable access to" offending content despite receiving "actual knowledge".

The requirements for the exemption. The Court set them out: the intermediary's function is limited to "providing access to a communication system"; it does not initiate, select the receiver, nor select or modify the information; it "observes due diligence"; it has not "conspired, abetted or induced the commission of an unlawful act"; and it has not failed to "expeditiously remove or disable access" to content after receiving notice of its unlawfulness. It also noted that the IT Act makes the "directors, manager, secretary or other officer of a company" liable if the contravention of the Act or Rules has been because of that person's neglect.

Comparative law. The Court examined X v. Twitter [2017] NSWSC 1300 (Australia); Google Spain SL v. AEPD, Case C‑131/12 (CJEU); Eva Glawischnig‑Piesczek v. Facebook Ireland, Case C‑18/18 (CJEU); Google Inc. v. Equustek Solutions Inc., 2017 SCC 34 and Equustek v. Jack, 2018 BCSC 610 (Canada); and Indian authorities including Swami Ramdev v. Facebook Inc., 2019 SCC OnLine Del 10701, YouTube LLC v. Geeta Shroff, 2018 SCC OnLine Del 9439, and ABC v. DEF, CS(OS) No. 160/2017.

Global removal and no prior restraint. Reasoning by reference to Google Inc. v. Equustek, the Court stressed that for its order to be effective "even within India", an intermediary would have to block the content globally — something a search engine can do with ease. At the same time, responding to Google's submissions, the Court stressed that it did not contemplate any "prior restraint or blanket‑ban", and that it was "conscious" that it should not place an "untenable burden" on an intermediary, ensuring that any directions are possible to carry out and proportionate in removing only the offending content. The Court highlighted that X's photographs were not obscene or offensive in themselves, but had been taken from her social media accounts without consent.

Decision and principle evolved
  1. The petition was dismissed — but only after the Court issued detailed directions and suggestions as to what a court should direct, and to whom, in cases of this kind.
  2. Effective and implementable directions are essential at the interim stage; otherwise subsequent adjudication is rendered infructuous. A takedown order that leaves the content accessible elsewhere is no order at all.
  3. Directions must run to a chain of actors — the website owner/host (who alone can remove the source page), the search engines (who must de‑index), the ISPs (who must block), and the law enforcement agencies — and must address re‑publication, not merely the first upload.
  4. Section 79 is a conditional safe harbour; the 2021 Rules "widen the scope of losing the safe harbour" and make the exemption available only to the extent the intermediary obeys the Rules.
  5. No prior restraint, no blanket ban, no untenable burden — directions must be proportionate and confined to the offending content.
  6. Global de‑indexing is a proportionate and feasible remedy for a search engine in appropriate cases.
💡 Exam takeaways
  • This is the answer to the "victims' delay" problem identified in §6.4 above: rather than dilute Shreya Singhal's reading‑down, the Court made the court order itself more useful — by directing a chain of intermediaries and requiring de‑indexing, not merely source‑page removal.
  • Cite it for the proposition that a search engine is an intermediary with a "social obligation" to be proactive in de‑indexing illegal content once it has knowledge — it is "unfathomable" that search engines "feign helplessness" in the face of links to illegal content.
  • Cite it for the 2021 Rules' architecture — the 36‑hour removal clock, the 72‑hour information‑provision clock, the Grievance Officer, and the SSMI obligations.
  • Note the doctrinal limit: the Court was careful to disclaim prior restraint and blanket bans, expressly invoking My Space's warning about intermediaries removing content whose context they cannot evaluate.
  • Do not confuse it with the later Sahyog‑portal litigation, in which a different petition challenged the use of s.79(3)(b) and Rule 3(1)(d) as an independent executive takedown power. That is a separate and unresolved controversy.

17.2 Critical analysis — the four live debates

⚖️ Debate 1 — The victims' delay versus private censorship

Shreya Singhal's reading‑down of "actual knowledge" to require a court order or Government notification was necessary to prevent intermediaries from becoming unaccountable private censors adjudicating contested claims — defamation, IP infringement, obscenity — without judicial oversight. But it generated a persistent secondary problem: victims of clearly unlawful content (revenge pornography, defamation, harassment) must obtain a court order before an intermediary is legally obliged to act, a process that can take months, during which the harm compounds through re‑posting. The 2021 Rules' 36‑hour takedown timeline — tightened to 3 hours for court/Government orders and 2 hours for NCII by the 2026 Amendment — partially addresses this through rapid‑response incentives. But the core doctrinal gap, that private notice alone cannot trigger the loss of safe harbour, remains constitutionally necessary and practically unresolved. X v. Union of India is the leading judicial response: not a dilution of Shreya Singhal, but a more effective use of the court order itself, directed at a chain of actors.

⚖️ Debate 2 — Specific knowledge versus proactive monitoring

My Space insists that knowledge must be specific to particular infringing works, rejecting general or catalogue‑wide awareness as a trigger for liability. Yet the 2021 and 2026 Rules increasingly require or encourage proactive detection tools — for CSEAM, for non‑consensual intimate imagery, and now for synthetically generated information. This creates a visible tension between the judicially endorsed "notice‑specific" model and the executively driven "proactive monitoring" trend. The countervailing authority is Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish (2024): if possession of child sexual abuse material is independently punishable under s.15 POCSO and s.67B, then platform‑level detection duties follow for the most serious categories — a different logic from copyright's.

⚖️ Debate 3 — Corporate versus personal liability of officers

Avinash Bajaj distinguishes corporate intermediary liability from the personal liability of individual officers, and does so through s.85's due‑diligence proviso. But the IT Act contains no dedicated "officer in default" provision analogous to company‑law equivalents, leaving courts to develop the distinction case by case. X v. Union of India noted that the IT Act makes "directors, manager, secretary or other officer of a company" liable where the contravention is because of that person's neglect. For problem questions naming individual directors or officers of platform companies, the answer must separate the two heads of liability explicitly.

⚖️ Debate 4 — Traceability versus privacy

Rule 4(2) of the 2021 Rules requires an SSMI to enable identification of the first originator of information, on the order of a court or a competent authority under s.69(3), for specified serious offences. On end‑to‑end encrypted platforms this requires weakening or breaking encryption for everyone — a direct Article 21 privacy intrusion post‑K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1 — and one the Supreme Court in Shreya Singhal was plainly unwilling to contemplate. The litigation (WhatsApp v. Union of India, Delhi High Court, pending as of 2026) remains unresolved by the Supreme Court. State the mandate, the privacy objection, the unresolved status — and note that traceability is triggered only judicially or by a competent authority, not by administrative fiat.

Nov–Dec 2025Q.7 · 10–12 marks"Explain intermediary liability under the IT Act as amended, with case law; discuss obligations regarding removal of unlawful content."
Model answer

Thesis. Intermediary liability in India rests on a conditional safe harbour: s.79(1) exempts an intermediary from liability for third‑party information, data or communication links made available or hosted by it, notwithstanding anything contained in any law, but only if it satisfies the s.79(2) conditions and does not fall within the s.79(3) disqualifiers. The content of the obligation is supplied not by the Act but by the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, as amended in 2022, 2023, 2025 and 2026.

(1) Who is an intermediary — s.2(1)(w). Any person who, on behalf of another, receives, stores or transmits an electronic record or provides any service with respect to it — including telecom and internet service providers, web‑hosting providers, search engines, online‑payment sites, online‑auction sites, online market places and cyber cafés (s.2(1)(na)). The definition is record‑specific and the list is inclusive; intermediary status is a question of fact for trial (Google India v. Vishakha Industries, 2019 SCC OnLine SC 1587).

(2) The safe harbour — s.79(2). Three gates: (a) function limited to providing access; or (b) the intermediary does not initiate the transmission, select the receiver, or select or modify the information; and (c) in every case, it observes due diligence and the prescribed guidelines. The correct formulation, per the Division Bench in Amazon Seller Services v. Modicare, FAO(OS) 133/2019 (31 January 2020), is "either Section 79(2)(a) or Section 79(2)(b), and Section 79(2)(c)". s.79 is an affirmative defence, not a blanket immunity (My Space), and it is for the plaintiff to establish the wrong before the defence is invoked.

(3) The disqualifiers — s.79(3). The safe harbour is lost where the intermediary has conspired, abetted, aided or induced the unlawful act, or where, on actual knowledge or Government notification, it fails to expeditiously remove or disable access without vitiating the evidence.

(4) "Actual knowledge" — the Shreya Singhal reading‑down. In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, (2015) 5 SCC 1, the Supreme Court read s.79(3)(b) down so that actual knowledge arises only from a court order or a notification by the appropriate Government or its agency, and correspondingly read down Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules. Two caveats: the order or notification must conform to the Article 19(2) subject matters, and "unlawful acts" beyond Article 19(2) cannot form part of s.79. In copyright claims, however, a specific, work‑and‑URL‑identifying notice from the rights‑holder suffices without a court order (My Space).

(5) Obligations regarding removal of unlawful content. Under the 2021 Rules as amended: Rule 3(1)(a) publish rules, privacy policy and user agreement; Rule 3(1)(b) inform users of the prohibited‑content categories and take reasonable efforts to prevent them; Rule 3(1)(d) remove or disable access on a court order or Government notification — within 36 hours, and since 20 February 2026 within 3 hours; Rule 3(2) Grievance Officer, 24‑hour acknowledgement and 7‑day resolution; Rule 3(2)(b) act on non‑consensual intimate imagery within 2 hours; Rule 3(3) automated detection, prominent labelling and provenance metadata for synthetically generated information; Rule 4 enhanced SSMI obligations. Non‑compliance forfeits the safe harbour and exposes the intermediary to prosecution under any law.

(6) Case law. Shreya Singhal (s.79(3)(b) read down); My Space (specific knowledge in copyright; s.79 and s.81's proviso read harmoniously; no catalogue‑wide injunction; 36‑hour notice‑and‑takedown substituted); Google India v. Visaka (AP HC 2011) (s.79 a fact‑dependent defence; failure to act on notice defeats it; a company can be prosecuted); Google India v. Vishakha (SC 2019) (intermediary status a trial question; pre‑substitution s.79 did not protect against ss.499/500 IPC); Vyakti Vikas Kendra v. Jitender Bagga (36‑hour takedown directed, anticipating Shreya Singhal); Avinash Bajaj (corporate versus personal liability of officers); Amway v. 1Mg as set aside by Amazon v. Modicare (no passive/active distinction); X v. Union of India (effective, implementable, chain‑wide directions; global de‑indexing; no prior restraint).

Conclusion. The regime is a judicially supervised balance: immunity conditioned on passivity and diligence, with the duty to remove triggered only by a court order or Government notification (save in copyright), and with the executive steadily expanding the content of due diligence — most recently toward automated detection and deepfake labelling. The enduring question is how far due diligence may be pushed toward proactive monitoring before it collides with the specific‑knowledge principle that both Shreya Singhal and My Space were at pains to protect.
Nov–Dec 2025Q.4 · problem questionAn intermediary allows creators to upload videos; one contains obscene/child content. Discuss the liability of the creator and of the platform.
Model answer

Thesis. The two liabilities are separate and differently sourced. The creator answers as author under the substantive criminal law; the platform answers, if at all, under s.79, and only if it has forfeited the safe harbour.

(1) The creator's liability. Under the IT Act: s.67 (publishing or transmitting obscene material in electronic form — up to 3 years and ₹5 lakh on first conviction, 5 years and ₹10 lakh on second); s.67A (material containing sexually explicit acts — up to 5 years and ₹10 lakh on first conviction, 7 years on conviction or failure to pay); and, if the content involves a child, s.67B — which punishes not only publishing or transmitting but also collecting, seeking, browsing, downloading, advertising, promoting, exchanging or distributing material depicting children in a sexual act or conduct, and expressly extends to possession for storage or distribution, with the Explanation defining "children" as persons under 18. Under Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish, 2024 SCC OnLine SC 2611, possession of child sexual abuse and exploitative material is independently punishable under s.15 POCSO as well as s.67B. Concurrently, the creator faces s.294 BNS (sale of obscene objects to a minor / obscene acts and songs) and, where the material involves a minor, the POCSO Act itself. Where the content is synthetically generated, the 2026 Amendment Rules add labelling and provenance obligations on the platform side.

(2) The platform's liability — run the s.79 test. Gate 1 (s.79(2)(a) or (b)): a video platform provides services in addition to access, so it must satisfy s.79(2)(b) — it does not initiate transmission (the creator does), does not select the receiver (any user may view), and does not select or modify the content (upload and playback are automated). On the My Space reasoning, automated format conversion and recommendation do not amount to "modification" at the prima facie stage. Gate 2 (s.79(2)(c)): has it published terms tracking Rule 3(1)(b), appointed a Grievance Officer, and complied with the takedown clocks? Gate 3 (s.79(3)): has it conspired, abetted, aided or induced? Has it received a court order or Government notification and failed to act within the prescribed clock?

(3) The critical point. A private complaint from a viewer — however graphic — does not by itself forfeit the safe harbour after Shreya Singhal. But the 2021 Rules' grievance machinery and the 2‑hour NCII clock create a practical duty to act quickly, and the 2026 Amendment Rules' automated‑detection obligations for CSEAM and NCII push toward proactive detection. Applying S. Harish's possession‑liability logic, a platform's own storage of the material may attract independent s.67B exposure where it lacks adequate detection systems — the sharpest available argument against the platform.

Conclusion. The creator is liable under ss.67/67A/67B (and POCSO and s.294 BNS); the platform is liable only if it fails the s.79(2) gates or falls within s.79(3). The decisive questions are (i) whether it received a court order or Government notification and failed to act within the clock, and (ii) whether, for the most serious categories, its failure to deploy detection systems amounts to possession or facilitation under s.67B as explained in S. Harish.
Essay10–15 marksCritically analyse the Supreme Court's treatment of s.79(3)(b) in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India and its continuing relevance to platform content-moderation practice.
Model answer

Thesis. The reading‑down of s.79(3)(b) is the single most consequential judicial intervention in Indian intermediary law: it converted a provision that would have made platforms private censors into one that reserves the trigger for removal to judicial and executive authority — and it left a practical gap that the 2021 Rules and X v. Union of India have been struggling to close ever since.

(1) The problem. Read literally, s.79(3)(b) required an intermediary to remove content upon receiving "actual knowledge" from any source — including, on Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules, an "affected person in writing or through e‑mail signed with electronic signature". The petitioners argued this forced intermediaries to exercise their own judgment on contested claims, without any of the safeguards that s.69A and the 2009 Blocking Rules provided.

(2) The Court's reasoning. s.79 is an exemption provision, closely related to the offence provisions; under s.69A, blocking can occur only by a reasoned order after procedural safeguards including a hearing to the originator and intermediary; and "the intermediary applying its own mind… is noticeably absent" from that scheme. Practically, it would be "very difficult for intermediaries like Google, Facebook etc. to act when millions of requests are made and the intermediary is then to judge as to which of such requests are legitimate and which are not."

(3) The holding. s.79(3)(b) read down so that actual knowledge arises only on a court order or a notification by the appropriate Government or its agency; Rule 3(4) read down correspondingly so that "the knowledge spoken of in the said sub‑rule must only be through the medium of a court order"; and the two caveats — the order or notification must conform to Article 19(2), and "unlawful acts beyond what is laid down in Article 19(2) obviously cannot form any part of Section 79".

(4) Critical analysis — the case for the reading‑down. It prevents privatisation of censorship; it avoids a chilling effect on lawful speech; it aligns intermediary duties with the procedural safeguards of the 2009 Blocking Rules; and it is consistent with the Court's general hostility to overbroad speech offences, demonstrated in the same judgment by the striking down of s.66A.

(5) Critical analysis — the case against, or at least the cost. It leaves victims without a swift remedy: a victim of revenge pornography, harassment or defamation must obtain a court order before the intermediary is legally obliged to act, and by then the content has multiplied. It creates an asymmetry: a platform may know with certainty that content is unlawful, and yet be entitled to leave it up. And it produces a jurisdictional oddity — a Government notification, but not a private complaint, triggers the duty, even where the complaint is better evidenced.

(6) Continuing relevance. (a) The reading‑down is the reason the 2021 Rules are drafted around a Grievance Officer and a 36‑hour clock rather than a private‑complaint trigger. (b) The 2026 Amendment Rules compress the clock to 3 hours for court/Government orders and 2 hours for NCII — an executive acceleration that works within the Shreya Singhal framework rather than against it. (c) X v. Union of India (2021) is the leading judicial response: it does not dilute the reading‑down but makes the court order itself effective by directing a chain of actors — host, search engines (de‑indexing), ISPs (blocking) and law enforcement — and by addressing re‑publication. (d) The reading‑down also explains the copyright exception in My Space: where no Article 19(1)(a) interest is engaged, a specific notice suffices.

Conclusion. The reading‑down of s.79(3)(b) is constitutionally necessary and remains the foundation of Indian intermediary law. Its cost — delay for victims — is being managed not by reopening the constitutional question but by making the court order faster, broader and harder to evade, and by expanding the content of due diligence through the Rules. The unresolved frontier is the 2026 Rules' push toward proactive monitoring, which tests how far the executive may go before it trespasses on the principle that an intermediary should not adjudicate contested claims on its own judgment.
Essay10–15 marksDiscuss how the Delhi High Court in My Space Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. harmonised s.79 of the IT Act, the proviso to s.81, and s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act, 1957.
Model answer

Thesis. The Division Bench's achievement was to read three provisions — which the Single Judge had treated as competing regimes — as supplementary parts of a single scheme, and to replace an unworkable catalogue‑wide injunction with a specific, 36‑hour notice‑and‑takedown regime.

(1) The three provisions. s.79 exempts an intermediary from liability for third‑party information, subject to s.79(2) and (3). s.81 gives the IT Act overriding effect, but its proviso preserves the right of any person to exercise any right under the Copyright Act, 1957 or the Patents Act, 1970. s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act deems copyright infringed where a person "permits for profit any place to be used for the communication of the work to the public", unless he "was not aware and had no reasonable ground for believing" that the communication would be an infringement.

(2) The interpretive problem. The proviso to s.81 was inserted at the same time as s.79 was substituted, which led the Single Judge to conclude that the Copyright Act was a complete code and that s.79 could not be read harmoniously with it. MySpace argued the opposite: if Parliament intended an exception to the s.79 defence, it would have placed a proviso within s.79.

(3) The Court's harmonisation. Parliament's simultaneous addition of the proviso showed an intention not to disturb copyright holders' rights — and the Parliamentary Standing Committee's view that IT Act rights are "supplementary to and not in derogation of" the Copyright Act was a relevant (though not conclusive) tool. But "the true intent of Section 79 is to ensure that in terms of globally accepted standards of intermediary liabilities… an intermediary is granted certain protections… Section 79 grants a measured privilege to an intermediary. However, that would not mean that the rights guaranteed under the Copyright Act are in any manner curtailed." s.79 is neither an enforcement provision nor does it list out any penal consequences; it provides an affirmative defence. Reading them otherwise would leave copyright owners remediless (but for the proviso) and hold intermediaries liable irrespective of due diligence — discouraging investment and driving platforms to jurisdictions with laxer laws, which would make content harder, not easier, to take down.

(4) The knowledge standard under s.51(a)(ii). The Court rejected the Single Judge's "general awareness" test: in a virtual world where millions of videos are uploaded daily, "knowledge has a definite connotation, i.e. a consciousness or awareness and not mere possibility or suspicion of something likely". Automated, non‑human format modification and advertisement insertion do not amount to knowledge; only software requiring human approval can attribute knowledge, and that is a trial question. The onus is on the plaintiff to identify the specific works and their URLs. Following Viacom v. YouTube, "general knowledge that infringement is ubiquitous does not impose a duty on the service provider to monitor or search its service for infringement."

(5) The remedy. The Single Judge's order was set aside and substituted: SCIL to provide an updated catalogue of specific works with their locations/URLs; MySpace to remove or block access within 36 hours in accordance with Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules; and MySpace to keep an account of removals, viewings and proportionate advertisement revenue to enable calculation of damages at trial. The Court rejected a prospective injunction against future works — there can be no copyright in works not yet in existence, and such an order would be "impossible to monitor". Quoting Reno v. ACLU, it described the width of the directions as "to burn the house to roast the pig".

(6) Critical assessment. The harmonisation is principled: it preserves the copyright owner's substantive rights through the s.79 framework rather than despite it, and it protects both fair use and the rights of licensed uploaders, remix creators and adaptors, whom a catalogue‑wide filter would have prejudiced. But it is an interlocutory decision, and the extent of infringement, the revenue attributable to it, and the degree of human control over the automated processes were left for trial. The "specific knowledge" standard is also copyright‑specific: it does not automatically transpose to defamation or obscenity, which are governed by s.79(3)(b)'s "actual knowledge" as read down in Shreya Singhal.

Conclusion. My Space is the definitive Indian authority on copyright‑specific intermediary liability. Its three propositions — specific knowledge, not general awareness; s.79 and s.81's proviso read harmoniously, not as competing regimes; and no catalogue‑wide, prospective blocking obligations — remain the framework within which every Indian platform and rights‑holder now operates.
Essay10–15 marksCritically examine the due diligence obligations of Significant Social Media Intermediaries under the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, as amended in 2022, 2023 and 2026.
Model answer

Thesis. The Rules create a two‑tier regime: baseline due diligence for every intermediary under Rule 3, and enhanced, scale‑triggered obligations for Significant Social Media Intermediaries — those with more than 50 lakh registered users in India — under Rule 4. The amendments between 2022 and 2026 have progressively expanded both tiers, most recently toward automated detection and labelling of synthetically generated information.

(1) Baseline obligations (Rule 3). Publish rules, privacy policy and user agreement (Rule 3(1)(a)); inform users of the Rule 3(1)(b) prohibited‑content categories and take reasonable efforts to prevent their publication (Rule 3(1)(b), as amended in 2022); notify users at least annually (Rule 3(1)(f)); appoint a Grievance Officer, acknowledge complaints within 24 hours and resolve within 7 days (Rule 3(2), as amended in 2026); act on court‑ordered or Government‑notified unlawful content within 3 hours (Rule 3(1)(d), reduced from 36 hours by the 2026 Amendment); act on non‑consensual intimate imagery within 2 hours (Rule 3(2)(b), reduced from 24 hours); and, since 2026, deploy automated tools to prevent unlawful SGI, and prominently label permissible SGI with permanent metadata, provenance markers and a unique identifier (Rule 3(3)).

(2) SSMI obligations (Rule 4). Appoint a Chief Compliance Officer, a Nodal Contact Person (24×7) and a Resident Grievance Officer, all resident in India; enable identification of the first originator of information, on the order of a court or a competent authority under s.69(3), for specified serious offences; provide a voluntary user verification mechanism; and publish a monthly compliance report. Since the 2026 Amendment, an SSMI must also take reasonable and proportionate technical measures to verify user declarations about synthetic content.

(3) Part III — the Digital Media Ethics Code. A separate, three‑tier grievance structure for publishers of news and current affairs content, online curated content and OTT platforms: self‑regulation by the publisher (with an India‑based Grievance Redressal Officer); an industry self‑regulatory body headed by a retired Supreme Court or High Court judge or an eminent person; and an Inter‑Departmental Committee headed by the Secretary, Ministry of Information and Broadcasting, which hears only escalated grievances but may order deletion, modification or sanctions.

(4) The consequence of non‑compliance. Failure to observe the Rules forfeits the s.79 safe harbour, exposing the intermediary to prosecution under any law. As the Delhi High Court put it in X v. Union of India, the exemption exists for intermediaries "only to the extent that they obey the Rules".

(5) Critical analysis. (a) Proportionality and scale: concentrating obligations on platforms above 50 lakh users is rational, but it creates a compliance asymmetry — global firms can deploy moderation infrastructure at scale; smaller domestic intermediaries cannot. (b) Vagueness: "reasonable efforts" (Rule 3(1)(b)) and "reasonable and proportionate technical measures" are undefined, leaving platforms to guess at the standard and incentivising over‑removal — the real censorship risk is private, not state, action. (c) The monitoring drift: Rule 3(3)'s automated‑detection obligations sit uneasily with My Space's insistence that knowledge be specific and that courts will not impose catalogue‑wide monitoring, though S. Harish supports detection duties for CSEAM. (d) Traceability: Rule 4(2)'s first‑originator mandate is constitutionally contested on Article 21 privacy grounds and remains unresolved (WhatsApp v. Union of India). (e) Part III's reach: regulating news and curated content through an executive committee chaired by a Ministry Secretary raises free‑speech concerns that the Shreya Singhal reasoning on vagueness and overbreadth makes very difficult to dismiss.

Conclusion. The SSMI regime is the most ambitious intermediary‑regulation architecture in India's history and has been amended five times between 2021 and early 2026. It has demonstrably "sharpened" intermediary liability and created real accountability structures — but at the cost of vague standards, an unresolved traceability mandate, and a steady drift toward proactive monitoring that tests the limits of the specific‑knowledge principle the courts have twice affirmed.
DU self‑learning exercisePrinted in the DU case material, p.125"Due diligence under section 79 of the Information Technology Act has been narrowed whereas the safe harbour rule is wide. Analyse with reasons in support of the statement." [Hint — Amazon Seller Services Pvt. Ltd. v. Amway India Enterprises Pvt. Ltd., Del (DB), decided on 31 January 2020]
Model answer

Understanding the proposition. The statement captures a genuine asymmetry in the drafting and judicial construction of s.79. The safe harbour — s.79(1) — is drafted in the widest possible terms: "notwithstanding anything contained in any law for the time being in force", an intermediary "shall not be liable for any third party information, data, or communication link made available or hosted by him". The due diligence condition — s.79(2)(c) — by contrast, has been construed narrowly and demandingly. The result is that the door is wide but the keyhole is small.

(1) Why the safe harbour is wide. (a) The non‑obstante clause: after the 2008 Amendment, the exemption operates notwithstanding any law — the IPC, the Copyright Act, the Trade Marks Act — and not merely, as under the pre‑2008 text, "under this Act, rules or regulations made thereunder". (b) The only restrictions written into the section are s.79(2) and s.79(3): "but subject to the provisions of sub‑sections (2) and (3)". As the Division Bench put it in My Space, "irrespective of any other law, an intermediary is guaranteed a safe harbor and the only restriction comes from Section 79(2) and (3)." (c) The s.81 proviso does not oust it: "proviso to Section 81 does not preclude the affirmative defence of safe harbor for an intermediary in case of copyright actions." (d) No passive/active distinction: "there does not appear to be any distinction between passive and active intermediaries so far as the availability of the safe harbour provisions are concerned" (Amazon v. Modicare). (e) Value‑added services do not dilute it — s.2(1)(w) itself envisages that intermediaries may provide them. (f) The knowledge trigger is narrow: after Shreya Singhal, actual knowledge means a court order or Government notification, not a private complaint; and after My Space, in copyright, only a specific, work‑and‑URL‑identifying notice suffices — a catalogue‑wide list is no notice at all.

(2) Why due diligence has been narrowed. (a) In Amway v. 1Mg, the Single Judge held that the "bare minimum" required to avail of the exemption under s.79(2)(c) was "to observe due diligence required under Section 79(2)(c) of the IT Act", and that this had to be measured "as per the Platforms' own policies, and as per the Intermediary Guidelines 2011" — with the consequence that "non‑compliance with the Platforms' own policies would take them out of the ambit of the safe harbour". This converts a statutory standard into a self‑referential one: the stricter a platform's published policy, the stricter the duty it has breached. (b) The 2021 Rules converted due diligence into a long, itemised checklist — publication, user notification, grievance officer, takedown clocks, preservation, monthly reports — each item a potential point of failure. (c) The 2026 Amendment Rules narrowed it further still: a 3‑hour takedown clock, a 2‑hour NCII clock, 7‑day grievance resolution, and mandatory automated detection, labelling and provenance for synthetic content. (d) Even in My Space — the most intermediary‑friendly decision in the field — the Court held that "if copyright owners, such as SCIL inform MySpace specifically about infringing works and despite such notice it does not take down the content, then alone is safe harbor denied": the safe harbour is conditional on acting, not merely on having policies.

(3) Reconciling the two — the Division Bench's answer. In Amazon v. Modicare the Division Bench set aside the Single Judge's judgment, holding that she had "misinterpreted Section 79 of the IT Act in concluding that is restricted to 'passive' intermediaries", that value‑added services do not dilute the safe harbour, and that "the enforcement of such a policy is another matter" — the obligation under s.79(2)(c) is to publish the policy, not necessarily to enforce every clause of it. The Court also restored the correct burden sequence: the plaintiff must first establish a violation of its rights before the affirmative defence of s.79 is invoked. In effect, the Division Bench widened the safe harbour back out and relaxed the narrowing of due diligence that the Single Judge had effected.

(4) Assessment — is the statement still true? Partly, and increasingly so. The judicial widening of the safe harbour (no passive/active distinction; value‑added services irrelevant; specific knowledge required) has been matched and overtaken by an executive narrowing of due diligence: five amendments between 2021 and 2026, hour‑scale takedown clocks, automated detection mandates, and labelling obligations that go well beyond anything the courts have required. The safe harbour remains wide in principle and narrow in practice, because compliance with the Rules at internet scale is itself the binding constraint.

Conclusion. The statement is accurate as a description of the gap between the breadth of s.79(1) and the specificity of the due‑diligence condition, and the 2021–2026 amendments have widened that gap further. But Amazon v. Modicare corrects an important over‑narrowing: due diligence under s.79(2)(c) is a duty to publish and maintain lawful‑content policies and to act on the triggers the law recognises — not a duty to enforce every term of the platform's own policy against every third party.
DU self‑learning exercisePrinted in the DU case material, p.125"Under section 79 of the IT Act, 2000, the court held — for an intermediary it is required that they initiate transmission, select the receiver of the transmission, select or modify the information contained in the transmission. 'One can be intermediary and still be deprived of the safe harbour provision.' Under section 2(1)(w) of the IT Act, 2000, is it necessary that an intermediary must be active or passive? Under what condition can the safe harbour rule under section 79 be made applicable to passive players in the online medium which are placed outside India?"
Model answer

(1) Correcting the premise. The proposition as printed inverts the statutory language. s.79(2)(b) requires that the intermediary does not (i) initiate the transmission, (ii) select the receiver of the transmission, and (iii) select or modify the information contained in the transmission. The three are things the intermediary must refrain from doing, and they are conjunctive — all three must be absent. In My Space the Division Bench applied the test limb by limb and found the requirements prima facie satisfied, precisely because MySpace did none of them: it did not initiate (the "share" feature's "usage… rests purely in the hands of third party users"), it did not select the receiver ("anyone with Internet access can open its website and be a receiver/viewer"), and its modification was "to the format and not to the content" and was "an automatic process without either MySpace's tacit or expressed control or knowledge".

(2) "One can be an intermediary and still be deprived of the safe harbour." This is correct, and s.79(3) explains why. The two disqualifiers are conspiracy, abetment, aiding or inducement of the unlawful act (s.79(3)(a)) and failure to expeditiously remove material upon actual knowledge or Government notification, without vitiating the evidence (s.79(3)(b)). Both are consistent with full intermediary status under s.2(1)(w). Google India v. Visaka Industries is the illustration: Google India was squarely an intermediary — a web‑hosting service provider running a Google Group — and yet lost the exemption because, despite notice, it "did not move its little finger to block the said material". Status and protection are separate questions.

(3) Active or passive? Under s.2(1)(w), the definition is functionally neutral — it turns on whether the entity receives, stores, transmits or provides a service with respect to a third party's electronic record, not on how commercially engaged it is. Under s.79, the position has been settled by the Division Bench in Amazon Seller Services v. Modicare: "there does not appear to be any distinction between passive and active intermediaries so far as the availability of the safe harbour provisions are concerned." What matters is which gate applies: a pure conduit satisfies s.79(2)(a); an entity providing services in addition to access must satisfy s.79(2)(b) — showing that it did not initiate the transmission, select the receiver, or select or modify the information. Value‑added services (warehousing, logistics, packaging, payment collection, returns) do not dilute the safe harbour, because s.2(1)(w) itself envisages them. The contrary view of the Single Judge in Amway v. 1Mg — that a platform providing such services is a "massive facilitator" outside the safe harbour — was set aside. So: an intermediary need not be passive; but it must satisfy the applicable s.79(2) gate and must not fall within s.79(3).

(4) Passive players placed outside India. The safe harbour is available to them, subject to three conditions. (a) Jurisdiction: under s.75, the Act applies to offences and contraventions committed outside India where the computer, computer system or computer resource is located in India, and intermediaries are brought within the provision by notification under s.75(2). The doctrinal filter is the targeting / purposeful availment test (Banyan Tree Holding v. A. Murali Krishna Reddy): mere accessibility of a website in India is not enough; there must be a deliberate targeting of the Indian market. MySpace's India‑centric website, its India office and its India‑specific operations were exactly the kind of facts that established targeting. (b) The applicable gate: a passive offshore player — a caching server, a conduit, a hosting provider that does not select or modify content — will ordinarily satisfy s.79(2)(a) (function limited to providing access) or s.79(2)(b), together with s.79(2)(c) due diligence. (c) Compliance with the takedown regime: it must act on a court order or Government notification — and, as X v. Union of India (2021) held by reference to Google Inc. v. Equustek Solutions, 2017 SCC 34, a search engine can, with comparative ease, implement global de‑indexing, so offshore location is not a technological excuse. Conversely, where the platform is not targeted at India and holds no Indian computer resource, an Indian court would ordinarily decline jurisdiction.

Conclusion. s.79(2)(b) is a list of things an intermediary must not do; status as an intermediary under s.2(1)(w) does not guarantee the safe harbour, which is lost on conspiracy/abetment or on failure to act on actual knowledge; s.79 draws no active/passive distinction, but the applicable gate depends on whether the entity merely provides access or provides services in addition to access; and an offshore passive player can claim the safe harbour provided Indian jurisdiction is properly engaged under s.75 and the targeting test, and provided it complies with the takedown regime.
Short notes5 marks eachWrite short notes on any five of the following.
Model answer skeletons

(a) Intermediary — s.2(1)(w). Any person who, on behalf of another, receives, stores or transmits an electronic record or provides any service with respect to it; includes telecom, network and internet service providers, web‑hosting providers, search engines, online‑payment sites, online‑auction sites, online market places and cyber cafés. Record‑specific; inclusive list; status is a question of fact for trial (Google India v. Vishakha, 2019).

(b) Cyber café — s.2(1)(na). A facility offering internet access to the public in the ordinary course of business. Expressly included in the s.2(1)(w) list, hence an intermediary owing s.79 due diligence, and additionally regulated by the IT (Guidelines for Cyber Café) Rules, 2011: registration with a designated agency; verification of every user's identity (photograph where identity cannot be proved); a log register maintained for one year; monthly reports to the licensing agency; and inspection by a police officer not below the rank of inspector. Read with s.67C preservation and s.72A's prohibition on disclosure in breach of a lawful contract.

(c) The safe harbour under s.79. s.79(1) exempts an intermediary, notwithstanding any law, from liability for third‑party information, data or communication links. Conditional on s.79(2)(a) or (b) and (c); lost under s.79(3)(a) or (b). An affirmative defence, not a blanket immunity (My Space), pleaded by the defendant after the plaintiff establishes a wrong (Amazon v. Modicare).

(d) "Actual knowledge" after Shreya Singhal. Read down to mean a court order or a notification by the appropriate Government or its agency, conforming to the Article 19(2) subject matters; "unlawful acts" beyond Article 19(2) cannot form part of s.79; Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules read down correspondingly. Copyright exception: in a s.51(a)(ii) claim, a specific, work‑and‑URL‑identifying notice from the rights‑holder suffices (My Space).

(e) The 36‑hour rule and its successors. Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules required action within 36 hours of actual knowledge, with 90‑day preservation; read down in Shreya Singhal so that knowledge must come through a court order. Applied judicially in Vyakti Vikas Kendra v. Jitender Bagga (2012) and in the substituted relief in My Space (2016); codified as Rule 3(1)(d) of the 2021 Rules; reduced to 3 hours by the Amendment Rules, 2026, with a 2‑hour clock for non‑consensual intimate imagery.

(f) Corporate versus personal liability of officers. Avinash Bajaj v. State (Del HC, 29 May 2008): a prima facie case was made out against the company under ss.292(1)(a) and 292(2)(d) IPC and s.67 IT Act; the charges under s.292 IPC against the MD individually were quashed, but the prosecution under s.67 read with s.85 IT Act continued. Under s.85, persons in charge of and responsible for the conduct of a company's business are liable unless they prove the offence was committed without their knowledge or that they exercised all due diligence. The IT Act has no dedicated "officer in default" provision.

(g) Synthetically generated information (SGI). Defined by the Amendment Rules, 2026 (in force 20 February 2026) as audio, visual or audio‑visual information artificially or algorithmically created, generated, modified or altered using a computer resource so as to appear authentic, depicting persons, events or scenes that have not occurred. Rule 2(1A) treats SGI as "information" for Rule 3(1)(b), 3(1)(d) and Rules 4(2) and 4(4). Rule 3(3) requires automated detection of unlawful SGI, prominent labelling, prefixed audio disclosure, and permanent metadata/provenance with a unique identifier, which the intermediary must not allow to be removed.

19.2 Common exam mistakes — avoid every one of these

  • Treating s.79 as absolute immunity. Always work through s.79(2)'s preconditions and s.79(3)'s exceptions before concluding that an intermediary is protected.
  • Confusing "intermediary status" with "safe harbour". An entity can be an intermediary and still lose the protection — Google India v. Visaka is the authority.
  • Remembering only the s.66A holding of Shreya Singhal. The case also upheld s.69A and read down s.79(3)(b). The s.79 limb is the one that matters in this Unit.
  • Saying that any private complaint triggers the takedown duty. It does not — only a court order or a Government notification, except in copyright.
  • Applying My Space's "specific knowledge" standard uncritically to every context. It was decided in the copyright context, under s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act, and does not transpose identically to defamation or obscenity.
  • Citing the 2011 Intermediaries Guidelines Rules as current law. They were superseded by the 2021 Rules, which have been amended five times.
  • Citing the Single Judge in Amway v. 1Mg without noting the reversal. The Division Bench in Amazon v. Modicare set the judgment aside.
  • Getting the s.79(2) connectors wrong. (a) and (b) are joined by "or"; (c) is cumulative; the three limbs of (b) are joined by "and".
  • Forgetting the evidence‑preservation limb of s.79(3)(b) — removal must be effected "without vitiating the evidence in any manner", read with s.67C and the Second Amendment Rules, 2026.
  • Omitting Part III and the SSMI obligations. The syllabus names the Digital Media Ethics Code expressly.
  • Ignoring the burden‑of‑proof sequence. Plaintiff establishes the wrong first; only then does the defendant invoke s.79.
  • Writing about s.79 without mentioning s.81's proviso and s.51(a)(ii). In any copyright‑flavoured question, all three must appear.
