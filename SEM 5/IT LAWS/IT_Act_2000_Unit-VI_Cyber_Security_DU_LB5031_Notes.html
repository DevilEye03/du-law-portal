🎯 THE ONE IDEA THAT CARRIES THIS ENTIRE UNIT

Unit V asked how far a private platform can go in controlling third‑party content. Unit VI turns the question around and asks how far the State can go in controlling the internet itself. The IT Act gives the executive three distinct and formidable powers: s.69 (intercept, monitor or decrypt the content of communications), s.69A (block public access to information), and s.70 (declare critical computer resources "protected systems", with unauthorised access punishable by up to ten years). Each is procedurally fenced by the 2009 Rules, and each is now governed by a single constitutional yardstick — the legality → legitimate aim → proportionality test laid down by the nine‑Judge Bench in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1.

  • s.69 — covert access to the content of a targeted communication. Grounds: sovereignty and integrity of India, defence, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, preventing incitement to a cognizable offence, or investigation of any offence. Non‑compliance by a subscriber or intermediary: up to 7 years.
  • s.69A — overt denial of public access to already‑existing information. Grounds identical to s.69 but without the general "investigation" limb. Intermediary non‑compliance: up to 7 years.
  • s.70 — declaration of a protected system; unauthorised access: up to 10 years — the second‑most severe penalty in the Act after cyber terrorism (s.66F, life imprisonment).
  • s.69B — authorise an agency to monitor and collect traffic data for cyber security. s.70A — the NCIIPC as National Nodal Agency. s.70B — CERT‑In, which issued the April 2022 Directions mandating 6‑hour incident reporting and 180‑day log retention.
  • The interface: Shreya Singhal upheld s.69A while striking down s.66A; Puttaswamy made privacy a fundamental right; Anuradha Bhasin held that indefinite internet shutdowns are impermissible and must be periodically reviewed and published. The right to be forgotten, expressly named in the syllabus, remains uncodified in India.

If you remember one sentence for the exam: Every exercise of the ss.69, 69A and 70 powers must satisfy the Puttaswamy three‑fold test — legality, legitimate aim, and proportionality (including least‑restrictive means and periodic review) — and s.69A survived constitutional challenge in Shreya Singhal precisely because, unlike s.66A, it is narrowly drawn and procedurally safeguarded.

📍 Five things to keep straight before you start
  • s.69 and s.69A are different powers and must never be conflated. s.69 is covert interception of content for intelligence and investigation; s.69A is overt blocking of public access to content already published. Different objects, different procedural machinery, different constitutional profiles. Students routinely lose marks by treating them as one power.
  • The 2009 Rules are the real law of both powers. s.69(2) and s.69A(2) both say only that the "procedure and safeguards" shall be "such as may be prescribed". Everything concrete — the competent authority, the Committee, the hearing, the review — lives in the IT (Procedure and Safeguards for Interception, Monitoring and Decryption of Information) Rules, 2009 and the IT (Procedure and Safeguards for Blocking for Access of Information by Public) Rules, 2009. Learn the two rule‑books separately.
  • Shreya Singhal is a Unit VI case as well as a Unit V case. In the same judgment the Court struck down s.66A (vague and overbroad) and upheld s.69A (narrowly drawn with adequate safeguards). Never describe the judgment as simply "anti‑government" — the discriminating application of the vagueness doctrine within one judgment is the point an examiner is looking for.
  • Puttaswamy supplies the master test, and you must name it. Legality, legitimate state aim, proportionality. Omitting the three‑fold test by name is the single most common lost‑marks error in this Unit. Anuradha Bhasin then extends it to network‑wide shutdowns: no indefinite suspension, periodic review, publication of orders.
  • There is no codified right to be forgotten in India. The syllabus names it, so you must address it — but the accurate answer is that India has only a fragmented body of High Court decisions (Delhi, Karnataka, Kerala, Gujarat) drawing on Google Spain SL v. AEPD (CJEU, 2014) and the Puttaswamy privacy right, and the narrower erasure right in s.12 of the DPDP Act, 2023. Saying "India has a GDPR‑style right to be forgotten" is a straight mark‑loss.

1Cyber security — the concept and the statutory architecture

Start here  The IT Act has no single "cyber security" chapter. Chapter XI contains the offences (ss.65–75); Chapter XI‑A and the group of sections 69 to 70B contain the State's coercive and protective powers. This Unit is about those powers — what they are, how they are procedurally fenced, and how they are constitutionally limited.

1.1 What "cyber security" means for this syllabus

Cyber security is the body of law, practice and technology directed at protecting computer resources, computer networks, and the information they hold from unauthorised access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification or destruction. For the purposes of Unit VI it is used in a narrower, state‑power sense: the legal framework by which the State (a) monitors communications (s.69), (b) blocks public access to information (s.69A), (c) collects traffic data (s.69B), and (d) protects critical infrastructure (ss.70, 70A, 70B).

The syllabus places all four in a single cluster headed "National Security", and then adds the critical qualifier — "and its interface with freedom of speech and privacy, right to be forgotten". That qualifier is the actual subject matter of the Unit. The powers are not controversial because they exist; they are controversial because of what they do to Articles 19(1)(a) and 21.

⚠️ The comparison that always gets examined

Note carefully the asymmetry in the grounds. s.69 permits interception "for preventing incitement to the commission of any cognizable offence relating to above or for investigation of any offence" — the general "investigation of any offence" limb makes s.69 a broad investigative tool. s.69A has no equivalent general investigative limb: blocking is confined to the Article 19(2)‑proximate grounds. That textual difference is precisely why the Supreme Court described s.69A as "a narrowly drawn provision with several safeguards" and why the distinction between the two powers is doctrinally significant, not merely terminological.

💡 Real example — how the powers look in practice

In 2018, the Ministry of Home Affairs issued a statutory order under Rule 4 of the 2009 Interception Rules authorising ten central agencies to intercept, monitor and decrypt information generated, transmitted, received or stored in any computer resource. The MHA was careful to emphasise that the order conferred no new powers and that every individual case still required prior approval of the Union Home Secretary. That single episode illustrates the whole Unit: the power is statutory, the procedure is the safeguard, and the public controversy is about whether the safeguard is real — the exact question Puttaswamy answered with the three‑fold test.

2Interception and monitoring — s.69 and the 2009 Interception Rules

2.1 The provision

📜 Section 69 — Power to issue directions for interception or monitoring or decryption of any information through any computer resource

(1) Where the Central Government or a State Government or any of its officers specially authorised by the Central Government or the State Government, as the case may be, in this behalf may, if satisfied that it is necessary or expedient so to do in the interest of the sovereignty or integrity of India, defence of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States or public order or for preventing incitement to the commission of any cognizable offence relating to above or for investigation of any offence, it may, subject to the provisions of sub‑section (2), for reasons to be recorded in writing, by order, direct any agency of the appropriate Government to intercept, monitor or decrypt or cause to be intercepted or monitored or decrypted any information generated, transmitted, received or stored in any computer resource.

(2) The procedure and safeguards subject to which such interception or monitoring or decryption may be carried out, shall be such as may be prescribed.

(3) The subscriber or intermediary or any person in‑charge of the computer resource shall, when called upon by any agency referred to in sub‑section (1), extend all facilities and technical assistance to — (a) provide access to or secure access to the computer resource generating, transmitting, receiving or storing such information; or (b) intercept, monitor, or decrypt the information, as the case may be; or (c) provide information stored in computer resource.

(4) The subscriber or intermediary or any person who fails to assist the agency referred to in sub‑section (3) shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to seven years and shall also be liable to fine.

Dissect it into five elements — this is the structure an examiner wants:

Element 1 — Who may issue the direction

The Central Government or a State Government, or an officer specially authorised by it. Critically, the direction is issued not to the intermediary but to an agency of the appropriate Government — the intermediary is only the object of the assistance duty in s.69(3).

Element 2 — The satisfaction

The authority must be satisfied that it is necessary or expedient so to act. Note the breadth: "necessary or expedient" is a disjunctive, executive‑friendly standard, in contrast to the "necessary"‑only language used in the 2017 Suspension Rules and read strictly in Anuradha Bhasin.

Element 3 — The enumerated grounds

Sovereignty and integrity of India; defence of India; security of the State; friendly relations with foreign States; public order; preventing incitement to the commission of any cognizable offence relating to the above; and — uniquely — "for investigation of any offence". This last limb is the textual source of s.69's breadth: it permits interception in ordinary criminal investigation, not merely for national‑security purposes.

Element 4 — The form and the record

The direction must be an order, must record reasons in writing, and must be subject to the procedure and safeguards prescribed under s.69(2). The reasons requirement is what makes the order judicially reviewable under Article 226 — the same logic the Supreme Court relied on when upholding s.69A.

Element 5 — The assistance duty and the penalty

On being called upon, the subscriber or intermediary or any person in charge must extend all facilities and technical assistance — to provide or secure access, to intercept/monitor/decrypt, or to provide stored information. Failure carries up to seven years' imprisonment and a fine. This is the operative obligation on telecom operators, ISPs, email providers and device custodians.

⚠️ The privacy objection to the confidentiality rules — and why it matters in Ratan Tata

Rules 15–17 make interception material secret. That protects lawful interception but also shields unlawful leakage from scrutiny: a victim whose intercepted conversation has been published cannot readily discover the source of the leak, because the State can invoke confidentiality. This is precisely the gap that Ratan Tata exposed, and the reason the case sits in this Unit. Say so explicitly in an exam — the critique is the mark.

Why you must mention both. A complete answer on interception law in India is not an s.69 answer — it is an s.69 + s.5(2) Telegraph Act answer. The Radia tapes in Ratan Tata were intercepted under the Telegraph Act, not the IT Act. Conversely, most modern messaging surveillance proceeds under s.69. The two regimes have converged procedurally (they share the same competent authority and the same Review Committee) but diverge on grounds — and that divergence is where a critical answer earns its marks.

⚖️ Doctrinal anchor — People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India, (1997) 1 SCC 301

The foundational pre‑Puttaswamy decision on telephone tapping. The Court held that the right to hold a telephone conversation in the privacy of one's home or office without interference can certainly be claimed as "right to privacy"; that "telephone conversation is an important facet of man's private life"; and that "telephone‑tapping would, thus, infract Article 21 of the Constitution of India unless it is permitted under the procedure established by law." It also held that because telephone conversations are an exercise of the freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a), interception must be a reasonable restriction under Article 19(2). Finding that s.5(2) of the Telegraph Act contained no procedural safeguards, the Court laid them down judicially — the template that Rule 419A and the 2009 Interception Rules later codified. Learn the PUCL three‑fold point: interception must be (i) under a procedure established by law, (ii) necessary in the enumerated interests, and (iii) minimally invasive. That is the same architecture Puttaswamy later restated as legality → legitimate aim → proportionality.

💡 Real examples
  • The Radia tapes (2008–2009). The Income Tax Department, acting on a complaint from the Finance Minister dated 16 November 2007, obtained Home Secretary authorisation under s.5(2) of the Telegraph Act and intercepted roughly 5,800 of Niira Radia's conversations. Some were leaked and published by Outlook and Open in November 2010. This is the factual matrix of Ratan Tata — and the reason that case, though listed in the IT Act syllabus, is in substance a Telegraph Act case.
  • Lawful interception by telecom operators. Every Indian ISP and telecom operator maintains a lawful‑interception gateway that a s.69 agency can access on production of the competent authority's order. The operator's legal exposure runs the other way too: it must comply with s.69(3) or face seven years.

3Blocking of public access — s.69A and the 2009 Blocking Rules

The most important section in this Unit  s.69A is the only power in the ss.69–70B cluster that the Supreme Court has expressly upheld on its merits. Its procedural safeguards are set out verbatim in the DU case material at Shreya Singhal paras 107–111. Learn it in detail.

3.1 The provision, verbatim from the DU case material

📜 Section 69A — Power to issue directions for blocking for public access of any information through any computer resource

(As set out in paragraph 2 of Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, (2015) 5 SCC 1 — DU case material, p. 132)

"(1) Where the Central Government or any of its officers specially authorised by it in this behalf is satisfied that it is necessary or expedient so to do, in the interest of sovereignty and integrity of India, defence of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States or public order or for preventing incitement to the commission of any cognizable offence relating to above, it may subject to the provisions of sub‑section (2), for reasons to be recorded in writing, by order, direct any agency of the Government or intermediary to block for access by the public or cause to be blocked for access by the public any information generated, transmitted, received, stored or hosted in any computer resource.

(2) The procedure and safeguards subject to which such blocking for access by the public may be carried out, shall be such as may be prescribed.

(3) The intermediary who fails to comply with the direction issued under sub‑section (1) shall be punished with an imprisonment for a term which may extend to seven years and shall also be liable to fine."

Note the four textual features that did the work in Shreya Singhal: (i) the grounds are confined to Article 19(2)‑proximate subjects; (ii) reasons must be recorded in writing, making the order assailable under Article 226; (iii) the direction runs to an agency of the Government or an intermediary; and (iv) the whole power is "subject to the provisions of sub‑section (2)" — i.e. to the procedural safeguards the 2009 Rules supply.

3.3 The Shreya Singhal reasoning on s.69A — paras 108 to 111

📜 The four propositions that saved s.69A

Para 109: "It will be noticed that Section 69A unlike Section 66A is a narrowly drawn provision with several safeguards. First and foremost, blocking can only be resorted to where the Central Government is satisfied that it is necessary so to do. Secondly, such necessity is relatable only to some of the subjects set out in Article 19(2). Thirdly, reasons have to be recorded in writing in such blocking order so that they may be assailed in a writ petition under Article 226 of the Constitution."

Para 110: "The Rules further provide for a hearing before the Committee set up — which Committee then looks into whether or not it is necessary to block such information. It is only when the Committee finds that there is such a necessity that a blocking order is made. It is also clear from an examination of Rule 8 that it is not merely the intermediary who may be heard. If the 'person' i.e. the originator is identified he is also to be heard before a blocking order is passed. Above all, it is only after these procedural safeguards are met that blocking orders are made and in case there is a certified copy of a court order, only then can such blocking order also be made. It is only an intermediary who finally fails to comply with the directions issued who is punishable under sub‑section (3) of Section 69A."

Para 111: "Merely because certain additional safeguards such as those found in Section 95 and 96 CrPC are not available does not make the Rules constitutionally infirm. We are of the view that the Rules are not constitutionally infirm in any manner."

The petitioners' attack (recorded at para 108) is worth stating because it shows you the whole argumentative structure:

Petitioners' attack on s.69A and the 2009 Rules
  • No pre‑decisional hearing is afforded by the Rules, particularly to the "originator" of information — defined in s.2(1)(za) as a person who sends, generates, stores or transmits any electronic message, or causes any electronic message to be sent, generated, stored or transmitted to any other person.
  • Procedural safeguards such as those provided under ss.95 and 96 of the Code of Criminal Procedure are not available.
  • The confidentiality provision (Rule 16) affects the fundamental rights of the petitioners.
Why the attack failed
  • s.69A is narrowly drawn: the grounds are Article 19(2)‑proximate.
  • Rule 8 does afford a hearing — to the originator where identified, and to the intermediary in every case, with at least 48 hours' notice.
  • The CrPC safeguards in ss.95 and 96 were not mandatory analogues; their absence does not render the Rules infirm.
  • Confidentiality is a reasonable adjunct to a blocking power, not an infringement.

3.4 How s.69A interlocks with s.79 and the 2021 Rules

🔗 The interlock — always make this connection
  • s.69A is the power; s.79 is the safe harbour; Rule 3(1)(d) of the 2021 Rules is the bridge. An intermediary that complies with a s.69A blocking direction, or with a court order or Government notification under Rule 3(1)(d), does not forfeit its s.79 protection. Indeed, in Shreya Singhal the Court read s.79(3)(b) down precisely because the s.69A scheme already required a reasoned order after a hearing — an intermediary should not be made to adjudicate on its own what the State may only block through that procedure.
  • The penalty is the same. s.69A(3) punishes an intermediary who fails to comply with a blocking direction with up to seven years; s.69(4) punishes a subscriber or intermediary who fails to assist with interception with up to seven years.
  • Rule 3(1)(d) as amended in 2026 requires removal or blocking of content covered by a court order or Government notification within three hours. So the practical timeline of a s.69A blocking is now: complaint → Nodal Officer → Designated Officer → Committee → hearing (≥48 hours) → Secretary DoIT approval → order → 3‑hour compliance.
💡 Real example

When the Government of India blocks a website, the sequence that actually runs is the Rule 6–8 procedure: a complaint goes to the Nodal Officer of, say, the Ministry of Home Affairs or the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting; it is transmitted to the Designated Officer (a Joint Secretary in MeitY); a Committee of Government officers examines it; the originator and the intermediary get at least 48 hours' notice; and only after the Secretary, DoIT, approves does the blocking order issue. The "48 hours" is not a formality — it is the constitutional safeguard. The emergency route under Rule 9 bypasses it, but only for a maximum of 48 hours pending the Committee's recommendation.

⚠️ The transparency deficit — the critique that earns marks

Rule 16 imposes strict confidentiality on requests, complaints and actions taken. In practice this has meant that blocking orders are frequently not published, that the confidentiality provision has been invoked to resist RTI disclosure of blocking grounds, and that affected originators and intermediaries report inconsistent notice‑and‑hearing compliance. Anuradha Bhasin's insistence that suspension orders be published and made available for judicial scrutiny is, in this sense, a corrective gloss — but whether it extends to individual s.69A content‑blocking orders (as opposed to area‑wide internet suspensions) remains an unresolved doctrinal question. Flag it as unresolved; do not resolve it for the examiner.

4Protected systems, NCIIPC and CERT‑In — ss.69B, 70, 70A, 70B

The protective limb  Where ss.69 and 69A are about watching and silencing, ss.69B, 70, 70A and 70B are about defending: collecting traffic data to spot intrusions, ring‑fencing critical infrastructure, and building national response capacity. These provisions attract far less controversy — but they carry the Act's second‑highest penalty.

4.1 Section 69B — monitoring and collection of traffic data for cyber security

📜 Section 69B — Power to authorise to monitor and collect traffic data or information through any computer resource for cyber security

(1) The Central Government may, to enhance cyber security and for identification, analysis and prevention of intrusion or spread of computer contaminant in the country, by notification in the Official Gazette, authorise any agency of the Government to monitor and collect traffic data or information generated, transmitted, received or stored in any computer resource.

(2) The procedure and safeguards subject to which such monitoring and collection of traffic data or information may be carried out, shall be such as may be prescribed.

(3) [Prescription of safeguards]
(4) The intermediary or any person in‑charge of the computer resource who fails to extend all facilities and technical assistance to the authorised agency shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to seven years and shall also be liable to fine.

🔍 s.69B is not s.69 — know the difference
  • Object: s.69B authorises collection of traffic data — who is talking to whom, when, from where, in what volume — not content. Section 69 authorises interception of content.
  • Trigger: s.69B is triggered by the purpose of enhancing cyber security and preventing intrusion or the spread of computer contaminant. It is not a law‑enforcement investigative power and has no "investigation of any offence" limb.
  • Form: s.69B is exercised by notification in the Official Gazette — a general, standing authorisation to an agency — whereas s.69 requires a case‑specific reasoned order of the competent authority.
  • Privacy posture: because traffic data is metadata rather than content, s.69B has attracted far less judicial attention. But post‑Puttaswamy the proposition that metadata is privacy‑neutral is no longer tenable: Puttaswamy expressly recognised informational privacy and the autonomy of the individual over data about herself.

4.2 Section 70 — protected systems

📜 Section 70 — Protected system

(1) The appropriate Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, declare any computer resource which directly or indirectly affects the facility of Critical Information Infrastructure, to be a protected system.

Explanation. — For the purposes of this section, "Critical Information Infrastructure" means the computer resource, the incapacitation or destruction of which, shall have debilitating impact on national security, economy, public health or safety.

(2) The appropriate Government may, by order in writing, authorise the persons who are authorised to access protected systems notified under sub‑section (1).

(3) Any person who secures access or attempts to secure access to a protected system in contravention of the provisions of this section shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to ten years and shall also be liable to fine.

(4) The Central Government shall prescribe the information security practices and procedures for such protected system.

Breaking s.70 into four components
  1. Who declares — the "appropriate Government" (Centre or State), by notification in the Official Gazette.
  2. What qualifies — a computer resource that "directly or indirectly affects the facility of" Critical Information Infrastructure. Note the breadth of "indirectly": a billing system, a logistics database or a supplier portal can be caught.
  3. What CII means — a resource whose "incapacitation or destruction" would have a "debilitating impact on national security, economy, public health or safety". The test is consequence‑based, not sector‑based.
  4. The offence — securing or attempting to secure access in contravention. Note that mere attempt is enough; there is no requirement of damage, and the punishment is ten years.
⚠️ The comparative point

Ten years is the second‑highest punishment in the IT Act. Only s.66F (cyber terrorism) carries more — life imprisonment. Unauthorised access to a protected system is therefore treated more severely than hacking (s.43/66, up to three years), cheating by personation (s.66C, up to three years), violation of privacy (s.66E, up to three years) or disclosure of information in breach of contract (s.72A, up to three years). State the hierarchy in your answer — it is a short, memorable point that examiners reward.

⚖️ The privacy critique of the 2022 Directions — a genuine Unit VI point

The 5‑year VPN subscriber retention and 180‑day in‑India log retention obligations sit uneasily with the Puttaswamy proportionality framework. Three specific objections: (1) the retention is indiscriminate — it applies to all users rather than to suspects, which is the precise vice the CJEU identified in Digital Rights Ireland (2014) and Tele2 Sverige/Watson (2016) when striking down blanket data‑retention mandates; (2) there is no access control — retention is required without specifying who may access the data or on what authority; and (3) there is no deletion obligation independent of the rolling period. The counter‑argument is that s.70B(6) is an express statutory authorisation, so the legality limb of Puttaswamy is satisfied, and that a six‑hour reporting duty is a reasonable and proportionate response to escalating attacks. The honest exam answer states both and leaves the question open — the Supreme Court has not definitively ruled on the Directions.

💡 Real examples of protected systems and CII

  • Banking and payment switches. NPCI's payment infrastructure, core banking systems and ATM networks are designated protected systems; unauthorised access is punishable by up to ten years.
  • UIDAI's Aadhaar infrastructure and NIC's government networks — both the target and the subject of protected‑system notifications.
  • Practical illustration of CERT‑In's reach. A Mumbai SaaS company discovers at 10 a.m. that a former employee's credentials were used to access its production database at 3 a.m. Under the 2022 Directions it must file an incident report with CERT‑In by 4 p.m. the same day — six hours from awareness, not from the completion of its forensic review. That is what s.70B looks like in practice.

5The constitutional interface — freedom of speech, privacy and the right to be forgotten

The heart of the Unit  The syllabus does not ask you to learn four sections and two rule‑books. It asks you to study those powers "and its interface with freedom of speech and privacy, right to be forgotten". This section supplies the constitutional framework; the five case briefs that follow apply it.

5.1 Two fundamental rights are engaged at once

5.2 The Puttaswamy three‑fold test — the master formula

📜 The three‑fold test — state it BY NAME in every answer

Any state action that infringes a fundamental right — including every exercise of the ss.69, 69A, 69B and 70 powers — must satisfy three requirements:

  1. LEGALITY — the action must be authorised by a valid law — "the law" in the sense of an enacted law or a valid subordinate legislation, not merely executive fiat.
  2. LEGITIMATE AIM — the State must pursue a legitimate constitutional aim — for a restriction on Article 19(1)(a), one of the subject matters in Article 19(2); for an Article 21 intrusion, a compelling state interest such as national security or public order.
  3. PROPORTIONALITY — the means adopted must have a rational nexus with the aim; must be necessary (no equally effective but less restrictive alternative); and must not be disproportionate in their territorial and temporal scope.

The three sub‑limbs of proportionality, as the Supreme Court has refined them (drawing on the German necessity test and the Canadian Oakes test, and expressly on Justice Chandrachud's formulation in the Aadhaar case): (i) a legitimate State aim; (ii) a rational nexus between the restrictive measures, the situation in fact, and the goal sought; (iii) necessity — the measures must be the least restrictive means reasonably available; and (iv) the restriction must not be disproportionate in its territorial and temporal scope to the object pursued.

💡 The aphorism to quote

Lord Diplock's formulation, quoted with approval in Anuradha Bhasin: "you must not use a steam hammer to crack a nut, if a nutcracker would do" (R v. Goldsmith, [1983] 1 WLR 151, 155). One sentence, high recall value, and it captures the whole of the proportionality limb.

5.3 How Shreya Singhal applied the framework to blocking

Because Shreya Singhal is the DU case for this Unit, the s.69A limb of the reasoning must be reproduced in constitutional terms. The Court's analysis proceeds in three steps:

Step 1 — Is s.69A a restriction on Article 19(1)(a)?

Yes. Blocking public access to information is a direct interference with the right to receive and disseminate information — a fortiori because the internet is a democratic medium and access to it is integral to Article 19(1)(a).

Step 2 — Is the restriction saved by an Article 19(2) ground?

Yes, and precisely so. The s.69A(1) grounds — sovereignty and integrity of India, defence of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, and preventing incitement to a cognizable offence relating to the above — track the Article 19(2) subject matters exactly. Contrast s.66A, whose grounds ("annoyance, inconvenience, danger, obstruction, insult, injury, criminal intimidation, enmity, hatred or ill‑will") lay wholly outside Article 19(2).

Step 3 — Is the procedure reasonable and just?

Yes. The 2009 Rules supply a hearing to the originator and the intermediary, a reasoned written order, judicial review under Article 226, and a Review Committee. The Court rejected the argument that the absence of ss.95–96 CrPC safeguards rendered the Rules infirm. Result: s.69A and the 2009 Rules upheld; s.66A struck down — in the same judgment.

🎯 The contrast that is the whole point

s.66A was struck down because it was vague (the terms "grossly offensive", "menacing", "annoyance", "inconvenience", "insult" were undefined and indefinable, using the Collins dictionary to demonstrate the range of meanings), overbroad (it swept in advocacy, discussion and dissent), disproportionate (three years' imprisonment for causing annoyance), and chilling (it had been used to arrest people for posting innocuous comments on Facebook). s.69A was upheld because it was narrowly drawn, ground‑confined, reason‑based, and procedurally safeguarded. Never describe Shreya Singhal as simply an anti‑government judgment — the discriminating application of the vagueness and overbreadth doctrine within one judgment is exactly what the examiner is testing.

5.4 From content‑blocking to network shutdown — Anuradha Bhasin

Shreya Singhal dealt with the blocking of specific content under s.69A. The adjacent question — whether the State may suspend the internet itself across a territory — was answered in Anuradha Bhasin. The Court held that the freedom of speech and expression, and the freedom to practise any profession or carry on any trade, business or occupation, through the medium of the internet, are protected by Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g); that any restriction must be in consonance with Articles 19(2) and 19(6), inclusive of the test of proportionality; and that an order suspending internet services indefinitely is impermissible under the Temporary Suspension of Telecom Services (Public Emergency or Public Service) Rules, 2017. The operative directions were: suspension may be for a temporary duration only; any suspension order must adhere to proportionality and must not extend beyond the necessary duration; suspension orders are subject to judicial review; the Review Committee must conduct a periodic review every seven working days; and all existing suspension orders must be reviewed and those not complying revoked. The Court also directed that all orders under s.144 CrPC and the Suspension Rules be published, and held that repetitive s.144 orders would be an abuse of power.

5.5 The right to be forgotten — the syllabus's hardest question

📜 The accurate position as of August 2026

India has no codified right to be forgotten. There is no provision in the IT Act, and no dedicated statute, conferring an EU‑style right. What exists is: (1) the erasure right in s.12 of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, operationalised by the DPDP Rules, 2025 — a narrower right, tied to the withdrawal of consent or the exhaustion of the specified purpose; and (2) a fragmented and non‑uniform body of High Court decisions — from Delhi, Karnataka, Kerala and Gujarat — recognising a limited right to have outdated or irrelevant personal information delinked from search results or removed from court‑judgment repositories, reasoned from the Puttaswamy privacy right. There is no Supreme Court authority definitively settling the doctrine's scope.

⚠️ How to answer a "right to be forgotten" question without losing marks
  1. Begin by saying India has no codified right. Never open by asserting a right exists.
  2. Then give the DPDP Act s.12 erasure right — and explain why it is narrower: consent‑based and purpose‑based, not time‑based.
  3. Then give the High Court delinking jurisprudence, drawing on Google Spain and Puttaswamy, and flag that it is fragmented.
  4. Then identify the three unresolved tensions: (i) de‑listing versus primary‑source removal — the EU right is a de‑listing right against search engines, not a right to erase the underlying article; (ii) privacy versus open justice — court judgments and judicial records resist erasure; and (iii) who decides — in the EU it is the data controller, subject to regulator oversight; India has no equivalent adjudicatory mechanism.
  5. Close by noting the asymmetry: India's surveillance proportionality jurisprudence is now well developed post‑Puttaswamy/Anuradha Bhasin; its forgetting jurisprudence is not. That asymmetry is itself a strong closing line.

6Case brief 1 — Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (the s.69A and s.66A limb)

Case brief 1 · the foundational cyber‑security decision — printed in full in the DU case material

Shreya Singhal and Ors. v. Union of India and Ors.

Supreme Court of India Writ Petition (Criminal) No. 167 of 2012 Decided 24 March 2015 (2015) 5 SCC 1; AIR 2015 SC 1523 R.F. Nariman J. (for himself and J. Chelameswar J.)
📌 How to use this brief

This judgment appears in both Unit V (intermediary liability, where the s.79(3)(b) reading‑down is the point) and Unit VI (cyber security, where the s.66A / s.69A holding is the point). The brief below gives the complete picture, but concentrates on the cyber‑security limb: the striking down of s.66A, the upholding of s.69A and the 2009 Blocking Rules, and the striking down of s.118(d) of the Kerala Police Act. The s.79 limb is summarised in the box at the end and set out fully in Unit V §8.

01 Facts

Two women, Shreya Singhal and Shreya Singhal's co‑petitioner, were arrested in Maharashtra in November 2012 under s.66A for posting a comment on Facebook criticising the bandh called by Shiv Sena following the death of Bal Thackeray. One of them had posted that the people of Mumbai need not be silent about the shutdown for one man, even if he was a great man. The comment was "disliked" by another user, who lodged a complaint. Both were arrested and later released on bail.

A second set of petitioners was People's Union for Civil Liberties, whose founder, V. Suresh, was arrested in August 2012 under s.66A for circulating a message inviting people to an All India Conference on Paramilitary Forces on 21 August 2012.

Section 66A had been inserted by the Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008 with effect from 27 October 2009. A batch of writ petitions under Article 32 was filed challenging its constitutional validity, together with a related challenge to s.69A and the 2009 Rules made under it. The Statement of Objects and Reasons of the 2008 Amendment Act stated that a rapid increase in the use of the internet had given rise to new forms of crimes — publishing sexually explicit material in electronic form, video voyeurism, breach of confidentiality and leakage of data by intermediaries, e‑commerce frauds like phishing, identity theft, and offensive messages through communication services — so that penal provisions were required.

02 The impugned provisions

📜 s.66A — Punishment for sending offensive messages through communication service, etc.

"Any person who sends, by means of a computer resource or a communication device, — (a) any information that is grossly offensive or has menacing character; or (b) any information which he knows to be false, but for the purpose of causing annoyance, inconvenience, danger, obstruction, insult, injury, criminal intimidation, enmity, hatred or ill will, persistently by making use of such computer resource or a communication device; or (c) any electronic mail or electronic mail message for the purpose of causing annoyance or inconvenience or to deceive or to mislead the addressee or recipient about the origin of such messages, shall be punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years and with fine.

Explanation. — For the purposes of this section, terms "electronic mail" and "electronic mail message" means a message or information created or transmitted or received on a computer, computer system, computer resource or communication device including attachments in text, image, audio, video and any other electronic record, which may be transmitted with the message."

s.69A is set out verbatim in §3.1 above.

03 Issues

  1. Whether s.66A violates the freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a), and if so whether it is saved by any of the eight subjects in Article 19(2).
  2. Whether s.66A is vague and overbroad, and therefore arbitrary under Article 14 and violative of Article 21.
  3. Whether the petitioners' rights under Articles 14 and 21 are breached because there is no intelligible differentia between those who use the internet and those who use other media.
  4. Whether s.69A and the 2009 Blocking Rules are constitutionally valid.
  5. Whether s.79(3)(b) and Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Intermediaries Guidelines Rules are constitutionally valid. (Unit V limb)

04 Arguments of both sides

Petitioners
  • s.66A infringes Article 19(1)(a) and is not saved by any of the eight Article 19(2) subjects. "Causing annoyance, inconvenience, danger, obstruction, insult, injury, criminal intimidation, enmity, hatred or ill‑will are all outside the purview of Article 19(2)."
  • Vagueness. "In creating an offence, Section 66A suffers from the vice of vagueness because unlike the offence created by Section 66 of the same Act, none of the aforesaid terms are even attempted to be defined and cannot be defined, the result being that innocent persons are roped in as well as those who are not. Such persons are not told clearly on which side of the line they fall; and it would be open to the authorities to be as arbitrary and whimsical as they like in booking such persons under the said Section."
  • Insidious censorship and chilling effect. "The enforcement of the said Section would really be an insidious form of censorship which impairs a core value contained in Article 19(1)(a). In addition, the said Section has a chilling effect on the freedom of speech and expression."
  • Viewers' rights. "Also, the right of viewers is infringed as such chilling effect would not give them the benefit of many shades of grey in terms of various points of view that could be viewed over the internet."
  • Article 14. There is no intelligible differentia between those who use the internet and those who by words spoken or written use other mediums of communication. "To punish somebody because he uses a particular medium of communication is itself a discriminatory object and would fall foul of Article 14 in any case."
  • On s.69A: there is no pre‑decisional hearing, particularly for the "originator" (s.2(1)(za)); the safeguards of ss.95 and 96 CrPC are unavailable; and the confidentiality provision affects fundamental rights.
  • On s.79: the requirement to remove content on a private complaint forces intermediaries to become private censors.
Respondents (Union of India)
  • Mr. Tushar Mehta, ASG: the legislature is in the best position to understand the needs of the people; the Court will interfere with the legislative process only when a statute is clearly violative of the rights conferred.
  • Article 14 satisfied: there is an intelligible differentia between the internet and other media, having a rational relation to the object sought to be achieved.
  • A lower standard of scrutiny: the internet is a unique and rapidly evolving medium; there is no clear judicial opinion on what standard of scrutiny should apply; and the Court should apply a standard that gives "reasonable latitude to the legislature".
  • Practical necessity: a small amount of restriction on the internet is needed to prevent abuse by miscreants and violators; the internet works through meme‑like viral diffusion, which can cause rapid harm; and since the internet offers a vast amount of anonymity, restrictions on the medium are justified in a way they are not on print media.
  • s.69A is constitutionally valid because it is narrowly drawn and procedurally safeguarded.

The twelve‑point case for a lower standard (the ASG's "information revolution" argument) was considered and rejected at para 28: the Court accepted that there was an intelligible differentia for Article 14 purposes, but held that this did not relax the content scrutiny applicable under Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(2) — "we are unable to agree with the learned Additional Solicitor General that there is a virtual absence of standards for the adjudication of restrictions under Article 19(2), and that no standard is possible".

05 Reasoning — the free‑speech framework

(a) The discussion / advocacy / incitement trichotomy (para 13). The Court held that the right to free speech includes the right to think, the right to propagate, and therefore the right to discuss and the right to advocate — but that incitement stands on a different footing. Relying on Abrams v. United States on the "market place of ideas" and on Brandeis J. in Whitney v. California — "if there be time to expose through discussion the falsehood and fallacies… the remedy to be applied is more speech, not enforced silence" — the Court held that advocacy of even a change in the law is protected; incitement to break the law is not. It illustrated the difference by Mark Antony's speech in Shakespeare's Julius Caesar: Antony begins cautiously, shows the crowd Caesar's mantle, describes who struck Caesar where, and says "I only speak right on… I tell you that which you yourselves do know" — until the crowd cries "We'll mutiny". That is advocacy that tips over into incitement.

(b) The clear and present danger test. The Court traced the American doctrine from Schenck through Gitlow, Whitney and Dennis to Brandenburg v. Ohio, quoting the reformulation: "The constitutional guarantees of free speech and free press do not permit a State to forbid or proscribe advocacy of the use of force or law violation except where such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action." It also noted the further refinement of the "true threat" doctrine in Virginia v. Black and Watts v. United States.

(c) The four differences between the US First Amendment and Article 19 (para 15). (i) The US First Amendment says Congress shall make no law; Article 19(1)(a) says the State may make a law imposing reasonable restrictions. (ii) Article 19(2) lists the permissible restrictions; the US Constitution has no such list. (iii) The US doctrine is content‑neutral in a way Article 19(2) is not. (iv) Indian law has long recognised that community standards matter — hence Ranjit Udeshi and the Hicklin test, later moderated by Aveek Sarkar's contemporary community standards.

(d) The concentric circles (para 33). Relying on Ram Manohar Lohia and Arun Ghosh, the Court held that "public order" is a narrower concept than "law and order": public order requires the "even tempo of the life of the community" to be disturbed in a given area, taking the whole of it as a unit.

(e) The UK genealogy. The Court traced s.66A's ancestry through the UK's s.43 of the Telecommunications Act, 1984 and its successor s.127 of the Communications Act, 2003, noting that the UK offence was drafted in materially narrower terms and that the Indian provision had been drafted without the limiting qualifiers.

06 Reasoning — why s.66A failed

Article 19(2) groundWhy s.66A failed
Public orderNo proximate nexus between the speech and public disorder. The offence did not require any tendency to disturb public order at all — "causing annoyance, inconvenience, danger, obstruction, insult, injury, criminal intimidation, enmity, hatred or ill will" are not public‑order concepts. Applying the concentric‑circle test, s.66A would criminalise speech at a level far below public disorder.
DefamationDefamation is a definable, narrow ground under Art. 19(2). But s.66A criminalises far more than defamation — it catches speech that is merely "grossly offensive" or "menacing" without any reputational injury to a particular person.
Incitement to an offences.66A has no incitement requirement at all. It does not require that the message incite anyone to do anything unlawful — only that it be "grossly offensive" or "menacing", or be sent for the purpose of causing annoyance.
Decency or moralitys.66A has nothing to do with decency or morality. It is not an obscenity provision; the subject matter is offensive speech generally, not indecent or obscene speech.
Vagueness (Art. 14 / Art. 21)The Court used the Collins dictionary to show that "annoyance" has a range of meanings, "grossly offensive" is undefined, and "menacing" is undefined. "A person… cannot know which side of the line he falls on." The provision therefore permits arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement.
Chilling effectEven apart from the text, the provision's enforcement had produced arrests for innocuous Facebook comments, deterring protected speech by others.

The refusal to read down (paras 48–49). The ASG invited the Court to read s.66A down so as to exclude the offending limbs. The Court refused, holding that to do so would amount to a "wholesale substitution" of a new provision for the one Parliament had enacted — an impermissible exercise of the judicial power. The remedy for a provision that is void for vagueness and overbreadth is striking down, not severance.

07 Reasoning and decision — s.69A and the 2009 Rules

Set out in full at §3.3 above. In summary: s.69A was held to be narrowly drawn with several safeguards; its grounds are relatable only to some of the Article 19(2) subjects; reasons must be recorded in writing so that the order may be assailed in a writ petition under Article 226; the Rules provide for a hearing before the Committee, in which the originator as well as the intermediary may be heard; and the Review Committee meets every two months. The Court expressly rejected the argument that the absence of ss.95 and 96 CrPC safeguards made the Rules infirm. s.69A and the 2009 Rules were upheld.

08 Decision — the four‑point conclusion (para 119)

Held
  1. Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 is struck down in its entirety as violative of Article 19(1)(a) and not saved by any of the subject matters in Article 19(2), and as being vague and overbroad.
  2. Section 69A and the Information Technology (Procedure and Safeguards for Blocking for Access of Information by Public) Rules, 2009 are constitutionally valid and do not violate Articles 14, 19(1)(a) or 21.
  3. Section 79 of the Act and Section 79(3)(b) are valid, but Section 79(3)(b) is read down to mean that an intermediary receives "actual knowledge" only when it receives a court order or a notification by the appropriate Government or its agency; and Rule 3(4) of the Intermediary Guidelines Rules, 2011 is read down to the same effect.
  4. Section 118(d) of the Kerala Police Act is struck down as unconstitutional, being violative of Article 19(1)(a) and not saved by any Article 19(2) subject matter. (The Court also held that Entry 1 List III — criminal law — was the competent legislative entry, and that Entry 2 List II (police) did not save the provision.)
💡 Exam takeaways
  • Learn the four‑point conclusion by heart. Most students remember only point 1. Points 2 and 3 are the ones that matter for Unit VI.
  • The internal contrast is the exam answer. s.66A struck down; s.69A upheld — in the same judgment, by the same judge, on the same day. The distinguishing features are: grounds confined to Article 19(2) subjects; reasons recorded in writing; procedural safeguards including a hearing to the originator; and independent oversight by a Review Committee.
  • Quote the Court's own words on why s.69A survived: "a narrowly drawn provision with several safeguards… reasons have to be recorded in writing in such blocking order so that they may be assailed in a writ petition under Article 226 of the Constitution."
  • Note the Article 19(2) tracking. s.69A's grounds are a near‑verbatim recitation of the Article 19(2) subjects — sovereignty and integrity, security of the State, public order, friendly relations, incitement to a cognizable offence. This is why it survived.
  • For the interface with freedom of speech and privacy: cite this case for the proposition that the internet is a democratic medium whose restriction must be justified by an Article 19(2) ground and by a procedure that is "just, fair and reasonable".
🔗 The s.79 limb (Unit V) — for completeness

The Court held that s.79 is an exemption provision closely related to the offence provisions; that under s.69A blocking can occur only by a reasoned order after safeguards including a hearing; and that the intermediary applying its own mind as to whether to take down content is "noticeably absent" from that scheme. It would be "very difficult for intermediaries like Google, Facebook etc. to act when millions of requests are made". Accordingly, s.79(3)(b) and Rule 3(4) were read down. Two caveats: the order or notification must conform to the Article 19(2) subject matters, and "unlawful acts beyond what is laid down in Article 19(2) obviously cannot form any part of Section 79". See Unit V §6 for the full analysis.

7Case brief 2 — Ratan Tata v. Union of India

Case brief 2 · the Radia tapes — lawful interception versus unlawful disclosure

Ratan N. Tata v. Union of India and Ors.

Supreme Court of India Writ Petition (Civil) No. 98 of 2010 Filed 26–29 November 2010; heard on notice from 2 December 2010 Substantive directions 17 October 2013 and 29 April 2014; heard again September 2022 The “Radia tapes” litigation
📌 Provenance and an important caution

This judgment is not printed in the DU case material; it is cited in the syllabus by number and date. It is also a procedural rather than a final merits judgment — the writ petition was heard on notice, the CBI was directed to investigate the leak, and the Union of India ultimately submitted (in September 2022, before a Bench of Justices D.Y. Chandrachud, Hima Kohli and P.S. Narasimha) that no criminality had been found in fourteen preliminary inquiries and that, after Puttaswamy, nothing survived in the matter. Do not state that the Court “dismissed” or “allowed” the petition on the merits — the case is important for the issues it framed and the transitional privacy reasoning it embodies, not for a clean ratio.

01 Facts

Niira Radia was a corporate lobbyist whose public relations firm, Vaishnavi Corporate Communications, counted the Tata Group and Reliance among its clients. On 16 November 2007 the Finance Minister forwarded a complaint alleging that within nine years she had built a business empire worth ₹300 crore, that she was an agent of foreign intelligence agencies, and that she was indulging in anti‑national activities.

Acting on that complaint, the Directorate General of Income Tax (Investigation) obtained authorisation from the Home Secretary under s.5(2) of the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 — the pre‑IT‑Act interception power — and intercepted Radia's telephones. Between August 2008 and July 2009 approximately 5,800 conversations were recorded, including conversations with politicians, journalists, ministers, bureaucrats and industrialists — among them Ratan Tata, then Chairman of the Tata Group.

In 2010 the transcripts were leaked and published. On 28 April 2010 Pioneer reported (subsequently taken down after the threat of legal action) that Radia's intercepts revealed criminal culpability on the part of the national telecom minister. On 15 November 2010 the Centre for Public Interest Litigation placed a CD containing transcripts before the Supreme Court in an unrelated matter; on 18 November 2010 Outlook uploaded audio copies of 140 intercepted conversations to its website, and Open published an expose with transcripts and audio.

On 26–29 November 2010 Ratan Tata filed a writ petition under Article 32 seeking: (i) a writ of mandamus directing the authorities to retrieve, as far as possible, all recordings and transcripts; (ii) a direction to the CBI to conduct a thorough inquiry into the leak; and (iii) a direction that there be no further publication of the recordings, whether as audio files through the internet or as transcripts in any media, print or electronic. He did not question the right of statutory authorities to record private conversations, or the use of transcripts by probe agencies for investigative purposes — his concern, as his counsel Harish Salve put it, was that "the audio content of personal conversation should not be put in the public domain". He contended that Article 21 extended to the protection of his reputation and of those similarly situated, and that all conversations having no relevance to the purpose for which they were recorded must be put out of the media's reach.

04 The three issues the Supreme Court framed

The three broad issues — this framing is the case's lasting contribution
  1. Right to privacy vis‑à‑vis the Government — does the State owe a duty of confidentiality in respect of material it has lawfully intercepted, and what are the consequences of a failure of that duty?
  2. Right to privacy vis‑à‑vis the Press — does the freedom of the press under Article 19(1)(a) extend to publishing confidential material the disclosure of which is in the public interest, and if so, how is it to be balanced against the privacy of those whose conversations were recorded?
  3. Right to know information — what is the scope of the public's right to know, particularly in relation to large‑scale impropriety such as the 2G spectrum scandal?

03 Issues, more narrowly

  1. Was the interception itself lawful — i.e. was there procedural compliance with s.5(2) of the Telegraph Act and the safeguards laid down in PUCL and codified in Rule 419A of the Telegraph Rules, 1951?
  2. Did the unauthorised leak and publication of lawfully intercepted private conversations violate Tata's right to privacy under Article 21?
  3. Was the Government under a statutory or common‑law duty of confidentiality in respect of the intercepted material?
  4. Did the press have a right under Article 19(1)(a) to publish the intercepts in the public interest, and did that right outweigh the privacy interest?
  5. Was the Government accountable for failing to take adequate steps to protect the confidentiality of the material and to retrieve leaked copies?

04 Arguments of both sides

Petitioner (Ratan Tata)
  • The interception was for tax evasion; the publication was not. Since Radia's phone was tapped for the purpose of investigating a possible offence, the tapes could be used for that purpose alone. Conversations with no bearing on the case under investigation should have been put beyond the media's reach.
  • The Government owns the responsibility of maintaining the privacy of a citizen. The availability of the intercepts with the media shows they were either stolen or leaked; those who stole or leaked them should be punished.
  • Article 21 violation. Unauthorised publication of his private conversations infringed his fundamental right to life, which includes the right to privacy, and Article 21 extended to the protection of his reputation.
  • Article 19(1)(a) violation. Making public his conversations also violated his own right to freedom of speech and expression.
  • Remedies sought: retrieval of the recordings, a CBI inquiry into the leak, and an injunction against further publication.
Respondents (Union of India, Outlook, Open, CPIL)
  • The Government: the tapping of Radia's phone was legitimate and conducted under the Indian Telegraph Act; the government denied responsibility for the leaks. In its affidavit it stated that “it is not possible or practical for the government to take steps to retrieve the various copies of some of the transcripts which have appeared in the print media or in the electronic media and which are being circulated on the internet”, and that the Income Tax department could not take action against any cell phone service provider who may have been responsible for the unauthorised supply of information.
  • The media (Outlook and Open): publication was protected by the freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a) as an exercise of investigative journalism in the public interest. They described Tata's petition as a “private interest litigation cloaked in the form of public interest litigation”.
  • Prashant Bhushan, for the Centre for Public Interest Litigation: “These are not private conversations. These are conversations between a lobbyist and her clients… bureaucrats, journalists and ministers. They show glimpses of all kinds of fixing and deal‑making and show how the whole ruling establishment functions. There is enormous public interest in putting these tapes out. It is absurd for Mr. Tata to say that this is invasion of privacy.”
  • CPIL's connected petition sought the release of all 5,800 conversations into the public domain and the framing of guidelines, submitting that till date there was no data protection law in India.

05 Reasoning

On the legality of interception. The Court proceeded on the premise — established in People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India, (1997) 1 SCC 301 — that lawful interception, if procedurally compliant, is not itself unconstitutional. PUCL had read procedural safeguards into s.5(2) of the Telegraph Act: interception only on a public emergency or in the interest of public safety; orders by the Home Secretary; two‑month validity and six‑month maximum; and a Review Committee headed by the Cabinet Secretary at the Centre and the Chief Secretary in a State.

On the leak — the distinct wrong. The crucial analytical move, and the reason this case belongs in a cyber‑security Unit, is the separation of two legally distinct events:

Distinction 1 — the legality of interception versus the legality of disclosure

An interception may be perfectly lawful, and yet the subsequent disclosure of the material may be unlawful. The two are governed by different norms: the first by the power and its procedural safeguards; the second by the State's obligation to safeguard the confidentiality of information it lawfully obtains, which is an independent intrusion on privacy.

On the duty of confidentiality. The Court examined whether Rule 419A of the Indian Telegraph Rules, 1951 imposed a statutory duty of confidentiality on the State, and if not, whether a common‑law duty of confidentiality applied. The practical difficulties identified in the commentary on the case — the advisability of the interception power being available to tax authorities; the practice of continuous renewals stretching into many years; the absence of records of the statutory Review Committee; the absence of statutory state liability for privacy breaches; and the liability of whistle‑blowers — were the substance of the proceedings.

On the press. Tata's own pleadings shifted: in the main petition he pressed a simple privacy‑violation claim; in a supplementary affidavit he conceded that the news media had a free‑speech right to publish investigative reports in the public interest and shifted to a claim of statutory negligence on the part of the Government; and in a further affidavit he renewed his attack on the media. The Court, while issuing notice and seeking the Government's response, ultimately did not deliver a definitive judgment on the press's rights.

On the investigation. By order dated 17 October 2013 the Supreme Court directed the CBI to investigate the leak. By order dated 29 April 2014 it recorded the receipt of the CBI's report in sealed covers and directed the Registry not to open them without the leave of the Court. The matter then lay dormant for eight years.

On the September 2022 hearing. The Additional Solicitor General apprised the Bench that fourteen preliminary inquiries had been registered and that no criminality was found in the transcripts; that the CBI had approached the Court for permission to conduct one more inquiry but did not press it; and that, pursuant to the judgment of the nine‑Judge Bench in K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, nothing survived in the matter. She also identified the three broad issues the Court had formulated — privacy vis‑à‑vis the Government, privacy vis‑à‑vis the Press, and the right to know information.

Principle evolved
  1. The distinction between the legality of interception and the legality of subsequent disclosure. A procedurally compliant interception does not license the disclosure of the material. The State owes a duty — statutory if Rule 419A is so read, and at the very least a common‑law duty — to maintain the confidentiality of intercepted communications.
  2. Privacy in telephone conversations is protected, but the right is not absolute. Following PUCL and R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu, the right to privacy inheres in telephone conversations and is protected by Article 21; but it must be balanced against the public interest and the press's Article 19(1)(a) right.
  3. The Court framed the three‑way tension — privacy against the Government, privacy against the Press, and the right to know — that later constitutional decisions have had to resolve. This framing, rather than any holding, is the case's contribution.
  4. A transitional precedent. Decided before Puttaswamy formally recognised privacy as a fundamental right with a proportionality framework, the case illustrates the pre‑Puttaswamy privacy jurisprudence that Puttaswamy later consolidated and strengthened.
⚠️ The criticism — and why it is a good exam point

The case is frequently criticised for not awarding Tata a specific remedy — no compensation, and no definitive finding of executive accountability for the leak. The Government's own affidavit had conceded that retrieval was “not possible or practical”. The doctrinal gap — that there was no statutory mechanism for redress where intercepted material is leaked — is precisely what Puttaswamy's proportionality framework was later designed to close, and what the DPDP Act, 2023 now partly addresses. In a critical answer, say: the case recognised the wrong but supplied no remedy, and the gap it exposed was structural rather than doctrinal.

💡 Exam takeaways
  • Use this case to prove the point that the Telegraph Act matters in an IT Act answer. The Radia tapes were intercepted under s.5(2) of the Telegraph Act, not under s.69 IT Act. A complete interception answer covers both.
  • Learn the “two events” distinction. It is the single most quotable proposition from the case and it travels well into any question about leaks of intercepted or privileged material.
  • Learn the three framed issues — privacy against the State, privacy against the Press, the right to know — because they are the cleanest way to structure an essay on the “interface with freedom of speech and privacy”.
  • Note the PUCL link. The whole procedural framework applied here was judicially created in PUCL and codified in Rule 419A and, later, the 2009 Interception Rules.
  • Do not overstate the holding. The petition was not decided on the merits in a conventional sense; the CBI found no criminality; and the Union of India submitted that nothing survived after Puttaswamy.

8Case brief 3 — Sreekanth C. Nair v. Developer of Web‑Site

Case brief 3 · blocking before the rules matured — the procedural limit on courts

Sreekanth C. Nair v. Licensee / Developer of the Website www.incometaxpune.com

High Court of Kerala at Ernakulam Crl. R.P. No. 2900 of 2008 Decided 28 August 2008 V. Ramkumar J. Available at indiankanoon.org/doc/1915848/
📌 Provenance and significance

The judgment is not printed in the DU case material; the syllabus gives the citation and the Indian Kanoon link. It is a very short revision petition — but it is the earliest of the prescribed Unit VI cases, and it is the one that shows what happened before the 2009 Blocking Rules were framed: blocking was governed by an executive order of 2003, and courts had to improvise. Read it as a historical‑development citation showing the evolution from ad hoc judicial blocking orders to the codified s.69A / 2009 Rules framework.

01 Facts

The revision petitioner, Sreekanth C. Nair, was the applicant in Crl. M.P. No. 4378 of 2008 before the Judicial First Class Magistrate‑I, Cherthala. He was researching Income Tax statutes and visited a website described as helpful for taxpayers — www.incometaxpune.com. On visiting the site he was redirected to a pornographic website which depicted numerous lascivious images of women, thereby clearly violating s.67 of the Information Technology Act, 2000.

The petitioner relied on the order dated 07.07.2003 issued by the Ministry of Communication and Information Technology, Government of India, which provided the procedure for blocking websites by submitting a complaint to the Director, CERT‑In (the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team functioning under the Department of Information Technology). As per clause (viii) of paragraph V(1) of that order, a court was given the power to issue a direction to the Director, CERT‑In for blocking any such pornographic websites. The petitioner therefore prayed that the court send a complaint to CERT‑In, directing it to investigate and block the website in the public interest.

02 Issues

  1. Whether the application filed by the petitioner satisfied the description of a “complaint” under s.2(d) of the Code of Criminal Procedure.
  2. Whether the Magistrate had jurisdiction to entertain such a complaint and issue directives under the Government Order of 07.07.2003.
  3. Whether the petitioner could invoke the court's powers to direct the Director, CERT‑In to block a pornographic website in the public interest.

03 The Magistrate's order under challenge

The learned Magistrate rejected the petition styled as a complaint, holding, inter alia:

“…that the petition filed by the petitioner does not satisfy the description of a ‘complaint’ as defined under Section 2(d) Cr. P.C and that even as per the Government Order produced by the petitioner, the authority who can entertain the complaint is the Director, CERT‑In and that merely because CERT‑In can take appropriate action on a complaint preferred before its Director on the basis of a court directive, the same is not an indication of investiture of jurisdiction on a Magistrate to receive such complaints and issue directives as per the said Government Order.”

The Magistrate further observed that “it would not be possible for the Magistrate to usurp the jurisdiction of the statutory functionary.”

04 Arguments

Revision petitioner (through Adv. D. Prem Kamath)
  • The website in question was an unmistakable pornographic website which had to be blocked in the public interest.
  • Whoever was responsible for hosting the website was guilty of an offence punishable under s.67 IT Act, which penalises publishing or transmitting in electronic form any material which is lascivious or appeals to the prurient interest, or which tends to deprave and corrupt persons likely to read, see or hear it.
  • The petitioner, having come across the obscene website, desired to lodge a complaint before the Director, CERT‑In, and it was for that purpose that he moved the court — the court being one of the authorities listed under the Government Order who were entitled to submit a complaint to the Director. The court below was therefore not justified in rejecting his complaint.
Respondent / the court below
  • The petition did not satisfy the description of a “complaint” under s.2(d) CrPC — an allegation made to a Magistrate with a view to his taking action, not a prayer for an administrative direction.
  • The authority who could entertain the complaint was the Director, CERT‑In, not the Magistrate.
  • Clause (viii) of paragraph V(1) of the 2003 order merely listed the persons and authorities who may submit a complaint to the Director, CERT‑In. It conferred a power to issue a directive only where there was non‑compliance with a request to submit a complaint — it did not invest the Magistrate with jurisdiction to act as a complainant.

05 Reasoning and decision

Justice V. Ramkumar held:

“If the petitioner had come across the obscene website as mentioned by him, then whoever be the person who has hosted the said website, prima facie, commits an offence punishable under Section 67 of the Information Technology Act, 2000. But the petitioner does not know the person or persons responsible for the said website. Only a person who is so responsible can be convicted and punished under Section 67 of the said Act. That is not the function of the court. That is the function of the authorities under the I.T Act and the Rules and Orders issued thereunder. Even the person shown as the accused/respondent in Annexure‑A private complaint is the licencee/developer of the website ‘www.incometaxpune.com’. The petitioner cannot expect the court to find out the author of the said website and issue notice to him.”

And on the interpretive point: “None the less, the only intention which can be gathered is that the provision is to confer a power to issue a directive, in case there is non‑compliance or the failure to comply with the request to submit a complaint to the Director, CERT‑In. If an interpretation as suggested by the petitioner is adopted, then the court will be placed in the embarrassing position of a complainant before the Director, CERT‑In for initiating action for blocking a website.”

The Court further held that the court below could not have taken cognizance of the offence under s.67 IT Act on the petition filed by the petitioner, particularly when the request in that petition was to block the website in the public interest. Finding no grounds to interfere with the Magistrate's order, the Court dismissed the revision petition.

Principle evolved
  1. A court is not a complainant. Clause (viii) of paragraph V(1) of the 2003 Government Order listed the authorities who could submit a complaint to the Director, CERT‑In. It did not convert a court into a complainant or invest it with jurisdiction to act as one. Reading it otherwise would place the court “in the embarrassing position of a complainant”.
  2. Identification of the offender is not the court's function. Where the petitioner does not know who is responsible for the offending content, the court cannot be expected to find the author and issue notice. That is the function of the statutory authorities under the IT Act and the Rules and Orders made under it.
  3. The blocking power is administrative, not judicial. Blocking under the 2003 order — and, after 2009, under s.69A and the Blocking Rules — is an executive function exercised through the Designated Officer and the Committee. The court's role is limited to issuing a directive where the statutory route is not complied with, and to entertaining a challenge under Article 226 or 227.
  4. Securing access to or knowledge of the content is not the same as being liable for it. A person who is redirected to obscene content — who neither published, transmitted nor caused it to be published — does not thereby commit an offence under s.67. The offence is that of the person who publishes or transmits.
💡 Exam takeaways
  • Cite it as the pre‑2009 baseline. Before the 2009 Blocking Rules, blocking was governed by an executive order of 7 July 2003 administered through CERT‑In. This case shows the improvised, non‑statutory character of that regime — a valuable contrast with the codified, judicially validated s.69A procedure.
  • Learn the “court is not a complainant” proposition. It is short, memorable, and directly answers any question about the respective roles of courts and of the Designated Officer in blocking.
  • It also makes the s.67 point. Merely encountering or being redirected to obscene content does not make the viewer an offender under s.67 — a useful corrective in any obscenity problem question.
  • Pair it with Shreya Singhal. In Shreya Singhal the Supreme Court upheld the s.69A scheme precisely because it vested the blocking decision in a reasoned, safeguarded executive process with judicial review available afterwards — the very allocation of functions the Kerala High Court had identified a year earlier.
  • Note the 2026 development: the successor regime now routes complaints through the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal and through the Grievance Officer mechanism under Rule 3(2) of the 2021 Intermediary Guidelines, with CERT‑In remaining the technical nodal point.

9Case brief 4 — Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India

Case brief 4 · the case that supplies this Unit's master test

Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) and Anr. v. Union of India and Ors.

Supreme Court of India Writ Petition (Civil) No. 494 of 2012 (reference) Decided 24 August 2017 (2017) 10 SCC 1; 2017 SCC OnLine SC 996 Nine‑Judge Constitution Bench
📌 Provenance

The judgment is not printed in the DU case material; the syllabus cites it by citation, and the DU case material's own self‑learning exercise for this Unit gives the hint: “State does not have absolute power to restrict fundamental freedoms in cyberspace. Does it apply to information privacy also? Is the Right to information Privacy lies only against the State or does it extend against private sector too? (Hint — Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India)”. That exercise is answered in full in the PYQ bank at §12.

01 Citation and the Bench

Decided by a nine‑Judge Constitution Bench on 24 August 2017: Chief Justice J.S. Khehar and Justices J. Chelameswar, S.A. Bobde, R.K. Agrawal, R.F. Nariman, A.M. Sapre, D.Y. Chandrachud, S.K. Kaul and S. Abdul Nazeer. The Court delivered six separate opinions — the longest Constitution Bench output in recent Supreme Court history, running to nearly 550 pages — but was unanimous on the central holding. The original petitioner, Justice K.S. Puttaswamy, was a former judge of the Karnataka High Court.

02 Facts and the reference

The reference arose from challenges to the Aadhaar scheme's mandatory collection of biometric data. The threshold question was referred to a larger Bench: does the Indian Constitution guarantee a fundamental right to privacy at all?

The reference was necessary because of two earlier decisions of co‑equal Benches:

CaseBenchWhat it saidWhat Puttaswamy did with it
M.P. Sharma v. Satish ChandraEight‑Judge Bench, 1954Held that the Constitution did not specifically guarantee a right to privacy, and that the Indian Constitution did not recognise the American Fourth Amendment doctrine.Overruled to the extent it held that there is no fundamental right to privacy.
Kharak Singh v. State of U.P.Six‑Judge Bench, 1962Held that there was no right to privacy under the Indian Constitution, while striking down domiciliary visits as violative of personal liberty.Overruled to the extent it held that the right to privacy is not guaranteed by Part III.

03 Issues

  1. Whether the right to privacy is a fundamental right under Part III of the Constitution, and if so, under which Article(s), and subject to what limitations.
  2. Whether the rulings in M.P. Sharma and Kharak Singh, to the extent they held that privacy is not a fundamental right, were incorrect.
  3. What is the test by which any state action infringing privacy is to be judged.

04 Arguments

Petitioners
  • The right to privacy is an inherent right that flows from Articles 14, 19 and 21, even though it is not expressly enumerated.
  • Reliance on Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India and the line of cases reading Article 21 expansively to include dignity, autonomy and the freedom to develop one's personality.
  • Reliance on international human rights instruments — Article 12 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (“No one shall be subjected to arbitrary interference with his privacy, family, home or correspondence, nor to attacks upon his honour and reputation”) and Article 17 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.
  • Informational privacy is an essential trait of privacy in the age of the internet; the State's collection of biometric data diminishes autonomy and dignity.
  • Privacy is a precondition to the meaningful exercise of the freedoms guaranteed by Part III — without privacy, freedom of speech and association become impossible.
Respondents (Union of India and States)
  • The right to privacy is not expressly provided for in the Constitution; reading it into Article 21 would be judicial overreach.
  • Article 21 had no application in either M.P. Sharma or Kharak Singh, and those decisions remain binding.
  • Privacy is a vague and elastic concept that cannot supply a judicially manageable standard.
  • The welfare State's programmes — ration cards, Aadhaar, tax administration — require the collection of personal data, and an absolute privacy right would make governance impossible.

05 Reasoning

(a) Privacy is intrinsic to Article 21. All nine Judges agreed that privacy is intrinsic to the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21, and also flows from the freedoms guaranteed by Part III generally — Article 19 and Article 14. The Court held that privacy embraces bodily integrity, personal autonomy, and informational self‑determination.

(b) Privacy is not absolute, but it can be regulated only by law. Privacy can be regulated by the State in accordance with law; the regulation must be a proportionate response founded on a legitimate aim and a compelling state interest. A constitutional right cannot be outweighed by the executive, nor by legislation, without satisfying the three tests.

(c) The three‑fold test. The Court adopted the legality – necessity – proportionality framework:

📜 The three‑fold test — quote it verbatim in every Unit VI answer
  1. LEGALITY — the existence of a law; the action must be authorised by valid law, not merely by executive fiat.
  2. NECESSITY, in terms of a legitimate State objective — the State must pursue a goal that justifies the infringement: national security, public order, and the like.
  3. PROPORTIONALITY — a rational nexus between the object and the means required to achieve it, and a requirement that the means adopted be the least restrictive alternative reasonably available.

(d) Informational privacy. The Court expressly recognised informational privacy as a facet of the right: the right of the individual to control the dissemination of information about herself. This is the doctrinal foundation of the later right to be forgotten jurisprudence and of the DPDP Act.

(e) Privacy against private actors. The Court noted that in the age of technology, privacy concerns can arise from both the State and non‑State entities. Although the immediate case concerned the State, the reasoning recognises that a claim of violation of privacy may lie against private bodies as well — the point the DU self‑learning exercise asks you to develop.

(f) Sexual orientation and s.377. The Court described s.377 IPC as a “discordant note which directly bears upon the evolution of the constitutional jurisprudence on the right to privacy”, holding that sexual orientation is an essential attribute of privacy and that discrimination based on it is deeply offensive to dignity and self‑worth.

Principle evolved — the holdings
  1. The right to privacy is a fundamental right. It is protected as an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21, and as part of the freedoms guaranteed by Part III of the Constitution — traceable also to Articles 14 and 19.
  2. M.P. Sharma and Kharak Singh are overruled to the extent they held otherwise.
  3. The three‑fold test governs every infringement: legality, legitimate State aim, and proportionality (rational nexus + least‑restrictive means).
  4. Privacy is not absolute. It is subject to restrictions that satisfy the three‑fold test; but the burden lies on the State to justify the intrusion.
  5. The Aadhaar‑specific challenges were remitted to a separate Bench — subsequently decided in K.S. Puttaswamy (Aadhaar) v. Union of India, (2019) 1 SCC 1.
💡 Exam takeaways
  • Cite the three‑fold test by name. Omitting it is the single most common lost‑marks error in Unit VI.
  • Name the two overruled cases. M.P. Sharma (1954, 8‑Judge) and Kharak Singh (1962, 6‑Judge).
  • Apply the test to each of the Unit VI powers. s.69: legality (s.69(1) + 2009 Rules) ✓; legitimate aim (sovereignty, security, public order, incitement, and — problematically — “investigation of any offence”) ✓/partly; proportionality (Rule 8's alternative‑means requirement helps, but there is no access control and no independent judicial authorisation) — contestable. s.69A: legality ✓ (s.69A + 2009 Rules); legitimate aim ✓ (Article 19(2) grounds); proportionality ✓ (48‑hour hearing, Review Committee) — upheld in Shreya Singhal. s.70: legality ✓; legitimate aim ✓ (critical infrastructure); proportionality ✓ (access is restricted, not surveilled).
  • Note the doctrinal bridge. Puttaswamy is the reason Shreya Singhal's reading‑down of s.79(3)(b) makes sense, the reason the Rule 4(2) traceability mandate is contested, and the reason the CERT‑In 2022 Directions' retention obligations are debated.

10Case brief 5 — Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India

Case brief 5 · internet shutdowns — the doctrine of proportionality applied to the whole network

Anuradha Bhasin and Ors. v. Union of India and Ors.

Supreme Court of India Writ Petition (Civil) Nos. 1031 of 2019, 1164 of 2019; W.P. (Crl.) No. 225 of 2019 Decided 10 January 2020 (2020) 3 SCC 637; 2020 SCC OnLine SC 25 N.V. Ramana, Subhash Reddy and B.R. Gavai JJ.

01 Citation and provenance

Not printed in the DU case material; cited in the syllabus by citation. It is the most recent of the prescribed Unit VI cases and the one that carries the Puttaswamy framework from content‑specific blocking (s.69A) to network‑wide suspension — a different statutory regime, with different actors, and different constitutional stakes.

02 Facts

Following the abrogation of Article 370's special status for Jammu and Kashmir in August 2019, restrictions were imposed across the region. Section 144 of the Code of Criminal Procedure was invoked to ban public gatherings and curtail freedom of movement, and there was a complete suspension of landline, mobile phone and internet services from 4 August 2019. The suspension was continued by periodic orders under the Temporary Suspension of Telecom Services (Public Emergency or Public Service) Rules, 2017, framed under s.7 of the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 — a parallel regime to s.69A IT Act, applicable to network‑wide shutdowns rather than specific‑content blocking.

Three petitions were heard together:

PetitionPetitionerGrievance
W.P. (C) No. 1031 of 2019Anuradha Bhasin, editor of the Kashmir TimesPrint media came to a halt; the shutdown breached freedom of speech and expression and should be tested on the basis of necessity and proportionality. The Suspension Rules were of a temporary nature but the shutdown had become indefinite. The official orders must not be kept secret by the State, and the State must prove the necessity of the restrictions.
W.P. (C) No. 1164 of 2019Ghulam Nabi Azad, Member of ParliamentRestrictions must be based on objective reasons and not merely on conjectures; the State must choose the least restrictive measures; the internet shutdown violated not only free speech but also the right to trade and the ability of political representatives to communicate with their constituents.
W.P. (Crl.) No. 225 of 2019IntervenorsThe State failed to prove the necessity of the restrictions. “The people have a right to speak their view, whether good, bad or ugly, and the State must prove that it was necessary to restrict the same.” The validity of the restrictions should be tested on the proportionality test, considering not only the physical and legal restrictions but also the fear that such restrictions engender in the minds of the populace.

03 Issues

  1. Whether the freedom of speech and expression and the freedom to practise any profession or to carry on any occupation, trade or business over the internet are part of the fundamental rights under Part III of the Constitution.
  2. Whether a complete and indefinite prohibition of internet access is justified, and whether the restrictions were the least intrusive available.
  3. Whether the Government could claim privilege for not producing the orders passed under s.144 CrPC and the Suspension Rules.
  4. Whether the imposition of restrictions under s.144 CrPC was valid.

04 Arguments

Petitioners
  • The suspension of the internet was not in accordance with the Telecom Services (Public Emergency or Public Service) Rules, 2017.
  • The restrictions were not proportionate to the objective of safeguarding “public order”, and no imminent danger justified them. “Public order” is different from “law and order”, and the restrictions were imposed out of a danger to law and order.
  • The indefinite nature of the shutdown failed the four‑pronged test of proportionality: legitimate objective; rational nexus; absence of a less restrictive alternative; and non‑disproportionate impact.
  • The State must prove necessity and must publish its orders; it cannot claim blanket confidentiality.
  • Restrictions on internet access affected Article 19(1)(g) as well — the right to trade and the ability of representatives to communicate with constituents.
Respondents (Union of India)
  • The internet was never restricted in the Jammu and Ladakh regions.
  • Social media allowed people to send messages to a number of people at the same time and could be used to incite violence; the internet allowed the transmission of false news or fake images, used to spread violence; and the “dark web” allowed individuals to purchase weapons and illegal substances easily.
  • There was no technology to selectively block internet services, so a complete shutdown was the only option available.
  • Just a few select websites could not be targeted; the claims made on the stringency of the restrictions were grossly exaggerated.
  • The Suspension Rules were being reviewed strictly and used reasonably.

05 Reasoning

(a) Internet access and the fundamental rights. The Court held that the freedom of speech and expression, and the freedom to practise any profession or carry on any trade, business or occupation, through the medium of the internet, enjoy constitutional protection under Article 19(1)(a) and Article 19(1)(g). Any restriction must be in consonance with the mandate under Articles 19(2) and 19(6), inclusive of the test of proportionality. Quoting its own earlier reasoning: “Expression through the internet has gained contemporary relevance and is one of the major means of information diffusion. Therefore, the freedom of speech and expression through the medium of the internet is an integral part of Article 19(1)(a) and accordingly, any restriction on the same must be in accordance with Article 19(2) of the Constitution.”

(b) The doctrine of proportionality. The Court held that the doctrine is etched into the Indian Constitution by the use of the word “reasonable” in Article 19. It conducted an extensive comparative review of the German test (necessity / no equal alternative) and the Canadian Oakes test (minimal impairment), and observed that while all agree proportionality is the key tool, there is no agreement that proportionality and balancing are equivalent. It adopted Lord Diplock's aphorism — “you must not use a steam hammer to crack a nut, if a nutcracker would do” (R v. Goldsmith) — and set out the four requirements:

📜 The four‑pronged proportionality test
  1. The possible goal of the measure must be legitimate.
  2. The measures must be suitable for furthering that legitimate goal.
  3. It must be determined whether there is an equally effective but less restrictive alternative available.
  4. It must be determined whether the measures disproportionately affect the rights of the subjects.

And, on the dimension of the restriction: “The degree of restriction and the scope of the same, both territorially and temporally, must stand in relation to what is actually necessary to combat an emergent situation… The concept of proportionality requires a restriction to be tailored in accordance with the territorial extent of the restriction, the stage of emergency, nature of urgency, duration of such restrictive measure and nature of such restriction.”

(c) The legality of the shutdown. The Court outright rejected the State's justification for a total ban on the internet on the ground that it lacked the technology to selectively block — to accept that logic would give the State a green light to ban internet access completely every time. Since s.5(2) of the Telegraph Act permits suspension orders only in a situation of public emergency or in the interest of public safety, the Government first had to determine that a public emergency existed. And: “complete broad suspension of telecom services, be it the Internet or otherwise, being a drastic measure, must be considered by the State only if ‘necessary’ and ‘unavoidable’. In furtherance of the same, the State must assess the existence of an alternate less intrusive remedy.”

(d) Indefinite suspension. The Court noted that the Suspension Rules do not prescribe a maximum duration. Applying the principle of proportionality, it held that indefinite suspension is impermissible; since the Rules were silent, it fell to the Review Committee to determine duration and ensure the suspension did not extend beyond what was necessary. It further held that “orders passed mechanically or in a cryptic manner cannot be said to be orders passed in accordance with law”.

(e) Publication and judicial review. The Court held that the State could not claim exemption from producing the orders, because publication would facilitate the right of aggrieved persons to challenge their constitutionality. Although the Suspension Rules provide no appellate mechanism, administrative orders are challengeable before a High Court under Article 226.

(f) Section 144 CrPC. The Court held that s.144 may be exercised when there is present danger as well as apprehension of danger, but that it cannot be used to suppress legitimate opinions, grievances, or the exercise of any democratic rights; that an order must state material facts to enable judicial review; that the Magistrate is duty bound to apply the principle of proportionality and to balance the rights; and that repetitive orders would be an abuse of power.

Decision and principle evolved
  1. Freedom of expression and freedom to practise any profession over the internet are constitutionally protected under Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g); restrictions must satisfy Articles 19(2) and 19(6), including the test of proportionality.
  2. An order suspending internet services indefinitely is impermissible under the Temporary Suspension of Telecom Services (Public Emergency or Public Service) Rules, 2017. Suspension may be used for a temporary duration only.
  3. Any suspension order must adhere to proportionality and must not extend beyond the necessary duration; it is subject to judicial review.
  4. The Review Committee must conduct a periodic review every seven working days; all existing suspension orders must be reviewed and those not complying revoked.
  5. All orders under s.144 CrPC and the Suspension Rules must be published — the State cannot claim blanket confidentiality for orders restricting fundamental rights.
  6. Lack of technology to block selectively is not a justification for a total shutdown.
  7. Section 144 must be exercised on material facts, with proportionality applied; repetitive orders are an abuse of power.
⚠️ The mixed outcome — say so

The Court did not immediately restore internet access. It established the framework and directed the Government to review the suspension orders against it. The judgment has therefore been described both as “a ray of hope” for digital civil rights and as a missed opportunity because it produced no immediate relief. In an exam, note the mixed outcome — it demonstrates that you have read the operative part and not just the headlines.

💡 Exam takeaways
  • This is the case that extends Puttaswamy from content to network. Shreya Singhal validated the blocking of specific content; Anuradha Bhasin governs the suspension of the whole network. Keep the two statutory regimes distinct in your answer — s.69A IT Act on the one hand, the Telegraph Act and the 2017 Suspension Rules on the other.
  • Learn the four‑pronged test and the Diplock aphorism. One sentence each, high recall.
  • Learn the two “firsts”: the Court held that the “lack of technology” argument cannot justify a blanket shutdown, and that orders must be published.
  • Note the doctrinal gap that remains: whether Anuradha Bhasin's publication mandate extends to individual s.69A content‑blocking orders (as opposed to area‑wide suspensions) is unresolved. Flag it; do not resolve it.
  • Use it in any question about internet shutdowns — including the frequent problem questions on shutdowns ordered for examinations, elections or communal tension.

11.2 Critical analysis — the five live debates

⚖️ Debate 1 — Executive authorisation versus judicial authorisation

Under s.69, the “competent authority” is the Union or State Home Secretary — a member of the executive. Oversight is provided by a Review Committee headed by the Cabinet Secretary (and, in a State, by the Chief Secretary), meeting at least once in two months. The criticism is structural: the executive is authorising and reviewing its own action. Compare the UK's Investigatory Powers Commissioner and the American FISA Court — both introduce an element of independence. The counter‑argument is that ex parte judicial authorisation is incompatible with covert surveillance, since notice to the target would defeat the operation, and that retrospective review by a Committee with the power to set aside directions and order destruction of the material is a real, if imperfect, check. The honest exam answer says the Indian model relies on internal executive review and that this is its weakest feature.

⚖️ Debate 2 — The transparency deficit in the blocking regime

Shreya Singhal upheld s.69A partly because reasons must be recorded in writing so that the order may be assailed under Article 226. But Rule 16 imposes strict confidentiality on requests, complaints and actions taken, and in practice blocking orders are frequently not published; the confidentiality provision has been invoked to resist RTI disclosure of blocking grounds; and affected originators and intermediaries report inconsistent notice‑and‑hearing compliance. Anuradha Bhasin's insistence that suspension orders be published is a corrective gloss — but whether it extends to individual s.69A blocking orders is unresolved. The examination point is that the safeguard the Supreme Court relied on — writ review — is worthless if the aggrieved person cannot learn that an order exists or what its reasons were.

⚖️ Debate 3 — Differential judicial scrutiny: interception has escaped the proportionality test

s.69A received express judicial validation in Shreya Singhal. s.69 — interception — has never been the subject of an equivalently comprehensive Supreme Court constitutional review post‑Puttaswamy. The Telegraph Act analogue was scrutinised in PUCL (1997), but under a pre‑Puttaswamy framework. This is a live gap, because interception is arguably the more invasive power: covert access to content, as against overt denial of access. And s.69 contains the broadest ground of all — “for investigation of any offence” — which is not an Article 19(2) subject matter. On the Puttaswamy test, the legitimacy limb is therefore questionable for ordinary criminal investigation. This is the strongest single critical point available in the Unit.

⚖️ Debate 4 — CERT‑In's expanding mandate and the metadata problem

s.70B empowers CERT‑In to call for information and issue binding directions. The 2022 Directions — six‑hour incident reporting, 180‑day in‑India log retention, five‑year VPN/cloud subscriber retention — are among the world's most demanding. Scholars flag the tension between CERT‑In's technocratic cyber‑security mandate and its overlap with privacy‑implicating data retention. The counter‑argument is that s.70B(6) is an express statutory authorisation, so the legality limb is satisfied, and that rapid reporting is a proportionate response to escalating attacks. The EU's Digital Rights Ireland and Tele2 decisions supply the contrary comparative authority; the Indian courts have not ruled.

⚖️ Debate 5 — The right to be forgotten gap

Unlike the EU's Google Spain‑derived, GDPR‑codified right, India's approach remains a patchwork of inconsistent High Court decisions, with no Supreme Court ruling squarely addressing the doctrine's contours — the balance against open‑justice and public‑record principles, and the applicability to search‑engine de‑listing as against primary‑source removal. The DPDP Act's narrower “erasure” right, tied to consent withdrawal and purpose exhaustion, does not fully substitute for a EU‑style forgetting right rooted in the passage of time and diminished public interest. The asymmetry — robust surveillance proportionality, underdeveloped forgetting jurisprudence — is a strong closing line for any essay.

Nov–Dec 2024Q.6(b) · 10–12 marks"Discuss provisions relating to cybersecurity under the IT Act with the help of decided cases."
Model answer

Thesis. The IT Act's cyber‑security architecture rests on four distinct executive powers — interception (s.69), blocking (s.69A), traffic‑data collection (s.69B) and protected‑system designation (s.70, with the institutional machinery of ss.70A and 70B) — each procedurally fenced by the 2009 Rules and each governed by the legality → legitimate aim → proportionality test laid down in K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1.

(1) Interception — s.69 and the 2009 Interception Rules. The Central or State Government, or a specially authorised officer, satisfied of necessity or expediency in the enumerated interests — sovereignty and integrity of India, defence, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, preventing incitement to a cognizable offence, and investigation of any offence — may by reasoned written order direct any agency of the appropriate Government to intercept, monitor or decrypt information. The competent authority is the Union or State Home Secretary; Rule 8 requires consideration of alternative means; Rule 11 limits a direction to 60 days, renewable to a maximum of 180 days; Rule 22 requires a Review Committee to meet at least every two months with power to set aside directions. Subscribers and intermediaries must extend technical assistance on pain of seven years' imprisonment. The parallel power under s.5(2) of the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 and Rule 419A of the Telegraph Rules remains operative and shares the same authority and Review Committee.

(2) Blocking — s.69A and the 2009 Blocking Rules. The Central Government may, for reasons recorded in writing and on grounds confined to the Article 19(2) subjects, direct any agency of the Government or an intermediary to block public access to information. The procedure runs: complaint → Nodal Officer → Designated Officer (not below Joint Secretary) → Committee of Government personnel → Rule 8 hearing to the originator and intermediary with not less than 48 hours' notice → Secretary, DoIT approval → blocking order. Emergency blocking is permitted under Rule 9 without hearing, but the Committee must consider it within 48 hours. Rule 14 provides for a Review Committee every two months; Rule 16 for confidentiality.

(3) Traffic data — s.69B. The Central Government may, by notification, authorise an agency to monitor and collect traffic data (metadata, not content) to enhance cyber security and prevent intrusion or the spread of computer contaminant. Non‑compliance carries seven years' imprisonment.

(4) Protected systems and the institutions — ss.70, 70A, 70B. The appropriate Government may declare any computer resource affecting Critical Information Infrastructure a protected system; unauthorised access or attempt attracts up to ten years' imprisonment. s.70A creates the NCIIPC as National Nodal Agency; s.70B makes CERT‑In the national incident‑response agency, empowered under its Directions of 28 April 2022 to require six‑hour incident reporting, 180‑day in‑India log retention and five‑year subscriber retention by VPS, cloud, data‑centre and VPN providers, with non‑compliance punishable by up to one year.

(5) The decided cases. Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, (2015) 5 SCC 1 — s.69A and the 2009 Blocking Rules upheld as “a narrowly drawn provision with several safeguards”, while s.66A was struck down in the same judgment for vagueness and overbreadth. People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India, (1997) 1 SCC 301 — read procedural safeguards into the Telegraph Act power: “telephone‑tapping would, thus, infract Article 21… unless it is permitted under the procedure established by law.” Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1 — privacy is a fundamental right under Article 21; every intrusion must satisfy legality, legitimate aim and proportionality. Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India, (2020) 3 SCC 637 — internet access is protected by Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g); indefinite suspension is impermissible; periodic review and publication of orders are mandatory. Ratan Tata v. Union of India, W.P.(C) 98/2010 — distinguished the legality of interception from the unlawfulness of the subsequent leak of intercepted material. Sreekanth C. Nair v. Developer of Web‑Site, Crl. R.P. 2900/2008 (Ker) — a court is not a complainant before CERT‑In; blocking is an executive function.

Conclusion. The IT Act's cyber‑security powers are broad but not unbounded. The safeguards are real — reasons in writing, a hearing to the originator, independent review, temporal limits — and the Supreme Court has twice intervened to insist on them. What remains structurally incomplete is the extension of the proportionality framework to interception, which has never been comprehensively reviewed post‑Puttaswamy despite being the more invasive power, and the absence of a codified right to be forgotten to match India's now‑robust surveillance‑proportionality jurisprudence.
Essay10–15 marksCritically analyse the doctrine of proportionality as developed in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India and its application to state surveillance powers under the IT Act.
Model answer

Thesis. Puttaswamy supplied the master test; the Unit VI powers are its first and hardest application; and the application is incomplete — s.69A has been tested and upheld, while s.69 and the CERT‑In retention mandate have not.

(1) The doctrine. A nine‑Judge Constitution Bench unanimously held that the right to privacy is a fundamental right intrinsic to Article 21 and traceable to Articles 14 and 19, overruling M.P. Sharma v. Satish Chandra (1954) and Kharak Singh v. State of U.P. (1962) to the contrary. It adopted a three‑fold test — legality (a valid law), legitimate State aim (necessity in a democratic society), and proportionality (rational nexus plus least‑restrictive alternative) — later refined into four prongs by Anuradha Bhasin: legitimate goal; suitability; absence of an equally effective but less restrictive alternative; and non‑disproportionate effect. The Court also insisted on the territorial and temporal dimension: the restriction must be tailored to the extent of the emergency, the nature of the urgency, and the duration necessary.

(2) Applied to s.69A blocking — the successful application. Legality: s.69A is an enacted provision and the 2009 Rules are valid subordinate legislation. Legitimate aim: the grounds are a near‑verbatim recitation of the Article 19(2) subjects — sovereignty and integrity, defence, security of the State, friendly relations, public order, incitement to a cognizable offence. Proportionality: the Rules supply a hearing to the originator and the intermediary with at least 48 hours' notice; a reasoned written order assailable under Article 226; and a Review Committee that may unblock. The Supreme Court accordingly held s.69A to be “a narrowly drawn provision with several safeguards”. Contrast s.66A, struck down in the same judgment: its grounds lay wholly outside Article 19(2), and it was vague, overbroad and chilling.

(3) Applied to network shutdown — the corrective application. Anuradha Bhasin applied the same test to the Temporary Suspension of Telecom Services Rules, 2017 and s.144 CrPC. It held that internet access is instrumental to Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g); that “you must not use a steam hammer to crack a nut, if a nutcracker would do”; that lack of technology to block selectively is not a justification for a total shutdown; that indefinite suspension is impermissible; that the Review Committee must review every seven working days; and that orders must be published. Orders “passed mechanically or in a cryptic manner cannot be said to be orders passed in accordance with law”.

(4) The incomplete applications — where the critique lies. (a) s.69 interception has never been comprehensively reviewed post‑Puttaswamy, despite being the more invasive power. Its legitimate aim limb is itself questionable, because s.69(1) permits interception “for investigation of any offence” — a ground that is not an Article 19(2) subject matter. (b) Authorisation is executive: the competent authority is the Home Secretary and the Review Committee is headed by the Cabinet Secretary — the executive authorises and reviews its own action, with no judicial authorisation. (c) The CERT‑In 2022 Directions impose indiscriminate 180‑day log retention and five‑year VPN subscriber retention — precisely the vice identified in the CJEU's Digital Rights Ireland and Tele2 Sverige/Watson decisions, and not yet tested in India. (d) Rule 16's confidentiality undermines the very writ‑review safeguard on which Shreya Singhal's validation of s.69A rested.

Conclusion. Puttaswamy's proportionality doctrine is now the governing framework for every exercise of the ss.69, 69A and 70 powers, and it has already produced concrete results — the validation of a procedurally safeguarded blocking regime, and the prohibition of indefinite internet shutdowns. But its application is uneven: content blocking and network suspension have been judicially tested, while interception, indiscriminate data retention and the practical operation of the blocking rules have not. The doctrine is established; its enforcement is incomplete.
Essay10–15 marks"Discuss the right to be forgotten in Indian law — is there a codified right, and how do courts currently handle such claims?"
Model answer

Thesis. There is no codified right to be forgotten in India. What exists is the narrower erasure right in the DPDP Act, 2023, and a fragmented body of High Court decisions recognising a limited right to delinking, reasoned from the Puttaswamy privacy right and the European Google Spain doctrine.

(1) The European source. In Google Spain SL v. AEPD, Case C‑131/12 (CJEU, 2014), the Court of Justice held that a data subject may require a search engine to de‑list results for a search on his name where the information is inadequate, irrelevant, no longer relevant or excessive in relation to the purposes of processing and the time elapsed. The right was later codified in Article 17 of the GDPR. It is important to be precise about its character: it is a de‑listing right against search engines, not a right to erase the underlying article, and it is balanced against the public interest in access to information.

(2) The Indian statutory position. There is no provision in the IT Act conferring a right to be forgotten. The closest analogue is the right to erasure in s.12 of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, operationalised by the DPDP Rules, 2025: a Data Principal may require erasure of personal data that is no longer necessary for the specified purpose, or where consent has been withdrawn, subject to legal retention requirements. This is narrower than the European right — it is consent‑based and purpose‑based, not time‑based. A person whose ten‑year‑old acquittal report remains online cannot demand its erasure under s.12 merely because time has passed and public interest has waned.

(3) The Indian judicial approach. High Courts — in Delhi, Karnataka, Kerala and Gujarat — have recognised a limited right to have outdated or irrelevant personal information delinked from search results or removed from court‑judgment repositories, reasoning from the Puttaswamy privacy right and, expressly, from Google Spain. The claims that have succeeded typically concern acquittals, matrimonial disputes and sensitive personal matters. But the case law is fragmented and non‑uniform: there are no settled criteria for how much time must pass, how public interest is to be assessed, or whether relief runs against the search engine or the primary publisher. There is no Supreme Court authority definitively settling the doctrine's scope.

(4) The three unresolved tensions. (a) De‑listing versus primary‑source removal — the European right runs against search engines; Indian claimants often seek removal of the underlying article or judgment, which raises very different free‑expression and open‑justice questions. (b) Privacy versus open justice — court judgments are public documents and judicial records resist erasure; the Supreme Court has consistently resisted the redaction or removal of judgments. (c) Who decides — in Europe the data controller decides, subject to regulator oversight; India has no equivalent adjudicatory mechanism for a forgetting claim.

Conclusion. India has no codified right to be forgotten. It has a narrower statutory erasure right in the DPDP Act, and an inconsistent body of High Court decisions recognising a limited delisting right. The result is an asymmetry worth stating explicitly in an exam: India's surveillance‑proportionality jurisprudence is now well developed after Puttaswamy and Anuradha Bhasin, while its digital‑forgetting jurisprudence remains fragmented and uncodified — leaving a significant gap between India's protection of the individual against the State's eyes and its protection of the individual against the permanence of the record.
DU self‑learning exercisePrinted in the DU case material, p.179–180"The Intermediaries are required to take down or block content upon notification — the court has clarified that such takedown will only be upon receipt of an order from a government agency or a court and not at the discretion of the intermediary or on receipt of request by an affected person. Don't you think that the Court has diluted the due diligence obligation of the Intermediaries in India, to a great extent, as now Intermediaries instead of being proactive in monitoring obligations pertaining to the offensive matter on their websites, will wait for a government notification or court order to remove them from their site?"
Model answer

Understanding the question. The exercise asks whether the Shreya Singhal reading‑down of s.79(3)(b) and Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules — confining “actual knowledge” to a court order or a Government notification — has diluted the due diligence obligation, and whether the result is that intermediaries will now simply wait for a court order instead of proactively monitoring.

(1) The reading‑down and its rationale. In Shreya Singhal the Supreme Court held that s.79 is an exemption provision closely related to the offence provisions; that under s.69A blocking can occur only by a reasoned order after safeguards including a hearing; and that “the intermediary applying its own mind… is noticeably absent” from that scheme. Practically, it would be “very difficult for intermediaries like Google, Facebook etc. to act when millions of requests are made and the intermediary is then to judge as to which of such requests are legitimate and which are not.” The Court therefore read s.79(3)(b) down to require a court order or a notification by the appropriate Government or its agency, and read Rule 3(4) down correspondingly.

(2) Why it is not a “dilution” but a reallocation. (a) Constitutional necessity. If a private complaint could trigger the duty to remove, intermediaries would become unaccountable private censors adjudicating contested claims — defamation, trademark infringement, obscenity — with none of the procedural safeguards that s.69A and the 2009 Blocking Rules require. The reading‑down does not remove the duty to remove; it transfers the trigger from a private demand to an authoritative determination. (b) The s.69A architecture already contemplated this. Rule 6 of the 2009 Blocking Rules permits any person to complain to a Nodal Officer, which transmits it to the Designated Officer for Committee examination. The route from private complaint to removal therefore exists — it simply runs through the Government, not through the platform. (c) The due diligence obligation has not shrunk; it has changed shape. An intermediary must still publish rules, privacy policy and user agreement; inform users of the prohibited‑content categories; appoint a Grievance Officer; and act within the prescribed clock once a court order or Government notification arrives.

(3) The real cost, and how the Rules answer it. The legitimate concern is delay: a victim of revenge pornography, harassment or defamation must obtain a court order before the intermediary is legally obliged to act. The 2021 Rules answer this not by reopening the constitutional question but by compressing the compliance clock: the Grievance Officer must acknowledge within 24 hours and resolve within 7 days (reduced from 15 days in February 2026); removal on a court order or Government notification must occur within 36 hours under the 2021 Rules and 3 hours under the 2026 Amendment Rules; and action on non‑consensual intimate imagery is required within 2 hours. The 2026 Amendment Rules go further still, imposing automated‑detection obligations for specified categories and mandatory labelling and provenance metadata for synthetically generated information. So the executive has in fact expanded proactive‑monitoring expectations — the reading‑down did not end them.

(4) Where the genuine tension lies. (a) The My Space “specific knowledge” standard — that knowledge must be specific to particular infringing works — sits uneasily with Rule 3(3)'s automated‑detection mandate. (b) Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish (2024) holds that possession of child sexual abuse material is independently punishable under s.15 POCSO and s.67B, which supports platform‑level detection duties for the most serious categories on a different logic. (c) But the requirement that a platform remove content on a private complaint remains constitutionally untenable, because the same objection — that the platform cannot assess legitimacy — applies with undiminished force.

Conclusion. The reading‑down in Shreya Singhal did not dilute due diligence; it reallocated the trigger for removal from private demand to authoritative determination, in order to prevent intermediaries from becoming private censors. The duty to be proactive has not disappeared — it has been re‑expressed through the 2021 Rules as amended in 2022, 2023, 2025 and 2026, in the form of a Grievance Officer, compressed takedown clocks, automated detection for specified categories, and labelling obligations for synthetic content. The enduring question is how far that executive push toward proactive monitoring can go before it collides with the specific‑knowledge principle that both Shreya Singhal and My Space were at pains to protect.
DU self‑learning exercisePrinted in the DU case material, p.180"State does not have absolute power to restrict fundamental freedoms in cyberspace. Does it apply to information privacy also? Is the Right to information Privacy lies only against the State or does it extend against private sector too? (Hint — Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India)"
Model answer

(1) The State's power is not absolute — the framework. No fundamental right is absolute, and the freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a) is expressly “subject to” reasonable restrictions under Article 19(2). But the restriction must satisfy the Puttaswamy three‑fold test: legality (a valid law), legitimate aim (a constitutional ground), and proportionality (rational nexus and least‑restrictive means). In cyberspace specifically: s.66A was struck down in Shreya Singhal because it was vague, overbroad, unconfined to any Article 19(2) ground and chilling; s.69A was upheld because its grounds are confined to the Article 19(2) subjects, reasons must be recorded in writing, and the 2009 Rules supply a hearing and a Review Committee; and indefinite internet shutdowns were held impermissible in Anuradha Bhasin because they failed the proportionality and periodic‑review requirements. So the State's power in cyberspace is conditional, reasoned and reviewable — not absolute.

(2) Does the same framework apply to informational privacy? Yes — and more strongly. In Puttaswamy the nine‑Judge Bench held that privacy is a fundamental right intrinsic to Article 21 and traceable to Articles 14 and 19, and that it embraces informational privacy — the right of the individual to control the dissemination of information about herself. The Court expressly noted that privacy concerns in the age of technology can arise from both the State and non‑State entities. Every informational‑privacy intrusion must therefore satisfy the same three‑fold test: legality, legitimate aim, and proportionality. The Court was emphatic that a constitutional right cannot be outweighed by the executive, nor by legislation, without those three tests being met.

(3) Is the right available only against the State, or also against private parties? Both. Three points: (a) Puttaswamy itself recognised that privacy concerns arise from the State and from non‑State entities, so a claim of violation may lie against private bodies. (b) Part III fundamental rights generally operate against the State, but the DPDP Act, 2023 now imposes direct obligations on private Data Fiduciaries — consent requirements, purpose limitation, security safeguards, breach notification, data‑principal rights including access, correction and erasure, and grievance redressal. That is a statutory right available against private parties, enforceable through the Data Protection Board of India. (c) Independently, s.72A of the IT Act punishes any person, including an intermediary, who discloses personal information in breach of a lawful contract — up to three years' imprisonment and a fine of up to ₹5 lakh. And s.43A imposes civil liability on a body corporate for negligent handling of sensitive personal data. So the practical answer is that informational privacy in India is protected against the State by Part III read with Puttaswamy, and against private parties by statute — the IT Act, the DPDP Act, and the common law of confidence.

(4) The limit. The right is not absolute. The State may intrude for a legitimate aim satisfying the proportionality test — national security, public order, investigation. Private parties may process personal data where they have consent or a legitimate use. And the right must be balanced against competing rights: the freedom of the press under Article 19(1)(a), the public's right to know, and open‑justice principles. The tension between Puttaswamy privacy and the RTI Act's disclosure mandate under s.8(1)(j) is resolved case by case, applying the same three‑fold test to each disclosure.

Conclusion. The State's power to restrict fundamental freedoms in cyberspace is not absolute; it is conditioned by the Puttaswamy legality–legitimate‑aim–proportionality test, applied in cyberspace by Shreya Singhal (s.66A struck down, s.69A upheld) and by Anuradha Bhasin (indefinite shutdowns impermissible). That framework applies with particular force to informational privacy, which Puttaswamy recognised as a facet of the Article 21 right. And the right to informational privacy lies both against the State and against private parties — against the State through Part III, and against private parties through the DPDP Act, 2023, s.72A and s.43A of the IT Act, and the common law.
Essay10–15 marks"Distinguish the powers under ss.69, 69A and 70 of the IT Act, 2000, with reference to their respective procedural safeguards and judicial treatment."
Model answer

Structure. A tabular comparison is the right form for this question, followed by three short paragraphs on judicial treatment. Reproduce the table at §1.3 above and then add:

s.69 — interception. A covert power over the content of communications, exercised by a reasoned written order of the competent authority (the Union or State Home Secretary) directing an agency of the appropriate Government to intercept, monitor or decrypt. Its grounds are the widest in the cluster because they include “for investigation of any offence”. Its safeguards are in the 2009 Interception Rules: Rule 8's alternative‑means requirement; Rule 11's 60‑day validity renewable to 180 days; Rule 22's Review Committee every two months; Rules 15–17's confidentiality; and Rules 12–13's record‑keeping. Judicial treatment: the Telegraph Act analogue was scrutinised in PUCL (1997), which read procedural safeguards into s.5(2) and held telephone tapping to infract Article 21 unless permitted under the procedure established by law. But s.69 itself has never been comprehensively reviewed post‑Puttaswamy — the Unit's most important gap.

s.69A — blocking. An overt power over public access to already‑published information, exercised by a reasoned written order of the Central Government or an authorised officer, directed to an agency of the Government or an intermediary. Its grounds are confined to the Article 19(2) subjects, with no general investigative limb. Its safeguards are in the 2009 Blocking Rules: the Designated Officer (not below Joint Secretary); the Nodal Officer; the Committee of Government personnel; Rule 8's 48‑hour notice and hearing to the originator as well as the intermediary; the Secretary, DoIT's approval; Rule 9's emergency route with 48‑hour Committee consideration; Rule 14's Review Committee; and Rule 16's confidentiality. Judicial treatment: expressly upheld in Shreya Singhal as “a narrowly drawn provision with several safeguards”, the Court holding that the absence of ss.95 and 96 CrPC safeguards did not make the Rules infirm.

s.70 — protected systems. A protective power, exercised by notification in the Official Gazette declaring a computer resource affecting Critical Information Infrastructure a protected system, followed by a written authorisation of persons who may access it. Its “safeguard” is the access‑control mechanism itself; there is no hearing, no review committee and no reasons requirement, because the provision does not restrict speech but creates an access‑control offence. Judicial treatment: not seriously contested — the critical‑infrastructure logic is uncontroversial — but note that unauthorised access or attempt carries up to ten years, the second‑highest penalty in the Act after cyber terrorism (s.66F).

Conclusion. The three powers differ in object (content versus access versus infrastructure), addressee (a Government agency versus an intermediary or agency versus all persons), grounds (broadest for s.69; Article 19(2)‑confined for s.69A; consequence‑based for s.70), and judicial treatment (untested post‑Puttaswamy for s.69; upheld in Shreya Singhal for s.69A; uncontested for s.70). The common thread is the Puttaswamy three‑fold test, and the common weakness is the reliance on internal executive review rather than independent authorisation.

🎯 THE KEY RULE — one paragraph to memorise

The State's cyber‑security powers under the IT Act, 2000 are three distinct powers: s.69 (interception, monitoring or decryption of the content of communications — covert, directed to a Government agency, on the broadest grounds including “investigation of any offence”); s.69A (blocking of public access to published information — overt, directed to an agency or an intermediary, on grounds confined to the Article 19(2) subjects); and s.70 (designation of protected systems affecting Critical Information Infrastructure — protective, with unauthorised access or attempt punishable by up to ten years). 13.2 Common exam mistakes — avoid every one of these

  • Conflating s.69 and s.69A. One is covert interception of content directed to a Government agency; the other is overt blocking of public access directed to an agency or an intermediary. Different objects, different grounds, different rule‑books, different judicial treatment.
  • Citing Puttaswamy without stating the three‑fold test by name. This is the single most common lost‑marks error in the Unit.
  • Describing Shreya Singhal as simply an anti‑government judgment. It struck down s.66A and upheld s.69A — in the same judgment. The discriminating application of the vagueness doctrine is the point.
  • Assuming India has a GDPR‑style codified right to be forgotten. It does not. Only s.12 DPDP's narrower erasure right and a fragmented High Court jurisprudence.
  • Writing about interception without mentioning the Telegraph Act. s.5(2) and Rule 419A are the parallel, still‑operative regime, and the Radia tapes were intercepted under them.
  • Omitting the CERT‑In 2022 Directions. They are the operative cyber‑security law for every Indian business and the most testable recent development in the Unit.
  • Forgetting the penalty hierarchy. s.70 protected system — 10 years; s.69(4), s.69A(3), s.69B(4) — 7 years; s.70B(7) — 1 year; s.66F cyber terrorism — life imprisonment.
  • Stating that Anuradha Bhasin restored internet access. It did not — it set the framework and directed a review. Note the mixed outcome.
  • Confusing s.69A content‑blocking with network shutdown. They are different statutory regimes: the IT Act on one side, the Telegraph Act and the 2017 Suspension Rules on the other.
  • Overstating the Ratan Tata holding. The petition was not decided on the merits in a conventional sense; the CBI found no criminality in fourteen inquiries; the Union of India submitted that nothing survived after Puttaswamy.
  • Ignoring the s.79 / Rule 3(1)(d) interlock. s.69A is the power; the 2021 Rules supply the duty; s.79 supplies the safe harbour for compliance. All three must appear in a complete answer.
  • Failing to note the 3‑hour and 2‑hour clocks. Since 20 February 2026, removal on a court order or Government notification is required within 3 hours, and action on non‑consensual intimate imagery within 2 hours.
