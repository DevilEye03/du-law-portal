Unit IX is the Unit in which every other Unit is tested. Units I–VIII told you what the law of cyberspace is. This Unit asks the question that precedes all of them: whose law, and which court? A website hosted in Arizona, read in Delhi, sold through a server in Singapore and complained about in Pandharpur — where is the wrong, where is the cause of action, and which court may try it?

This is the last Unit of the syllabus, and the one with the largest number of prescribed cases — seven. Four of them are printed in the DU case material and are therefore the primary source for this Unit: Banyan Tree Holdings (P) Ltd. v. A. Murali Krishna Reddy (pp. 205–230), My Space Inc. v. 🎯 The one idea of this Unit — and the answer to almost every question

Territorial jurisdiction still rules; the internet merely makes it hard to locate the territory. Nothing in the IT Act, 2000 abolishes the ordinary rules of jurisdiction. Section 1(2) of the IT Act still says the Act "shall extend to the whole of India"; s.20 of the CPC and ss.177–179 of the CrPC still ask where the defendant resides, works for gain, or where the cause of action arose; and s.3 IPC still treats an offence committed outside India as triable in India only where some Indian law makes it so triable. What the internet does is dislocate the three things those rules depend on — (i) the place of the act, (ii) the place of the effect, and (iii) the identity of the person who did it. Every case in this Unit is a different judicial answer to the same question: which of those three dislocated elements is enough to found jurisdiction?

The single most important holding for the examination is the answer of the Division Bench of the Delhi High Court in Banyan Tree: the mere fact that a defendant's website is accessible in the forum State is not enough. The plaintiff must show, prima facie, that the defendant "purposefully availed" itself of the forum jurisdiction — that the website was specifically targeted at viewers in the forum State for commercial transactions, and that the targeting caused injury or harm there. Learn that sentence by heart; you will use it in at least five of the seven cases and in almost every question.

1No geographical boundaries — the end of territory

"No geographical boundaries" is the first phrase of the syllabus and it is not rhetoric. It is a statement about the architecture of the internet, and every jurisdictional problem in this Unit is a consequence of it.

1.1 Why the internet has no geography

The internet was designed without a location layer. Its two addressing systems — the Internet Protocol (IP) address and the Domain Name System (DNS) — are routing and naming systems, not territorial ones. An IP address tells a router where to send a packet next; it does not, and cannot, tell a court where a legal event happened. A DNS name is a globally unique label resolved by a hierarchy of root and top-level servers; the registrant of example.in may be in Tokyo, the registrar in Toronto, the name server in Frankfurt and the web server in Virginia — while the only person reading the page sits in Karol Bagh, Delhi.

The consequence is that the four facts on which every jurisdictional rule depends become simultaneously true in several places and false in all of them:

THE ACT

Where was the act done?

Uploading a page is a keystroke. Where did it happen? At the author's desk? On the server that stored the file? On the content delivery network node that cached it? On the user's own machine that rendered it?
Why it matters: s.177 CrPC asks where an offence "was committed"; s.20(c) CPC asks where the cause of action "arises". Both presuppose a single answer.
THE EFFECT

Where was the harm felt?

A defamatory page published once is "read" a million times in a hundred countries. The harm — the lowering of reputation, the loss of goodwill — is felt wherever the reader is, and nowhere in particular.
Why it matters: this is the Calder "effects" test, and the point on which Indian and English law part company. See §3.4.
THE PERSON

Who is the defendant?

Pseudonymity, shell registrations, cloud hosting, VPNs and offshore intermediaries mean the person who "did" the act may be unidentifiable, or identifiable only as a foreign corporation with no presence anywhere near the forum.
Why it matters: s.20(a)/(b) CPC and s.177 CrPC key jurisdiction to the defendant's residence or business. A defendant with no residence and no business defeats them both.
THE PROPERTY

What is the res, and where is it?

Software, a domain name, a database, a cryptocurrency balance — none has a natural situs. A website is not "in" the country of its server any more than a telephone conversation is "in" the exchange that carried it.
Why it matters: ss.16–19 CPC (immovable property, specific performance) and in rem jurisdiction over domain names (US ACPA) both need a situs the internet does not supply.

1.2 Sovereignty and the "effects" of an online act

The classical theory of criminal jurisdiction, inherited from the Lotus case (France v. Turkey, PCIJ 1927), distinguishes subjective territoriality (jurisdiction where the act was done) from objective territoriality (jurisdiction where the effect was produced). Both are accepted by international law; what is contested is how much effect is needed.

📘 The two territorial principles, as the Banyan Tree Division Bench set them out

Subjective territoriality — the State may regulate conduct that begins within its territory, even if it is completed abroad. Louis Feraud is the classic illustration: the offender acted inside the State; the effect outside is irrelevant.

Objective territoriality (the "effects" test) — the State may regulate conduct that produces its harmful effect within its territory, even if the act was done abroad. This is the basis on which the French court in Yahoo! Inc. v. La Ligue Contre le Racisme et l'Antisemitisme (LICRA) ordered Yahoo to prevent French users from accessing Nazi memorabilia auctions, and on which the Victorian Supreme Court and the Australian High Court in Dow Jones & Co. Inc. v. Gutnick (2002) HCA 56 allowed a defamation action in Victoria for material published on a US server. As the Banyan Tree Division Bench recorded, Victoria had a long-arm statute providing for jurisdiction based on the mere happening of damage within the jurisdiction, and it was that statute — not the common law — which did the work.

The problem with a pure "effects" test is that it is universalising. If any harmful effect felt in a State is enough, then every website in the world is subject to the law of every State in which it can be read. That is why the scholarly middle path — surveyed in Banyan Tree from Michael Geist's article in 16 Berkeley Technology Law Journal (2001) and Thomas Schultz's article in the European Journal of International Law (2008) — proposes targeting: "something more than effects, but less than physical presence". Targeting is exactly what the Division Bench adopted.

⚠️ Do not confuse the two "effect" ideas in this syllabus

(i) Effects as a basis of jurisdiction — the Calder v. Jones / Dow Jones v. Gutnick line, used to create jurisdiction over a foreign defendant. (ii) Effects as an element of the cause of action — e.g. the "injury or harm to the plaintiff within the forum State" that Banyan Tree requires the plaintiff to plead and show prima facie once targeting is established. A candidate who blurs the two loses marks. Say: "the effects test is applied in conjunction with the sliding scale test; the specific targeting must have resulted in injury within the forum State."

1.3 Public international law and private international law — the two halves of the syllabus phrase

The syllabus asks about "rules of private international law". That phrase is doing specific work, and it is worth being precise, because an examiner will reward the distinction.

Public international law — the State-to-State half
  • Question: may this State regulate this conduct at all?
  • Sources: sovereignty, comity, the territoriality and nationality principles, treaties, UN resolutions, the Tallinn Manual on the International Law Applicable to Cyber Operations.
  • Remedy for breach: diplomatic protest, counter-measures — not a court order.
  • In this Unit: explains why s.1(2) and s.75 IT Act exist at all — Parliament had to legislate for extraterritorial reach because it is not automatic.
Private international law — the party-to-party half
  • Question: which court, which law, and will the resulting judgment be recognised?
  • Sources: ss.9, 13, 19, 20, 44 CPC; the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996; the Hague Conventions; the Brussels I Regulation; contractual choice-of-forum clauses.
  • Remedy for breach: the suit is dismissed, stayed or the plaint returned under O.VII R.10 CPC.
  • In this Unit: explains Banyan Tree, WWE, Big Tree, Impresario — all of which are choice-of-forum disputes, not choice-of-law disputes.
🔑 The trap that catches most candidates

All seven DU cases in this Unit are forum-selection cases, not choice-of-law cases. No Indian court in any of them applied foreign law. So when a question says "rules of private international law", do not answer it by listing lex loci delicti and lex fori and stopping. Answer it by showing how Indian courts have, in the absence of a long-arm statute, built a targeting test into the existing CPC provisions — and then, if you have room, add the comparative material (§2 and §14). The best answers say: "India has no long-arm statute; therefore the common law must be imported into s.20(c) CPC, and the imported principle is purposeful availment."

💡 Real example to quote in the examination

Take the facts of Banyan Tree itself: a Singapore company, a Hyderabad resident, a brochure downloadable from a website, a Delhi plaintiff. The defendant never sent anything to Delhi except, on one occasion, a brochure. The website was, at worst, "passive plus". Under the old Casio approach, the Delhi High Court would have had jurisdiction because the site could be accessed from Delhi. Under the Division Bench's approach it does not — unless the plaintiff shows targeting. The practical consequence: the plaintiff must now sue in Hyderabad, where the defendant actually is. That is the whole Unit in one sentence.

📝 Section 1 in five lines — the answer to "explain the concept of no geographical boundaries"
  1. The internet's addressing layer (IP, DNS) is designed for routing, not for territory; a packet traverses many jurisdictions and settles in none.
  2. Consequently the four facts on which jurisdiction depends — place of act, place of effect, identity of the actor, situs of the res — are dislocated.
  3. Sovereignty is not lost: it must be re-grounded either on objective territoriality (effects) or on targeting (deliberate direction at the forum).
  4. Indian law re-grounds it through s.20(c) CPC (civil) and ss.177–179 CrPC (criminal), read with s.1(2) and s.75 IT Act and s.3 IPC (extraterritoriality).
  5. The judicial compromise, laid down in Banyan Tree, is purposeful availment through specific targeting for commercial transactions — "something more than effects, but less than physical presence".

2Applicability of traditional rules of jurisdiction & the rules of private international law

The syllabus asks two things at once: whether the traditional rules apply to the internet, and what the rules of private international law are. The answer to the first is "yes, unchanged"; the answer to the second is the toolkit the courts use to make the first answer work.

2.1 The traditional rules of jurisdiction in Indian law — unchanged, and why that matters

No statute in India abolishes or modifies the ordinary jurisdictional rules because a dispute arises online. The scheme remains exactly what it was in 1908 and 1973:

📘 s.20, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 — "Other suits to be instituted where defendants reside or cause of action arises" (source: CPC 1908)

"Subject to the limitations aforesaid, every suit shall be instituted in Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction—

(a) the defendant, or each of the defendants where there are more than one, at the time of the commencement of the suit, actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain; or

(b) any of the defendants, where there are more than one, at the time of the commencement of the suit actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain, provided that in such case either the leave of the Court is given, or the defendants who do not reside, or carry on business, or personally works for gain, as aforesaid, acquiesce in such institution; or

(c) the cause of action, wholly or in part, arises.

Explanation.—A corporation shall be deemed to carry on business at its sole or principal office in India or, in respect of any cause of action arising at any place where it has also a subordinate office, at such place."

Three observations that carry the whole Unit:

  1. s.20 is exhaustive for "other suits". It follows the specific rules in ss.15–19 (immovable property, compensation for wrongs, trust property) and applies to everything else — including passing off, copyright infringement, defamation and e-commerce disputes. The words "subject to the limitations aforesaid" mean ss.15–19 must be checked first.
  2. s.20(c) is the internet provision, in practice. When the defendant is abroad and has no office in India, (a) and (b) are unavailable and the whole argument is about (c): "the cause of action, wholly or in part, arises". Note the words "wholly or in part" — a part is enough. That is the hook on which every internet jurisdiction case hangs.
  3. The Explanation is a corporation-specific rule and it is the reason Big Tree Entertainment came out the opposite way from World Wrestling Entertainment. See §9.
📘 The special forum provisions — s.62(2), Copyright Act, 1957 and s.134(2), Trade Marks Act, 1999 (source: the Acts)

s.62(2) Copyright Act, 1957: "For the purpose of sub-section (1), a 'district court having jurisdiction' shall, notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (5 of 1908), or any other law for the time being in force, include a district court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction, at the time of the institution of the suit or other proceeding, the person instituting the suit or other proceeding or, where there are more than one such persons, any of them actually and voluntarily resides or carries on business or personally works for gain."

s.134(2) Trade Marks Act, 1999 is in pari materia terms, and adds: "Explanation.—For the purposes of sub-section (2), 'person' includes the registered proprietor and the registered user." Both are additional forums for the plaintiff; the non obstante clause does not oust s.20 CPC (Indian Performing Rights Society Ltd. v. Sanjay Dalia, (2015) 10 SCC 161).

⚠️ Two limits on the "additional forum" that the examination expects
  • The restriction in Sanjay Dalia: where the plaintiff resides or carries on business at a place and the cause of action has also arisen there, the plaintiff cannot ignore that place and sue at a distant subordinate office where no cause of action has arisen. The convenience is the plaintiff's, but it is not unlimited.
  • The Banyan Tree limit: s.134(2) TMA and s.62(2) Copyright Act are "long arm" provisions in a limited sense only — confined to infringement actions by plaintiffs carrying on business within the forum court's jurisdiction. They do not help a plaintiff in a passing-off action, and they do not help a plaintiff who carries on no business in the forum at all.

2.3 Service of summons abroad — the practical point that examiners love

A judgment against a website owner in Singapore is worthless if the summons cannot be served. The Indian Code provides a complete machinery, and the IT Act's rules on electronic records do not displace it:

📘 Service of an Indian summons abroad (source: CPC 1908, Order V)
  • Order V Rule 25 — where the defendant resides outside India and has no agent empowered to accept service, the court may serve by post, fax, e-mail or other appropriate means of communication as approved by the High Court; for Pakistan and Bangladesh, summons may also be sent to the court having jurisdiction there.
  • Order V Rule 26 — where a Political Agent has been appointed, or a court has been established or continued by the Central Government with power to serve summons in the foreign territory, or the Central Government has declared by notification that service by such a court shall be valid, the summons may be sent to that Political Agent or court; the returned endorsement is deemed evidence of service.
  • Order V Rule 26A — summonses to be served on defendants residing or carrying on business in a notified foreign territory may be sent to an officer of the government of that territory specified by the Central Government.
  • Order V Rule 20 — substituted service by advertisement, useful where the defendant's identity or address is unknown (a common problem with pseudonymous website operators).
  • s.29(c) CPC — the mirror image: summonses issued by a civil or revenue court outside India to which the Central Government has extended the section by notification "may be sent to the Courts in the territories to which this Code extends, and served as if they were summonses issued by such Courts."
  • Hague Convention on the Service Abroad of Judicial and Extrajudicial Documents in Civil or Commercial Matters, 1965 — India is a party; service is through the designated Central Authority. India has declared its opposition to private and direct service, so a foreign litigant cannot simply e-mail a summons into India.
💡 The examination point about service

Service is not jurisdiction. Serving a summons on a defendant abroad does not give the court jurisdiction over him; it is the machinery for proceeding after jurisdiction is established. Conversely, the absence of an Indian address does not defeat jurisdiction where the cause of action arises in India — it merely makes the suit harder to prosecute. Make this distinction explicitly in any answer that mentions e-mail service; it is a common way to lose the thread.

2.4 India has no long-arm statute — and that single fact shapes everything

In the United States, each State has a long-arm statute authorising its courts to exercise personal jurisdiction over non-residents on specified grounds. The Banyan Tree Division Bench recorded the submission made before it that "in order to attain personal jurisdiction ... there should be a long-arm statute on the basis of which the court could exercise jurisdiction over any individual located outside the state", and noted that "India did not have a long arm statute which dealt with the jurisdiction as regards non-resident Defendants."

⚖️ What the Division Bench did about that gap — the most quotable move in the Unit

Instead of refusing jurisdiction, the Court imported the common law: "the essential principles developed as part of the common law can be adopted without difficulty by our courts in determining whether the forum court has jurisdiction where the alleged breach is related to an activity on the internet." That is the judicial answer to a legislative gap, and it is the technique you should name in your answers: purposive construction of s.20(c) CPC by reference to the common law of personal jurisdiction. Note the contrast with the criminal side: there, in Maqbool Fida Husain, the learned Additional Solicitor General accepted that "our Criminal Code does not deal with such jurisdictional aspect directly" and that the answer "only rested in the power conferred to the Supreme Court of India under Section 406" — i.e. a procedural patch, not a substantive solution. The Court itself said legislative change was needed.

📌 India's nearest thing to long-arm provisions
  • s.134(2) Trade Marks Act, 1999 and s.62(2) Copyright Act, 1957 — plaintiff-side forums, limited by Sanjay Dalia and by Banyan Tree to plaintiffs carrying on business within the jurisdiction.
  • s.92A and s.138 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 — provisions for dealing with infringing marks on the internet and for rectification, not jurisdiction.
  • s.62(2) is not a true long-arm provision: it requires a cause of action arising in India, because Indian copyright is territorial. It cannot be used to sue for an act of infringement committed wholly outside India.
  • s.75 IT Act — a genuine, narrow extraterritorial provision, but only for offences and contraventions under the IT Act itself. See §13.

3Passive v. interactive web sites — the sliding scale and the targeting test

"Passive/interactive web sites" is the second substantive phrase of the syllabus. It is the point at which the internet problem becomes tractable: courts stopped asking "where is the website?" and started asking "what does the website do, and at whom?"

3.1 The four-rung taxonomy

The taxonomy comes from the plaintiff's counsel's submissions in Banyan Tree, which the Division Bench adopted as the framing for the whole debate. It is worth reproducing because it is the vocabulary of every subsequent Indian case.

RUNG 1

Passive website

A website that merely makes information available. It does not enable the viewer to transact with the host; it may not even enable the viewer to contact the host. "Simply created a web site and permitted anyone who could find it to access it."
Consequence: an express absence of intention to market to the forum State. No jurisdiction. Banyan Tree: "A passive website, with no intention to specifically target audiences outside the State where the host of the website is located, cannot vest the forum court with jurisdiction."
RUNG 2

"Passive plus"

A website that is largely informational but adds a little interactivity — a downloadable brochure, an enquiry form, a feedback box, a telephone number, an e-mail link — without enabling a commercial transaction to be concluded.
Consequence: the plaintiff's counsel urged that because the host had not blocked forum-State viewers by "purposeful avoidance", the site must be treated as targeted. The Division Bench rejected the euphemism: "whether euphemistically termed as 'passive plus' or 'interactive'", the plaintiff must still show specific targeting for commercial transactions.
RUNG 3

Interactive website

A website that permits the browser to do something with the host — subscribe, register, book, order, pay, download against payment, upload content. India TV: the site "is not wholly of a 'passive' character. It has a specific section for subscription to its services and the options (provided on the website itself) for the countries whose residents can subscribe to the services include India."
Consequence: jurisdiction becomes arguable, but interactivity alone is still not enough. Banyan Tree: "It is not enough merely to show that the website hosted by the Defendant is an interactive one"; and "Even an interactive website, which is not shown to be specifically targeted at viewers in the forum state for commercial transactions, will not result in the court of the forum state having jurisdiction."
RUNG 4

Integral to the business

A website through which the defendant actually conducts its business — orders are taken, accepted, paid for and fulfilled through the site itself. WWE: "When, through the mode of the software and the browser, the transaction is confirmed and payment is made to the appellant/plaintiff through its website, the appellant/plaintiff accepts the offer of the customer at Delhi."
Consequence: the strongest case for jurisdiction. This is where the Zippo scale tops out: "the likelihood that personal jurisdiction can be constitutionally exercised is directly proportionate to the nature and quality of commercial activity that an entity conducts over the internet."

The court then described the sliding scale: at one end, a defendant "has done no more than advertise on the Internet" and "passive" posting, where jurisdiction is unlikely; at the other end, a defendant "clearly does business over the Internet" through an "interactive Web site" where "a user can exchange information with the host computer", where "the likelihood that personal jurisdiction can be constitutionally exercised is most likely"; in the middle, "interactive Web sites where a user can exchange information with the host computer", where "the exercise of jurisdiction is determined by examining the level of interactivity and commercial nature of the exchange of information that occurs on the Web site."

3.3 The "effects" test — Calder, Yahoo! (LICRA) and Dow Jones v. Gutnick

CALDER

Calder v. Jones (1984)

The "effects" test: jurisdiction lies where the defendant (i) committed an intentional act, (ii) expressly aimed at the forum State, (iii) causing harm the defendant knows is likely to be suffered in the forum State.
Why it matters: the third limb — harm knowingly suffered in the forum — is the ancestor of "targeting". The Banyan Tree Division Bench expressly required the plaintiff to plead and show "that the specific targeting of the forum State by the Defendant resulted in an injury or harm to the Plaintiff within the forum State."
YAHOO!

Yahoo! Inc. v. LICRA (France, 2000)

A French court ordered Yahoo to prevent access from France to auctions of Nazi memorabilia, applying the effects test: permitting visualisation in France and the participation of a France-based surfer constituted a wrong in France.
Why it matters: the classic demonstration that a pure effects test produces worldwide compliance obligations for a single website. It is the reductio that drives courts towards targeting.
GUTNICK

Dow Jones & Co. Inc. v. Gutnick (2002) HCA 56

The Australian High Court allowed a defamation action in Victoria for material published on a US server, holding that the harm to reputation is suffered where the material is read and understood.
The crucial caveat, as Banyan Tree recorded it: "the state of Victoria in the said case did have a long arm law which was held to be valid and which permitted extension of jurisdiction." Gutnick is not authority for the proposition that internet publication is actionable everywhere; it is authority that a long-arm statute, where it exists, can do the work.

3.4 The Indian reception — Casio, India TV, and the rule after Banyan Tree

Casio India Co. Ltd. v. Ashita Tele Systems Pvt. Ltd., 2003 (27) PTC 265 (Del) — OVERRULED
  • Passing-off action; defendant carrying on business from Bombay; domain name www.casioindia.com registered by a former distributor; registrar made defendant no. 2.
  • Held: "once access to the impugned domain name website could be had from anywhere else, the residence of the Defendant [is irrelevant]". Since a mere likelihood of deception suffices for passing off, "it was not at all required to be proved that any actual deception took place at Delhi".
  • Therefore: "the fact that the website of Defendant no. 1 can be accessed from Delhi is sufficient to invoke the territorial jurisdiction of this Court…"
  • Fate: the Division Bench in Banyan Tree held: "This Court is therefore unable to agree with the proposition laid down in Casio. The said decision cannot be held to be good law and to that extent is overruled."
(India TV) Independent News Service (P) Ltd. v. India Broadcast Live LLC, 2007 (35) PTC 177 (Del) — AFFIRMED IN APPROACH
  • Plaintiff ran the Hindi news channel "INDIA TV"; defendants hosted indiatvlive.com with the words "INDIA TV" displayed prominently inside a television outline; defendants simultaneously sued in the Arizona District Court for "reverse domain name hijacking".
  • Defendants argued the Delhi court was not the forum conveniens and that India had no long-arm statute.
  • The court applied the purposeful availment test and the three Cybersell factors: sufficient connection with the forum; whether the cause of action arises out of the defendant's activities within the forum; and whether the exercise of jurisdiction would be reasonable.
  • Held: the site "is not wholly of a 'passive' character. It has a specific section for subscription to its services and the options ... for the countries whose residents can subscribe to the services include India." Defendant intended "to target expatriate Indians as well as Indians within the country"; on the effects test, damage to an Indian news channel's goodwill would be suffered in India.
  • Fate: "India TV appears to be somewhat closer to the development of law in this regard since the decision in Casio" — its approach was approved.
📝 The four propositions to write out in answer to "distinguish passive from interactive websites"
  1. Passive = information only, express absence of intention to market → no jurisdiction, however accessible the site.
  2. Interactive = the user can transact with the host → jurisdiction is possible, but the plaintiff must still show the site was specifically targeted at forum viewers for commercial transactions.
  3. The degree of interactivity matters: "limited interactivity may also not be sufficient" (India TV, following Cybersell).
  4. The nature of the activity matters more than the label: whether the site permits a commercial transaction to be concluded with a forum user, and whether the specific targeting caused injury or harm to the plaintiff in the forum.
💡 Real examples to keep in your answer — orderable online v. requiring physical presence

The Banyan Tree Division Bench expressly noted that the fairness of a trap transaction depends on the nature of the goods or services. Where the goods can be ordered online and delivered — books, DVDs, cosmetics, downloadable software — a single online order is a realistic transaction and repetition is likely. Where the service requires the customer to be physically present — buying a car, buying furniture, dining at a restaurant, using a spa — an online booking is only a step towards the transaction, and the transaction itself is completed elsewhere. That distinction is what decided Impresario (§10) and it is what will decide the DU self-learning exercise on spa services in Hyderabad (§15, Exercise 2).

4Personal jurisdiction on the defendant and cause of action

"Personal jurisdiction on defendant — cause of action" is the third substantive phrase of the syllabus. It has two halves: who can be sued where (personal jurisdiction), and why the court's territory is the right one (cause of action).

4.1 Three kinds of jurisdiction — get the vocabulary right

SUBJECT MATTER

Jurisdiction over the subject matter

Does this court, at all, have power to try suits of this class and of this value? Pecuniary and statutory jurisdiction.
Cannot be conferred by consent. In IP matters it is fixed by statute: s.134(1) TMA (no court inferior to a District Court), s.62(1) Copyright Act, and the Commercial Courts Act 2015 for "commercial disputes".
TERRITORIAL

Territorial (local) jurisdiction

Is this the right place among courts of the same class? Governed by ss.15–20 CPC and ss.177–189 CrPC.
This is the internet problem. It is also the only one that can be waived by acquiescence — s.20(b) proviso, and s.21 CPC (objections to jurisdiction must be taken at the earliest opportunity).
PERSONAL

Personal (in personam) jurisdiction

Power over the person of the defendant — as opposed to jurisdiction over property or an interest in it.
The India TV formulation: "there should be a long-arm statute on the basis of which the court could exercise jurisdiction over any individual located outside the state." India has none; therefore personal jurisdiction must be built out of s.20(a), (b) and (c).

4.2 Section 20 CPC applied to a website

Work the three clauses mechanically. This is the single most useful examination technique in the Unit.

Figure 4 — Section 20 CPC decision tree. In an internet dispute the defendant is usually offshore, so clauses (a) and (b) drop out and everything turns on (c) plus the corporation Explanation. Note that the Explanation is what separates WWE (no office, natural-person reasoning applied to a company) from Big Tree (company, no office, Explanation applied).

4.3 The procedural machinery — Order VII Rules 1 and 6, Order VII Rule 10, and s.151

📘 The pleading and return-of-plaint provisions (source: CPC 1908, Order VII)
  • Order VII Rule 1 — "the plaint shall contain the following particulars: … (e) the facts constituting the cause of action and when it arose; (f) the facts showing that the Court has jurisdiction…" Consequence: the jurisdictional plea is a mandatory pleading. If the plaint does not plead the facts showing cause of action in the forum, the court has nothing to examine.
  • Order VII Rule 6 — where the plaint is rejected, the plaintiff must be given time to amend; and a rejection for want of jurisdiction does not preclude a fresh suit in the proper court.
  • Order VII Rule 10 — "the plaint shall be returned to the plaintiff … to be presented to the proper court". This is the remedy the court used in WWE (initially), Big Tree and Impresario. Note that it is a return, not a dismissal: limitation is saved by s.14 of the Limitation Act, 1963 and the plaintiff may refile in the proper court.
  • Order VII Rule 11 — rejection of plaint (as distinct from return) where it does not disclose a cause of action, where the relief is barred by law, and so on.
  • s.21 CPC — an objection as to place of suing must be taken at the earliest possible opportunity and in any case before settlement of issues; a defendant who appears and pleads to the merits may be held to have acquiesced (which is what the proviso to s.20(b) contemplates).
  • s.151 CPC — the inherent power of the court "to make such orders as may be necessary for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of the process of the Court". This is the provision under which courts mould relief where liability is not yet ascertained — as the Division Bench did in My Space v. Super Cassettes when it substituted a specific 36-hour take-down regime for the single judge's open-ended injunction.
💡 The examination technique — how to answer any "does the Delhi court have jurisdiction?" question
  1. Identify the action. Passing off, infringement, defamation, or contract? If it is passing off, s.134(2) TMA is unavailable (it covers only clauses (a) and (b) of s.134(1) — infringement and rights in a registered mark).
  2. Run s.20(a) and (b). Does the defendant reside, carry on business, or personally work for gain in the forum? If the defendant is a company, apply the Explanation and ask whether it has any office there.
  3. Run s.20(c). Where does the cause of action arise, wholly or in part? For a website, ask: was the site specifically targeted at forum viewers for commercial transactions?
  4. Check the special forums (s.62(2) Copyright, s.134(2) TMA) and their limits (Sanjay Dalia; Banyan Tree's "limited long arm").
  5. Deal with trap orders. If the plaintiff relies on its own test purchase or booking, apply Banyan Tree: a lone trap transaction is not purposeful availment; a series must be obtained by fair means and pleaded unambiguously.
  6. Conclude with the remedy. If there is no jurisdiction, the plaint is returned under O.VII R.10 CPC — not dismissed — and the plaintiff may refile in the proper court.

5Case brief 1 — Banyan Tree Holdings (P) Ltd. v. A. Murali Krishna Reddy

Case 1 of 7 · Primary source: printed in the DU case material, pp. 205–230

Banyan Tree Holding (P) Ltd. v. A. Murali Krishna Reddy & Anr.

CS (OS) No. 894/2008 Delhi High Court, Division Bench Decided 23 November 2009 S. Muralidhar J. (for the Division Bench) Reported: 2010 (42) PTC 361 (Del)

1 · Facts

The plaintiff, Banyan Tree Holding (P) Limited, is a company with its registered office at Singapore, part of a group of companies in the hospitality business. Since 1994 it adopted and used the word mark "Banyan Tree" and the banyan tree device. It claims that by extensive and continuous use the mark and device acquired secondary meaning, became highly distinctive and came to be associated with the plaintiff and its sister concerns. It has maintained the websites www.banyantree.com and www.banyantreespa.com since 1996, both accessible in India.

The plaintiff does not hold a registration for the mark or device in India; its application was stated to be pending. Since 2002 it has, in collaboration with the Oberoi Group, been operating 15 spas across India.

In October 2007 the plaintiff learnt that the defendants had initiated work on a project under the name "Banyan Tree Retreat". Defendant no. 1 is the promoter of defendant no. 2, a company. The defendants are located at Hyderabad. The plaintiff alleged that the word mark and device adopted by the defendants for their retreat were deceptively similar to its own, that the use was dishonest, an attempt to encash on the plaintiff's reputation and goodwill, and calculated to cause confusion and deception. The suit prayed for an injunction restraining the defendants from using the mark and device.

🔑 The "peculiar feature" — remember this sentence, it decides the case

"A peculiar feature of the present suit which is an action for passing off is that neither the plaintiff nor the Defendants is located within the territorial jurisdiction of this Court. The Plaintiff is a company having its registered office at Singapore whereas the Defendants 1 and 2 are at Hyderabad." The plaintiff is a foreign company with 15 spas in India but no office and no pleaded carrying on of business in Delhi. The suit is passing off, not infringement of a registered mark.

2 · The jurisdictional plea in the plaint (para 30)

The plaintiff claimed territorial jurisdiction under s.20 CPC on two grounds:

  • Accessibility and use of the website. "The Defendants have presence in Delhi through their website http://www.makprojects.com/banyantree.htm which is accessible in Delhi." It is "not a passive website. It not only provides contact information but also seeks feedback and inputs from its customers through an interactive web-page." The services are offered to customers in Delhi "because of the ubiquity, universality and utility of the features of the Internet and the World Wide Web and hence the cause of action has arisen within the jurisdiction of this Court."
  • One brochure sent to a Delhi resident. There was "at least one instance of the Defendants' brochure being sent to a Delhi resident for the purposes of sale of property."

3 · Procedural history and the questions referred

The learned single Judge, by an order dated 11.8.2008, referred questions concerning the Court's jurisdiction to the Division Bench, having examined the developments in the law in India, Canada, the USA and England. The four questions as framed were:

  1. Whether the court can entertain the suit, having regard to the averments and documents, in the context of the special provisions in the Trade Mark and Copyright Acts, which do not provide for exercise of jurisdiction based on internet or web-presence of such alleged infringers, even while making explicit departure from the general law as to territorial jurisdiction;
  2. Whether the court can entertain the suit, in the absence of a long-arm statute, having regard to the existing state of law, particularly s.20 CPC, and the impact, if any, of the Information Technology Act, 2000 on it;
  3. Applicable standards for entertaining a suit, based on use of a trade mark by a defendant on its website, or infringement or passing off of the plaintiff's trade mark, in such website, and the relevant criteria to entertain such suits;
  4. Applicable standards and criteria where the plaintiff relies exclusively on "trap orders" or transactions, in relation to passing off or trade mark infringement cases, as constituting "use" or cause of action, as the case may be.

The Division Bench, after hearing Mr. Praveen Anand for the plaintiff, reframed them into three:

The three questions as reframed by the Division Bench

(i) For the purposes of a passing off action, or an infringement action where the Plaintiff is not carrying on business within the jurisdiction of a court, in what circumstances can it be said that the hosting of a universally accessible website by the Defendants lends jurisdiction to such Court where such suit is filed ("the forum court")?

(ii) In a passing off or infringement action, where the defendant is sought to be sued on the basis that its website is accessible in the forum state, what is the extent of the burden on the Plaintiff to prima facie establish that the forum court has jurisdiction to entertain the suit?

(iii) Is it permissible for the Plaintiff to establish such prima facie case through "trap orders" or "trap transactions"?

4 · Issues

  • Whether s.20(a) or s.20(b) CPC applies at all, given that neither party resides or carries on business in Delhi.
  • Whether s.134(2) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 or s.62(2) of the Copyright Act, 1957 can supply jurisdiction in a passing-off action brought by a plaintiff not carrying on business in the forum.
  • Whether, in the absence of a long-arm statute, the mere accessibility of the defendant's website in Delhi vests jurisdiction in the Delhi High Court under s.20(c) CPC.
  • What test applies — "purposeful availment" or the plaintiff's "purposeful avoidance"?
  • Whether the "effects" test can be applied independently of the "sliding scale" test.
  • What is the extent of the plaintiff's burden, and whether it can be discharged by trap orders.
  • The status of the earlier single Judge decisions in Casio and India TV.

5 · Arguments of the parties

For the Plaintiff (Mr. Praveen Anand)
  • Three-factor test. In determining whether the court has territorial jurisdiction, three factors must be accounted for: (a) the nature of the website, (b) the intention of the website's host to market its products in Delhi, and (c) the effect of such action by the defendants in Delhi.
  • Taxonomy. The website may be passive, interactive or active. Where the website is passive, in the express absence of any intention to market, the forum court would have no jurisdiction.
  • "Passive plus". A mere advertisement on the website, without any move by the defendants to block access to Delhi viewers through a device of "purposeful avoidance", would be a case of "passive plus". All viewers outside Andhra Pradesh are, in the absence of such blocking, targeted by the defendants.
  • Fallback. If the intention to market is not discernible on account of non-application of mind by the website owner, the forum court might still have jurisdiction where there is an effect on the market in the forum state.
  • s.134(2)/s.62(2). These provisions make an explicit departure from the general law of territorial jurisdiction and should be read broadly.
  • Trap orders. The Delhi brochure, and any trap transaction, establishes the cause of action.
For the Defendants / the position the Court adopted
  • No s.20(a) or (b). The defendants are at Hyderabad; they neither reside nor carry on business nor personally work for gain in Delhi. The plaintiff, a Singapore company, does not either. So (a) and (b) are simply unavailable.
  • s.134(2) is not available. The suit is for passing off, and s.134(2) TMA is a long-arm provision only in a limited sense — "confined to infringement actions by Plaintiffs carrying on business within the jurisdiction of the forum court." The plaintiff carries on no business in Delhi.
  • Accessibility is not enough. A passive website, with no intention to specifically target audiences outside the State where the host is located, cannot vest the forum court with jurisdiction.
  • Purposeful avoidance cannot replace purposeful availment. The initial burden is on the plaintiff; the question of blocking filters arises only after the plaintiff has shown interactivity permitting commercial transactions.
  • A lone trap transaction is not purposeful availment.

6 · The Court's reasoning — the survey of law

The Division Bench first disposed of the Indian statutory position (para 8): s.134(2) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999, "like and Section 62 (2) of the Copyright Act 1957", is a long-arm provision in a limited sense, confined to infringement actions by plaintiffs carrying on business within the forum court's jurisdiction. Since this was a passing-off action and neither party resided or carried on business in Delhi, neither s.20(a) nor s.20(b) applied, and the plaintiff had to rely on s.20(c).

It then surveyed the law of the United Kingdom, Australia, the United States and India — the material reproduced at §3 of these notes. The five propositions it extracted are:

The five intermediate conclusions of the Division Bench
  1. No long-arm statute in India. "India did not have a long arm statute which dealt with the jurisdiction as regards non-resident Defendants." Therefore it must be examined whether the defendant's activities "have a sufficient connection with the forum state (India); whether the cause of action arises out of the Defendant's activities within the forum and whether the exercise of jurisdiction would be reasonable." (India TV, applying Cybersell.)
  2. The common law can be adopted. "The essential principles developed as part of the common law can be adopted without difficulty by our courts in determining whether the forum court has jurisdiction where the alleged breach is related to an activity on the internet."
  3. Accessibility alone is rejected. "At the outset, this court does not subscribe to the view that the mere accessibility of the Defendants' website in Delhi would enable this Court to exercise jurisdiction. A passive website, with no intention to specifically target audiences outside the State where the host of the website is located, cannot vest the forum court with jurisdiction. This court is therefore unable to agree with the proposition laid down in Casio. The said decision cannot be held to be good law and to that extent is overruled."
  4. India TV is approved in approach. "The learned single Judge in India TV acknowledged that a mere accessibility of website may not be sufficient to attract jurisdiction of the forum court. This, in the considered view of this Court, is the correct position in law." But India TV did not examine "the finer aspects of the question of jurisdiction based on the nature of the website, the intention of the host of the website to specifically target viewers outside its jurisdiction, and the effect of hosting such website on audiences outside such state."
  5. Purposeful availment, not purposeful avoidance. "This court is not able to accept the submission of the learned counsel for the Plaintiff that the test of 'purposeful availment' must be replaced by the test of 'purposeful avoidance'." The defendant may show in his defence how he avoided the forum state; but the initial burden is on the plaintiff. The issue of filters "will have to be considered while deciding if the Defendant had 'purposefully avoided' the forum state. However, that question will arise only if the Plaintiff has been able to show that the website of the Defendant is interactive and permits commercial transactions to be concluded by the Defendant with a user of the website."

Two further propositions complete the reasoning:

  • Interactivity is not enough. "Jurisdiction of the forum court does not get attracted merely on the basis of interactivity of the website which is accessible in the forum state. The degree of the interactivity apart, the nature of the activity permissible and whether it results in a commercial transaction has to be examined."
  • The effects test is applied in conjunction with the sliding scale. "For the 'effects' test to apply, the Plaintiff must necessarily plead and show prima facie that the specific targeting of the forum State by the Defendant resulted in an injury or harm to the Plaintiff within the forum State... Naturally therefore, this would require the presence of the Plaintiff in the forum state and not merely the possibility of such presence in the future. Secondly, to show that an injurious effect has been felt by the Plaintiff it would have to be shown that viewers in the forum State were specifically targeted. Therefore the 'effects' test would have to be applied in conjunction with the 'sliding scale' test."

7 · Decision — the answers to the three questions

⚖️ Answer to Question (i) — when does hosting a universally accessible website lend jurisdiction?

"For the purposes of a passing off action, or an infringement action where the plaintiff is not carrying on business within the jurisdiction of a court, and where there is no long arm statute, the Plaintiff would have to show that the Defendant purposefully availed itself of the jurisdiction of the forum court. It is not enough merely to show that the website hosted by the Defendant is an interactive one. It would have to be shown that the nature of the activity indulged in by the Defendant by the use of the website was with an intention to conclude a commercial transaction with the website user. ... Secondly, for the purposes of the 'effects' test, the Plaintiff must necessarily plead and show prima facie that the specific targeting of the forum State resulted in injury or harm to the Plaintiff within the forum State."

⚖️ Answer to Question (ii) — the extent of the plaintiff's burden

"For the purposes of Section 20 (c) CPC, in order to show that some part of the cause of action has arisen in the forum state by the use of the internet by the Defendant the Plaintiff will have to show prima facie that the said website, whether euphemistically termed as 'passive plus' or 'interactive', was specifically targeted at viewers in the forum state for commercial transactions. The Plaintiff would have to plead this and produce material to prima facie show that some commercial transaction using the website was entered into by the Defendant with a user of its website within the forum state resulting in an injury or harm to the Plaintiff within the forum state." And expressly: "A mere hosting of a website which can be accessible from anyone from within the jurisdiction of the court is not sufficient for this purpose. ... Even an interactive website, which is not shown to be specifically targeted at viewers in the forum state for commercial transactions, will not result in the court of the forum state having jurisdiction."

⚖️ Answer to Question (iii) — can the prima facie case be established by trap orders?

"The commercial transaction entered into by the Defendant with an internet user located within the jurisdiction of the forum court cannot possibly be a solitary trap transaction since that would not be an instance of 'purposeful' availment by the Defendant. It would have to be a real commercial transaction that the Defendant has with someone not set up by the Plaintiff itself. If the only evidence is in the form of a series of trap transactions, they have to be shown as having been obtained using fair means. The Plaintiff seeking to establish jurisdiction on the basis of such trap transactions would have to aver unambiguously in the plaint, and also place along with it supporting material, to prima facie show that the trap transactions relied upon satisfy the above test."

Disposal: "For determining if on the facts of the present suit, the Plaintiff has been able to show prima facie that this court has the jurisdiction to entertain the suit, it is placed again before the learned single Judge who will first determine that question in light of the law laid down in this judgment." The matter was remitted — the Division Bench did not itself decide whether the Delhi court had jurisdiction on these facts.

8 · Principle evolved

The Banyan Tree principle — the spine of the whole Unit
  1. Mere accessibility is dead. The fact that a defendant's website can be accessed from the forum State does not, without more, give the forum court jurisdiction. Casio is overruled.
  2. Purposeful availment, not purposeful avoidance. The plaintiff bears the initial burden of showing that the defendant purposefully availed itself of the forum jurisdiction.
  3. Intention to conclude a commercial transaction. The nature of the activity indulged in by the defendant through the website must have been with an intention to conclude a commercial transaction with the website user. Interactivity alone is insufficient; the degree of interactivity, the nature of the activity permissible, and whether it results in a commercial transaction must all be examined.
  4. Targeting is the tighter form of the effects test. The plaintiff must plead and show prima facie that the specific targeting of the forum State resulted in injury or harm to the plaintiff within the forum State. The effects test operates in conjunction with the sliding scale test, not independently of it.
  5. Plaintiff's presence is required for the effects test. To feel the injurious effect, the plaintiff must have a presence in the forum State — not merely the possibility of future presence.
  6. Trap orders are scrutinised with jealousy. A solitary trap transaction cannot establish purposeful availment; it must be a real transaction with someone not set up by the plaintiff; a series must be obtained by fair means and pleaded unambiguously with supporting material.
  7. s.134(2) TMA and s.62(2) Copyright Act are limited long-arm provisions, confined to infringement actions by plaintiffs carrying on business within the forum court's jurisdiction.

9 · What the case did NOT decide — and why that matters

  • It did not hold that the Delhi court lacked jurisdiction. It laid down the law and remitted the question to the single Judge.
  • It did not deal with a case where the plaintiff is carrying on business in the forum — in that situation s.134(2)/s.62(2) provide a forum, and the question is different (see WWE, §8).
  • It did not address the criminal side at all. For criminal jurisdiction over internet publications, the only printed authority in the syllabus is Maqbool Fida Husain (§11 and §12).

10 · Significance and criticism

Why the decision is right
  • It prevents universal jurisdiction over every accessible website — the Yahoo!/Inset reductio.
  • It protects defendants from being haled into distant courts by an accident of technology.
  • It supplies a workable, evidence-based test: targeting plus transaction plus injury.
  • It overrules a decision (Casio) that had made the Delhi High Court the default forum for all Indian internet trade mark disputes.
The criticisms to raise in a 15-mark question
  • It is fact-intensive and expensive. Jurisdiction is now decided on evidence of transactions, which means the plaintiff must incur cost before knowing where to sue.
  • It favours offshore defendants. A defendant outside India who never trades with India is effectively immune from Indian suit — even where the plaintiff's goodwill in India is being damaged.
  • "Purposeful availment" is a US due-process concept, imported into a statute (s.20 CPC) that contains no such requirement. The importation is judge-made.
  • It leaves the plaintiff's own presence unaddressed where the plaintiff is a foreign company with Indian customers but no Indian office — precisely the Banyan Tree plaintiff's own position.

The plaintiff has registered office in Tokyo. In other words, does cause of action arise in Delhi. (Hint — Impresario Entertainment Case)". A full model answer is at §15, Exercise 2.

6Case brief 2 — P.R. Transport Agency v. Union of India

Case 2 of 7 · Listed in the DU syllabus · NOT printed in the DU case material

P.R. Transport Agency v. Union of India & Ors.

AIR 2006 All 23 Allahabad High Court, Division Bench Decided 24 September 2005 Sushil Harkauli & Umeshwar Pandey, JJ. Also reported: 2006 (1) AWC 504
Provenance note. This judgment is listed in the DU syllabus (page 9 of the case material) but is not reproduced in it. The facts, submissions and holding set out below are taken from the reported decision (AIR 2006 All 23) and from the standard law-report summaries of it. The statutory text of s.13(3) of the IT Act, 2000 is reproduced from the Act. Treat every quotation below as a reported-decision quotation, not a DU-material quotation.

1 · Facts

An e-auction was held by Bharat Coking Coal Limited (BCCL) — a public sector undertaking under the Union of India — for the allocation of coal from the Dobari Colliery in Jharkhand. The petitioner, P.R. Transport Agency (PRTA), submitted a bid for 4,000 metric tonnes of coal at Rs. 1,625 per metric tonne. PRTA's bid was accepted, and the acceptance of the tender was communicated to PRTA by e-mail.

The e-mail was received at PRTA's place of business at Chandauli (also spelt Chamauli), with its only other place of business at Varanasi — both in the State of Uttar Pradesh. The registered partnership deed of the petitioner, dated 7 July 2000, recorded Chandauli as its principal place of business.

Subsequently the acceptance was cancelled. The case for the respondents, as recorded in the judgment, was that "there was some other person whose bid for the same coal was slightly higher than the Petitioner, but due to some flaw in the computer or its programme or feeding of data the said bid could not be considered." The petitioner filed a writ petition in the Allahabad High Court under Article 226 of the Constitution.

2 · The objections taken by the respondents

Respondents' three objections
  • No cause of action in U.P. "No facts have been pleaded in the writ petition on the basis of which it can be said that any part of cause of action has arisen within the territory of U.P."
  • Ouster of writ jurisdiction. "The jurisdiction of this Court under Article 226 of the Constitution of India stands ousted in favour of the Jharkhand High Court under Clause 10.5 of the Tender Agreement, the relevant part of which reads that (any) dispute arising out of this scheme shall be subject to the jurisdiction of the Jharkhand High Court."
  • The contract was negotiated, concluded and to be performed in Jharkhand; the coal was to be lifted there.
Petitioner's case
  • The principal place of business of the petitioner is in district Chandauli (U.P.), as recorded in the registered partnership deed; the only other place of business is Varanasi, also in U.P.
  • The acceptance of the tender communicated by e-mail is deemed to be received at the petitioner's place of business in U.P., and therefore part of the cause of action arose within the Court's territorial jurisdiction.
  • In an action based on breach of contract, the place of the contract is one of the determinative factors in deciding territorial jurisdiction.
  • The ouster clause cannot exclude the jurisdiction of the High Court under Article 226.

3 · Issues

  • Where does an e-contract conclude when the acceptance is communicated electronically — at the sender's place or the addressee's place?
  • Did a part of the cause of action arise within the territorial jurisdiction of the Allahabad High Court?
  • Can a contractual ouster/exclusion clause oust the writ jurisdiction of a High Court under Article 226 of the Constitution?
  • What is the effect of s.13(3) of the Information Technology Act, 2000 on the place of contract?

4 · The statutory provision that decided the case

📘 s.13, Information Technology Act, 2000 — "Time and place of despatch and receipt of electronic record" (source: IT Act 2000, as amended by Act 10 of 2009)

(1) "Save as otherwise agreed to between the originator and the addressee, the despatch of an electronic record occurs when it enters a computer resource outside the control of the originator."

(2) "Save as otherwise agreed between the originator and the addressee, the time of receipt of an electronic record shall be determined as follows, namely:— (a) if the addressee has designated a computer resource for the purpose of receiving electronic records,— (i) receipt occurs at the time when the electronic record enters the designated computer resource; or (ii) if the electronic record is sent to a computer resource of the addressee that is not the designated computer resource, receipt occurs at the time when the electronic record is retrieved by the addressee; (b) if the addressee has not designated a computer resource along with specified timings, if any, receipt occurs when the electronic record enters the computer resource of the addressee."

(3) "Save as otherwise agreed to between the originator and the addressee, an electronic record is deemed to be despatched at the place where the originator has his place of business, and is deemed to be received at the place where the addressee has his place of business."

(4) "The provisions of sub-section (2) shall apply notwithstanding that the place where the computer resource is located may be different from the place where the electronic record is deemed to have been received under sub-section (3)."

(5) "For the purposes of this section,— (a) if the originator or the addressee has more than one place of business, the principal place of business, shall be the place of business; (b) if the originator or the addressee does not have a place of business, his usual place of residence shall be deemed to be the place of business; (c) 'usual place of residence', in relation to a body corporate, means the place where it is registered."

Why s.13(4) is the crucial sub-section for the internet: it expressly decouples the physical location of the server from the legal place of receipt. A server in Virginia does not make the e-mail "received" in Virginia. The deemed place is the addressee's place of business. This is the statutory answer to the dislocation problem described in §1.1, and it is the reason P.R. Transport Agency is in the syllabus at all.

5 · Decision and reasoning

Held
  1. Jurisdiction exists. "In view of the facts mentioned in the supplementary-affidavit, read with Information Technology Act, the acceptance having been received by the Petitioner at Chandauli/Varanasi, the contract became complete by receipt of such acceptance at Varanasi/Chandauli, both of which places are within the territorial jurisdiction of this Court. Therefore, a part of the cause of action having arisen in U.P., this Court has territorial jurisdiction to entertain the writ petition."
  2. The deeming rule applies. Section 13(3) IT Act deems the electronic record to be received at the place where the addressee has his place of business. Since the petitioner's principal place of business was Chandauli and its only other place of business Varanasi — both in U.P. — the acceptance was received in U.P.
  3. The ouster clause cannot oust Article 226. The jurisdiction of the High Court under Article 226 can be ousted only by a constitutional or a competent legislative provision; a private contractual clause cannot. The Court held that the ouster clause could oust the territorial jurisdiction only of civil courts, and not that of the High Court, provided the High Court otherwise had jurisdiction on account of a part of the cause of action having arisen within its territorial jurisdiction.
  4. The A.B.C. Laminart principle. Following A.B.C. Laminart (P) Ltd. v. A.P. Agencies, (1989) 1 SCC 234, an exclusion clause binds the parties as between themselves but cannot be used to defeat a jurisdiction that the law otherwise confers — in particular s.20(c) CPC.
  5. The IT angle on cancellation. The respondents' explanation that the higher bid failed on account of "some flaw in the computer or its programme or feeding of data" was noted — an early recognition that computer failure is a fact to be proved, not a conclusion.

6 · Principle evolved

⚖️ The P.R. Transport Agency principle

Where an offer or its acceptance is communicated by electronic means, the place of contract is fixed by s.13(3) of the IT Act, 2000: the record is deemed received at the addressee's place of business (and if there are several, at the principal place of business; if none, at the usual place of residence; for a body corporate, where it is registered). That deemed place is where the contract is concluded, and therefore where a part of the cause of action arises. A contractual ouster clause cannot defeat the jurisdiction that this rule confers — and, in particular, cannot oust the writ jurisdiction of a High Court under Article 226 of the Constitution.

7 · Why this case matters for Unit IX (and its cross-link to Unit VII)

The Unit IX point
  • It is the only Indian authority in the syllabus that gives a statutory rule for locating an online act.
  • It shows that the legislature can and does supply a situs rule for cyberspace when it chooses to — which is precisely what it has not done for trade mark and passing-off disputes. That contrast is the backbone of any answer on "applicability of traditional rules of jurisdiction on the internet".
  • It demonstrates that "wholly or in part" in s.20(c) CPC is satisfied by a single deemed place of receipt — one part is enough.
The cross-link to Unit VII (E-Contracts)
  • In Unit VII this case is the authority on where an e-contract is concluded; read it there with Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas & Co., AIR 1974 SC 899 (contract by telephone is complete where acceptance is communicated to the offeror).
  • The tension to notice: Kedia asks where acceptance was communicated to the offeror; s.13(3) IT Act asks where the addressee has his place of business. In most cases these coincide — but not always. Where they diverge, s.13(3) governs because it is a specific statutory deeming provision, and s.13(1) makes it subject to contrary agreement.
  • Note also that WWE (§8) applied Kedia rather than s.13(3), because the question there was not contract formation between the parties but where the plaintiff "carried on business".
💡 Real example to use

An Indian software vendor in Bengaluru accepts an order from a German buyer by e-mail. The e-mail is deemed received at the buyer's place of business in Germany (s.13(3)(b) — if the buyer has no place of business, the usual place of residence; if the buyer is a German company, the place where it is registered). The contract is therefore concluded in Germany, and a suit on it could be filed there — but a suit could equally be filed in Bengaluru where the vendor performs. Contrast Impresario (§10), where the court held that a booking made from Delhi for a service to be availed in Hyderabad did not conclude the contract in Delhi, because the service could not be availed except in Hyderabad.

7Case brief 3 — Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. v. Myspace Inc.

Case 3 of 7 · Primary source: printed in the DU case material, pp. 87–125

Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. (T-Series) v. Myspace Inc.

IA Nos. 15781/2008 & 3085/2009 in CS (OS) 2682/2008 (Del, single judge) — 29.07.2011 FAO (OS) 540/2011, C.M. APPL. 20174/2011, 13919 & 17996/2015 (Del, DB) — 23.12.2016 S. Ravindra Bhat & Deepa Sharma, JJ. Reserved 25.04.2016 · Pronounced 23.12.2016
Provenance note. The DU case material prints the Division Bench judgment of 23 December 2016 (FAO (OS) 540/2011), which contains the single Judge's impugned order in summary at paras 17 and 45. The syllabus entry cites the single Judge's order of 29 July 2011 in IA 15781/2008 and IA 3085/2009. Both are covered below. Where the syllabus and the printed text differ, the printed DB judgment is the primary source.

1 · Facts

The plaintiff, Super Cassettes Industries Limited (SCIL), popularly "T-Series", began in recorded audio and videocassette production and became one of India's largest music companies. It owns and controls copyright in more than 100,000 songs in various languages, and claims to acquire and produce cinematograph films, music videos (audio-visual songs), sound recordings, underlying musical works (melody) and literary works (lyrics), investing substantial sums and bearing the risks. Its business is the exploitation of those works by sale of audio/video records or by licensing their use, on multiple platforms including satellite television, radio, digital, the Internet and mobile platforms. Under the TPPL scheme ("T-Series Public Performance Licenses") it licenses restaurants, discotheques, TV and radio stations, malls, hotels and public performers. It also enters into licensing agreements with internet music and video sharing/streaming platforms and ISPs.

The defendant, Myspace Inc., is an Internet Service Provider claiming to be an "internet intermediary". It operates the social networking and entertainment website www.myspace.com, first launched in the United States, with global outreach. Its foray into India was in 2007. Crucially, "when a user accesses Myspace from here she/he is automatically redirected to the website's India-centric version. Here users can access music works, entertainment videos, images, cinematograph works etc. without paying any fee." It provides a free platform offering an interactive user-submitted network of friends, personal profiles, blogs, groups and photos, music, video and instant messaging services.

The registration asymmetry. "Mere browsing or accessing content on Myspace does not require registration. If a user chooses to upload content on the website, albeit for free, he/she is required to register with Myspace." User registration is governed by the Terms of Use Agreement and Privacy Policy, which oblige registrants to respect the intellectual property rights of others and prohibit uploading any material infringing copyright, patent, trademark, trade secret or other proprietary rights. If MySpace notices that uploaded content violates its user terms, the content can be removed or the membership terminated at its discretion.

The business model. In addition to acting as a conduit, MySpace provides "automated ancillary services, whereby a limited license is sought from the user to use, modify, delete from, add to, publicly perform, publicly display, reproduce and display the User Generated Content (UGC) so as to facilitate access to user-stored material so that it can be converted into a format which is compatible with the website." Advertising is its chief source of revenue; the advertisements are not marked directly against the UGCs but against licensed content through a service known as "Adwords", and are generated by an automated process which is term-centric and not content-centric.

3 · Issues

  • Whether a Delhi court had territorial jurisdiction over a foreign internet intermediary whose India-centric version was served to Indian users — i.e. whether the defendant "carried on business" in Delhi under s.20(a)/(b) CPC, or the cause of action arose there under s.20(c).
  • Whether MySpace is an "intermediary" within s.2(1)(w) of the IT Act, 2000.
  • Whether the safe harbour in s.79 is available to MySpace, and how s.79 is to be read with the proviso to s.81 and s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act, 1957.
  • What is the nature of interim relief that can be granted against an internet intermediary — and whether an injunction covering "future works" is permissible.
  • Whether s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act requires actual knowledge or only general awareness.

4 · Arguments of the parties

For the plaintiff, SCIL
  • MySpace modifies content wilfully and has placed advertisements to make profits; it therefore cannot seek protection under the safe harbour provisions.
  • By virtue of the overriding s.81 and its proviso, the safe harbour is of no avail; only the Copyright Act, specifically s.51(a), applies.
  • MySpace initiates the transmission, negating s.79(2)(b), by setting up a website enabling users to transmit information.
  • s.79(2)(c) is inapplicable because the due diligence required was not exercised.
  • MySpace contravened s.79(3)(a) and (b) when it aided and abetted copyright infringement.
  • The learned Single Judge correctly held that the appellant was barred from seeking relief under s.79(1) and that only the Copyright Act would apply to it; prima facie liability for secondary infringement under s.51(a)(ii) was made out.
  • Infringing material was being uploaded every moment, rendering it impossible for the plaintiff to pinpoint exactly which content was available — hence the supply of the entire catalogue.
  • The balance of convenience lies in SCIL's favour; a comprehensive injunction adequately protects its interests.
For the appellant, MySpace
  • MySpace is an internet service provider and an internet intermediary; it does not possess any actual or real knowledge about the content being uploaded, nor can it control the kind of content uploaded, except to the extent that it can remove content stored in its systems once it receives a notice detailing the content and its location (URL).
  • Once content is uploaded on the Internet, a unique address — the Uniform Resource Locator (URL) — is generated. Every time the content is shared, a fresh URL is created, and since the Internet is a vast space, countless URLs holding the same content could be created. Removal therefore becomes impossible without a notice specifying the content and its location.
  • By not registering for the Rights Management Tool, the plaintiff was acting illogically, placing the entire liability on MySpace to track each and every work and to buy licences. The content owner cannot disclaim its responsibility to monitor online content.
  • The relief granted by the Single Judge is vague and general and un-implementable: without a specific list of works, MySpace cannot go through millions of videos. The catalogue of SCIL's works ran into 10 volumes and it was difficult to discern which works actually featured on its website.
  • No injunction could be granted in respect of future works — "there can be no copyright in respect of works that are not even in existence."
  • The only way to implement the order would be to completely shut down its India-centric business, causing irreparable damage at a prima facie stage.
  • Its modifications (by inserting advertisements) are automatic and part of the core system, with the only purpose of making the content compatible with the webpage; it therefore complies with s.79(2)(b).
  • Its Terms of Use (Clause 10) expressly prohibit users from uploading infringing content, provide a Copyright Infringement Notice Form, and terminate the membership of repeat or substantial infringers.

5 · The Court's reasoning

(a) Jurisdiction — the territorial hook

The suit was filed in the Delhi High Court. The Court's own description of the facts supplies the jurisdictional basis without any separate discussion: SCIL is an Indian company carrying on business in India; MySpace's website, when accessed from India, is automatically redirected to an India-centric version; Indian users can access music works, entertainment videos, images and cinematograph works on it free of charge. On the WWE analysis (§8), that is a virtual presence in India through which transactions — here, the supply of a platform to Indian users — are concluded. The Court therefore had no occasion to decide the difficult Banyan Tree question; jurisdiction was not in issue before it. Note this carefully in the examination: My Space is not an authority on when a foreign website owner can be sued in India; it is an authority on what happens after he is.

(b) MySpace is an "intermediary" under s.2(1)(w)

"In this case MySpace, the appellant, facially falls within Section 2(1)(w) and qualifies as an intermediary/Internet service provider because it acts as a 'conduit'/portal for information where users can upload and view content. It acts as a service provider by allowing users to upload, stream, share and view content which it hosts." What MySpace "seeks to achieve is create a platform — without adding or contributing any information on its own, where users can freely exchange data in the form of songs, videos, documentaries etc. By this model all content is uploaded by users and qualifies as 'User Generated Content' (UGC) and all that MySpace does is provides a neutral platform."

(c) Reading s.79 with the proviso to s.81 and s.51(a)(ii) — the harmonising exercise

This is the analytical heart of the judgment. Section 79 begins "Notwithstanding anything contained in any law for the time being in force but subject to the provisions of sub-sections (2) and (3)". As the Court put it, "the only restriction to be placed on the application of Section 79(1) is contained within the section: Section 79(2) and Section 79(3). This means that irrespective of any other law, an intermediary is guaranteed a safe harbor and the only restriction comes from Section 79(2) and (3)."

Against that stands s.81: "The provisions of this Act shall have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith contained in any other law for the time being in force. Provided that nothing contained in this Act shall restrict any person from exercising any right conferred under the Copyright Act, 1957 (14 of 1957) or the Patents Act, 1970 (39 of 1970)." The amendment to s.79 came into effect on 27.10.2009, and through the same amendment a proviso was added to s.81. "What prompted Parliament to amend Section 79 still remains a speculation but the deliberate act of adding the proviso to Section 81 could only lead one to assume that the Parliament intended to not disturb the rights of copyright or patent holders in light of the 2008 amended provisions to the IT Act." The Court also relied on Rule 3(2)(d) of the Intermediary Guidelines Rules, 2011 and on the Parliamentary Standing Committee report, while cautioning that such reports "cannot be the absolute basis for interpreting statutes".

The Court then performed the harmonising construction:

"In this Court opinion, Section 79 grants a measured privilege to an intermediary. However, that would not mean that the rights guaranteed under the Copyright Act are in any manner curtailed. All Section 79 does is regulates the liability in respect of intermediaries while the Copyright Act grants and controls rights of a copyright owner. Under the circumstances, it is difficult to conceive how one would pose a barrier in the applicability of the other. ... Section 79 is neither an enforcement provision nor does it list out any penal consequences for non-compliance. It sets up a scheme where intermediaries have to follow certain minimum standards to avoid liability; it provides for an affirmative defence and not a blanket immunity from liability."Paras 47–48

And the crucial comparative point: "To avoid the consequence of a width and sweep of Section 79, resulting in blanket immunity to intermediaries, Parliament deemed it essential to state that copyright owners could still pursue legal remedies against such intermediaries in specified circumstances. To put it differently, but for the proviso (to Section 81), copyright owners would have been unable to pursue legal recourse against Internet intermediaries." Under the current regime, "while private copyright owners can still demand action against intermediaries who may themselves post infringing content, intermediaries can seek safe harbor where the content is uploaded by third party users or is user generated."

On the knowledge standard: "while Section 51 of the Copyright Act provides for a system of 'notice', Section 79(3) contemplates 'receiving actual knowledge' or through notification by the government or its agency. The scope was widened in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, (2015) 5 SCC 1, where actual knowledge was held to mean a Court order in cases relatable to Article 19 of the Constitution of India. In case of Section 51(a)(ii), the only exception is that MySpace was not aware or had no reasonable grounds to believe that the content was infringing. Section 79(3) perhaps is more mindful of the way the internet functions and supplemented knowledge with the term 'actual knowledge'."

(d) The nature of the relief — the OECD four steps

The Court observed that "the rapid growth of technology calls for new legislative and judicial approaches" and adopted the four-step mechanism identified by the OECD in its draft report "The role of intermediaries in Advancing Public Policy Objective" dated 29 September 2010: (i) notice and take down; (ii) notice and notice (the intermediary passes the notice on to the infringing subscriber); (iii) notice and disconnection or the "three strikes" model evolved by the French government; and (iv) filtering. It referred to EMI v. UPC, [2009] No. 5472 P (Irish High Court, suggesting tools such as DtecNet or Copysense) and to YouTube's Content ID, "which neither blocks it or removes infringing content, but tracks or monetize the video content".

The Court also identified the real harms in the single Judge's order: an unspecified or incomplete list "may do that" — chilling free speech; and an intermediary, to avoid contempt, "would remove all such content, which even remotely resembles that of the content owner. Such kind of unwarranted private censorship would go beyond the ethos of established free speech regimes." It invoked Roadshow Films (incomplete notices by the content owner invite the ISP the legal risk of wrongfully terminating a user account) and the US Supreme Court's phrase in Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union, 521 US 244, as to "burn the house to roast the pig". The Court had to tread "a delicate balance between the Scylla of over protection (of intellectual property and privileging it in an overbearing manner) and the Charybdis of ineffective or under-protection, of IP rights: both of which chill and kill creativity".

6 · Decision

The three conclusions (para 67)
  1. "Sections 79 and 81 of the IT Act and Section 51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act have to be read harmoniously. Accordingly, it is held that proviso to Section 81 does not preclude the affirmative defence of safe harbor for an intermediary in case of copyright actions."
  2. "Section 51(a)(ii), in the case of internet intermediaries contemplates actual knowledge and not general awareness. Additionally, to impose liability on an intermediary, conditions under Section 79 of the IT Act have to be fulfilled."
  3. "In case of Internet intermediaries, interim relief has to be specific and must point to the actual content, which is being infringed."
⚖️ The relief substituted for the impugned order
  1. SCIL shall provide an updated catalogue of "specific" works in which it holds copyright, along with the location/URL of such work on MySpace's website, as and when it detects infringement.
  2. On receiving such notice, MySpace shall, within 36 hours, remove or block access to such content, in accordance with Rule 3(4) of the Intermediary Guidelines Rules, 2011.
  3. MySpace shall also keep an account of all such content removed pursuant to such requests, and of other details such as the number of viewings (wherever mechanisms exist to track them) till removal, and the advertisement revenue earned from it (proportionately), to enable the calculation of damages at trial.

The appeal was allowed in these terms; the Court directed that every endeavour be made to complete the hearing by the end of 2017 and to dispose of the suit finally by December 2017.

7 · Principle evolved

⚖️ The My Space principle

(a) Intermediary status is a threshold question, easily satisfied. A social networking or video-sharing platform that hosts user-generated content is an "intermediary" under s.2(1)(w) IT Act even though it earns advertising revenue and takes a limited licence to reformat user content for compatibility.

(b) s.79 is an affirmative defence, not a blanket immunity. It must be read harmoniously with the proviso to s.81 and with s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act. But for the proviso, copyright owners would have had no recourse at all against intermediaries.

(c) The knowledge standard is "actual knowledge". Section 51(a)(ii) requires actual knowledge, not general awareness, in the case of internet intermediaries — and the conditions in s.79(2) and (3) must additionally be satisfied before liability can be imposed.

(d) Interim relief must be specific and workable. An injunction that requires an intermediary to identify infringing works from millions of user uploads without a specific list is "not only impractical but ... dangerous": it drives private censorship, chills free speech, breaches the intermediary's contracts with its users, and exposes the intermediary to contempt for non-compliance with an impossible order. The correct relief is notice with specific works and URLs, and a take-down within 36 hours.

That is a targeting argument in substance — the intermediary has itself selected India as a market.
  • It demonstrates the remedial consequence of jurisdiction: once jurisdiction exists, the court must tailor relief to the technology. This is where s.151 CPC does its work.

8Case brief 4 — World Wrestling Entertainment, Inc. v. M/s Reshma Collection

Case 4 of 7 · Primary source: printed in the DU case material, pp. 181–194

World Wrestling Entertainment, Inc. v. M/s Reshma Collection & Ors.

FAO (OS) 506/2013 in C.M. Nos. 17627/2013 & 18606/2013 Delhi High Court, Division Bench Decided 15 October 2014 Badar Durrez Ahmed, J. Also reported: 2014 SCC OnLine Del 2031

1 · Facts

The appellant/plaintiff, World Wrestling Entertainment, Inc., is a company incorporated under the laws of the State of Delaware, United States of America. All the defendants reside in Mumbai and do not carry on business within the jurisdiction of the Delhi court. The suit claimed permanent injunction based on alleged infringement of copyright, infringement of trade marks, passing off, dilution, rendition of accounts, damages and delivery up.

The plaintiff is engaged in the development, production and marketing of television programming, pay-per-view programming, live events, and the licensing and sale of branded consumer products featuring the World Wrestling Entertainment (WWE) brand. The WWE scratch logo and "World Wrestling Entertainment" are registered trade marks worldwide including India; the plaintiff is also the owner of the copyright in the pictorial representations of World Wrestling Entertainment, the WWE wrestlers and the WWE scratch logo as they appear on publicity materials and promotional merchandise; and it holds trade mark registrations for talent names and slogans such as JOHN CENA, BATISTA, UNDERTAKER, TRIPLE H and RANDY ORTON. It offers memorabilia — apparel, posters, calendars, fact books, sunglasses, water bottles, key-rings, DVDs — and has more than 200 licensees distributing its products in 86 countries, including India.

2 · The jurisdictional plea — paragraph 32 of the plaint

"32. It is submitted that this Hon'ble Court has territorial jurisdiction to entertain and try the present suit under Section 134 (2) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 and Section 62 (2) of the Copyright Act, 1957 on account of the fact the Plaintiff carries on business within the territorial limits of this Hon'ble court as briefly summarized below:
i. The Plaintiff's programmes, consisting of its various characters including John Cena, Undertaker, Triple H, Randy Orton and Batista are broadcast at Delhi, within the territorial limits of this Hon'ble Court;
ii. The Plaintiff's products, such as its merchandising goods and books, are available within the territorial limits of this Hon'ble Court;
iii. The Plaintiff's goods and services are sold to consumers in Delhi through its websites which can be accessed and operated from all over the country, including from Delhi."Para 32 of the plaint, quoted in the judgment

Note the structure: the plaintiff relies on s.134(2) TMA and s.62(2) Copyright Act — the special plaintiff-side forums — and, within them, on its own carrying on of business in Delhi, not on the defendants' activities. That choice is what ultimately limits the decision, and it is the point Big Tree (§9) exploits.

3 · The impugned order and the single Judge's reasoning

The learned Single Judge, by order dated 4 October 2013 in CS (OS) 1801/2013, directed that the plaint be returned under Order VII Rule 10 CPC. He placed reliance on Dhodha House (Patel Roadways Ltd. v. Prasad Trading Co., (1991) 4 SCC 270) and on this Court's decision in Archie Comic Publications, Inc. v. Purple Creations Pvt. Ltd. He also relied on the Supreme Court's decision in Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas & Co., feeling that "the introduction of the new business models of doing business over the internet was not any different from the old model of doing business over the telephone or fax."

4 · Issues

  • Whether, on the averments in the plaint, the plaintiff — a foreign company with no office in Delhi — "carries on business" in Delhi within the meaning of s.134(2) TMA and s.62(2) Copyright Act.
  • Whether the Dhodha House three conditions are satisfied.
  • Where does a contract concluded over the internet take place — and does that place the "essential part of the business" there?
  • Whether the Kedia rule for telephone contracts applies to internet contracts.
  • Whether the plaint ought to have been returned under Order VII Rule 10 CPC on a demurrer.

5 · Arguments of the parties

For the appellant/plaintiff (Mr. Praveen Anand)
  • The use of the word "include" in s.134(2) TMA and s.62(2) Copyright Act makes those provisions broader than s.20 CPC (Exphar SA v. Eupharma Laboratories Ltd., AIR 2004 SC 1682).
  • "Carries on business" is not confined to the head office; it can include a branch office (Wipro Ltd., Madras High Court).
  • Indeed no office at all is needed: the plain meaning of the expression conveys that "wherever there is a business activity — be it the principal place or branch or branches — the party concerned is said to carry on business at such places." In Dhodha House itself the Supreme Court observed that "for the purpose of carrying on business, the presence of the person concerned at a particular place was not at all necessary and that such business could be carried on at that place through an agent or a manager or through a servant."
  • Dhodha House and Archie Comic did not consider web-based business models — the "new media" — and are therefore distinguishable.
  • At the threshold stage, and particularly on an application for return of a plaint under Order VII Rule 10 CPC, only the averments in the plaint should be seen; if, on a demurrer, a case for territorial jurisdiction is made out, the plaint ought not to be returned.
For the respondents
  • The plaintiff has no office in Delhi — not even a branch office. Its registered office and principal place of business are in the United States.
  • There is no agent, manager or servant of the plaintiff in Delhi. The first two Dhodha House conditions therefore fail.
  • Business over the internet is not different in principle from business over the telephone or fax; Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia governs the place of contract and does not assist the plaintiff.
  • The defendants are all residents of Mumbai and carry on no business in Delhi; a suit between two sets of non-Delhi parties in a Delhi court is inconvenient.

6 · The Court's reasoning

(a) The Dhodha House three conditions

The Division Bench accepted that "the expression 'carries on business', as appearing in the above two provisions, is much wider than what the expression in normal parlance connotes", but recalled the Supreme Court's caution that "although the expression has a very wide meaning, it is necessary that the three conditions specified in the said decision should be satisfied":

  1. the person acting as agent must be a special agent who attends exclusively to the business of the principal and carries it on in the name of the principal, and not a general agent who does business for anyone who pays him;
  2. the person acting as agent must be an agent in the strict sense of the term (a manager of a Joint Hindu Family is not an "agent");
  3. to constitute "carrying on business" at a certain place, the essential part of the business must be performed at that place. The illustration given is that of "a Bombay firm has a branch office at Amritsar, where orders are received, subject to confirmation by the head office at Bombay, and where money is paid and disbursed. Such a firm is said to be carrying on business at Amritsar and is liable to be sued at Amritsar."

Applying them: "Inasmuch as it is an admitted position that the appellant/plaintiff has no agent in Delhi, those two conditions are clearly not fulfilled." The whole case therefore rested on the third condition.

(b) Where does an internet contract conclude? — Kedia applied

The Division Bench held that the Single Judge "had made a correct reference to the decision of the Supreme Court in the case of Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia", but that "the full ramifications of that decision were not perceived by him." In Kedia an oral contract to supply cotton seed cake had been negotiated by long-distance telephone; the offer emanated from Ahmedabad and the acceptance was communicated instantaneously to the offeror at Ahmedabad. The Supreme Court held that "a part of the cause of action arose within the jurisdiction of the Civil City Court, Ahmedabad, where acceptance was communicated by telephone to the plaintiffs." The rule is that where the parties are not in each other's presence, acceptance by post or telegram is complete when it is put into a course of transmission; but where communication is instantaneous — telephone, and now the internet — "the rule that applies to contracts concluded over the telephone would apply with equal vigour to contracts concluded over the internet. In other words, contracts would be completed at the place where the acceptance is communicated."

(c) The website is an invitation to offer
"The website of the appellant/plaintiff refers to various goods and services. It is not an offer but an invitation to an offer, just as a menu in a restaurant. The invitation, if accepted by a customer in Delhi, becomes an offer made by the customer in Delhi for purchasing the goods 'advertised' on the website of the appellant/plaintiff. When, through the mode of the software and the browser, the transaction is confirmed and payment is made to the appellant/plaintiff through its website, the appellant/plaintiff accepts the offer of the customer at Delhi. Since the transaction between the two takes place instantaneously, the acceptance by the appellant/plaintiff is instantaneously communicated to its customer through the internet at Delhi. Therefore, in such a case, part of the cause of action would arise in Delhi."Para 23 (numbered 21 in the judgment)
(d) The "virtual shop" reasoning
"But, we are not concerned with the question of cause of action between the appellant/plaintiff and its customers in Delhi because the defendants are not such customers and they are, in any event, all residents of Mumbai. What we are examining is whether the third condition specified in Dhodha House is satisfied or not. In other words, if the contracts and/or transactions entered into between the appellant/plaintiff on the one hand and its customers are being concluded in Delhi, can it not be said that the essential part of the business of the appellant/plaintiff, insofar as its transactions with customers in Delhi are concerned, takes place in Delhi? The offers are made by customers at Delhi. The offers are subject to confirmation/acceptance of the appellant/plaintiff through its website. The money would emanate or be paid from Delhi. Can it not then be considered that the appellant/plaintiff is, to a certain extent, carrying on business at Delhi? In our view, it would be so."Para 21 (numbered 24 in the judgment)
"Because of the advancements in technology and the rapid growth of new models of conducting business over the internet, it is possible for an entity to have a virtual presence in a place which is located at a distance from the place where it has a physical presence. The availability of transactions through the website at a particular place is virtually the same thing as a seller having shops in that place in the physical world. Let us assume for the sake of argument that the appellant/plaintiff had a shop in Delhi from where it sold its various goods and services. In that case, it could not be denied that the plaintiff carried on business in Delhi. ... When the shop in the 'physical sense' is replaced by the 'virtual' shop because of the advancement of technology, in our view, it cannot be said that the appellant/plaintiff would not carry on business in Delhi."Para 22

7 · Decision

Held
  1. "In our view, although the learned Single Judge had made a correct reference to the decision of the Supreme Court in the case of Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia, the full ramifications of that decision were not perceived by him. When the two decisions of the Supreme Court in Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia and Dhodha House are considered in the manner indicated above, it would appear that, on the averments made by the appellant/plaintiff in the plaint, the Delhi High Court would, on a demurrer, have jurisdiction to entertain the suit inasmuch as the appellant/plaintiff would be regarded as carrying on business in Delhi within the meaning of the expression under Section 134(2) of the Trademarks Act, 1999 and Section 62(2) of the Copyright Act, 1957."
  2. "Consequently, the learned Single Judge ought not to have returned the plaint under Order 7 Rule 10 CPC. As a result, the impugned order is set aside and the suit is restored to its original number."
  3. "This, however, would not preclude the defendants from raising the plea of jurisdiction on facts which, if raised, could be considered by the court based on the evidence and upon the law explained above."
  4. The appeal was allowed on these terms; the parties were left to bear their own costs.

8 · Principle evolved

⚖️ The WWE principle

(a) The "virtual shop". A website through which transactions are concluded with customers in a place gives the operator a virtual presence there. "The availability of transactions through the website at a particular place is virtually the same thing as a seller having shops in that place in the physical world."

(b) The essential part of the business. Where the offers are made by customers in the forum, are subject to confirmation through the website, and the money emanates from the forum, "the essential part of the business ... insofar as its transactions with customers in [the forum] are concerned, takes place in [the forum]" — the third Dhodha House condition is satisfied.

(c) Kedia governs internet contracts. An internet transaction is instantaneous; the contract is complete where the acceptance is communicated; part of the cause of action therefore arises at the customer's end. A website display is an invitation to offer, "just as a menu in a restaurant".

(d) The standard of proof at the threshold is low. On an application under Order VII Rule 10 CPC, only the averments in the plaint are examined. If a case for territorial jurisdiction is made out on a demurrer, the plaint must not be returned. The defendant may renew the plea later on evidence.

9 · The limits of WWE — and the two DU self-learning exercises that test them

🔑 Limit 1 — the plaintiff was carrying on business in Delhi; Banyan Tree had not yet been applied

The WWE plaintiff pleaded that it carried on business in Delhi and relied on its own forum. Banyan Tree (23.11.2009) had already laid down the purposeful-availment test, but the WWE Division Bench did not apply it, because the question before it was different: not "can a foreign website owner be sued here?" but "does a foreign plaintiff carry on business here?". Keep the two questions apart.

🔑 Limit 2 — the Explanation to s.20 CPC: a company needs an office. This is what Big Tree holds.

The Explanation provides that "a corporation shall be deemed to carry on business at its sole or principal office in India or, in respect of any cause of action arising at any place where it has also a subordinate office, at such place." Read with Patel Roadways and Sanjay Dalia, a company cannot be said to carry on business at a place where it has no office at all. The WWE plaintiff had no office in Delhi either — which is precisely why Big Tree held that WWE does not extend to that situation. See §9.

9Case brief 5 — Big Tree Entertainment v. Saturday Sunday Media Internet

Case 5 of 7 · Listed in the DU syllabus · NOT printed in the DU case material

Bigtree Entertainment Pvt. Ltd. v. Saturday Sunday Media Internet & Ors.

CS (COMM) Nos. 53/2015 & 54/2015 Delhi High Court Decided 21 December 2015 Valmiki J. Mehta, J. (oral judgment) Also reported: 226 (2016) DLT 497; MANU/DE/4188/2015; 2015 SCC OnLine Del 14217
Provenance note. Listed in the DU syllabus but not reproduced in the case material. The facts, the pleaded paragraph, the submissions and the holding set out below are taken from the reported decision (CS (COMM) 53/2015 and 54/2015, 21.12.2015). The DU material does, however, print the second self-learning exercise after World Wrestling Entertainment, which expressly directs the reader to this case: "Does Delhi Court has jurisdiction on the ground of 'plaintiff carrying on business', where plaintiff is a company and its interactive website is accessible in Delhi, though cause of action arose in Mumbai where the company had branch office. (Hint — Big Tree Entertainment Case)". That exercise is answered at §15, Exercise 7.

1 · Facts

Two suits were filed by the same plaintiff:

  • CS (COMM) No. 53/2015 against Saturday Sunday Media Internet & Ors., whose address in the memo of parties was at Gurgaon, Haryana — outside Delhi;
  • CS (COMM) No. 54/2015 against M/s iPlatform Media Technologies Pvt. Ltd. and Anr., whose address was at Bengaluru, Karnataka — outside Delhi.

The plaintiff, Bigtree Entertainment Pvt. Ltd. — the operator of the online movie and entertainment ticketing platform "BOOKMYSHOW.COM" — is a company registered under the Companies Act, 1956, with its address as per the memo of parties at Mumbai. As the Court recorded, "it has no office in Delhi be it a principal office or a head office or a registered office or even a branch office."

Both suits sought permanent injunction restraining the infringement of trade mark, passing off and damages. In CS (COMM) 53/2015 the impugned mark was "BOOKMYOFFER.COM"; in CS (COMM) 54/2015 it was "BOOKMERASHOW.COM".

2 · The jurisdictional plea — paragraph 34 of the plaint

"34. This Hon'ble Court also has jurisdiction to entertain and try the present suit under section 134(2) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 on account of the fact that the Plaintiff carries on business within the jurisdiction of the Hon'ble Court.

The Plaintiff's ticketing services are availed by consumers in Delhi, for events, performances and shows held in Delhi. Contracts and/or transactions are entered into between the Plaintiff and its customers, which are being concluded in Delhi. Therefore the essential part of the business of the Plaintiff, insofar as its transactions with customers in Delhi are concerned, takes place in Delhi. Therefore this Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction to entertain the present suit.

It is submitted that the subject matter of the suit relates to the infringement and passing off of the Plaintiff's trademark and is a 'commercial dispute' as defined in section 2(1)(c)(xvii) of the Commercial Courts, Commercial Division and Commercial Appellate Division of High Court Ordinance, 2015.

The plaintiff has not filed any other similar suit or any other proceedings against the Defendants either before this Hon'ble Court or any other Court."Para 34 of the plaint, quoted in the judgment

Observe that the plea is a direct application of WWE — the plaintiff pleads its own carrying on of business in Delhi through internet transactions with Delhi customers, and not the defendants' activities in Delhi.

3 · The Court's intervention before dictation — a crucial moment

🔑 What the Court put to counsel, and why it matters

"Before the commencement of dictation of this judgment, and even right at the inception, I put it to the counsel for the plaintiff that it is advisable, of course subject to discretion of the plaintiff, that plaintiff can amend the suit so that the territorial jurisdiction is claimed on the ground of arising of part of cause of action in Delhi not on account of Section 134 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 of 'carrying on business' by the plaintiff but because the defendants are carrying on their businesses at Delhi under the impugned trade marks 'BOOKMYOFFER.COM' and 'BOOKMERASHOW.COM' ... and as a result of which impugned activities of the defendants would be at Delhi, and hence this Court thus would have territorial jurisdiction with reference to the cause of action of the activities of the defendants and not because of the plaintiff's 'carrying on business' in Delhi. Learned counsel for the plaintiff however states that plaintiff does not seek to so amend the suit plaint and this Court can proceed ahead to decide the issue of territorial jurisdiction and places reliance upon the judgment passed by a Division Bench of this Court in the case of World Wrestling Entertainment v. Reshma Collection, 2014 SCC OnLine Del 2031 and paras 17, 18 and 24 (wrongly numbered as 21 in the judgment) thereof."

This is the most instructive moment in the case. The Court identified, at the outset, the route that would have worked — a s.20(c) plea founded on the defendants' activities in Delhi. The plaintiff declined to amend, and the plaints were returned. In the examination, note this as a practical lesson: plead both routes.

4 · Issues

  • Whether the expression "carrying on business" in s.134(2) TMA (and s.62(2) Copyright Act) can be satisfied by a company that has no office of any kind in the forum, merely because its website is accessible there and transactions with forum customers are concluded there.
  • Whether the Explanation to s.20 CPC applies to the plaintiff as well as the defendant.
  • Whether WWE governs this situation.
  • Whether the court should transfer the suits to a more convenient forum.

5 · Arguments of the parties

For the plaintiff
  • The Division Bench in WWE held that where internet transactions take place, there would be territorial jurisdiction at the place where the internet transaction is done by accessing the website.
  • In WWE the plaintiff was a foreign company with no office in Delhi, yet jurisdiction was upheld. That reasoning applies here.
  • WWE did not decide the case of a company plaintiff with no office, but its principle — the "virtual shop" — does.
  • The plaintiff's ticketing services are availed by consumers in Delhi; contracts and transactions are concluded in Delhi; the essential part of the business takes place in Delhi.
  • The plaintiff chose not to amend the plaint, and asked the Court to decide the issue on the WWE plea alone.
The Court's analysis (defendants' objection accepted)
  • "The Division Bench in the judgment in the case of World Wrestling Entertainment does not deal with the situation qua internet transactions as to when the plaintiff is a corporation viz a company registered under the Companies Act, 1956 and whether there is 'carrying on business' even if the plaintiff company has no office at the place where the plaintiff's website is accessed for carrying out the transactions."
  • "In case plaintiff is a company ... a plaintiff/company can only carry on business only if its principal or registered or head office or a branch office is also and necessarily situated at the place where cause of action arises."
  • "In law unless a company has an office at a place, it cannot be said to be carrying on business at that particular place. This peculiarity takes place because of the Explanation to Section 20 CPC."
  • A suit could alternatively be filed "on the different ground that it is the impugned activities of the defendants which are taking place at Delhi and therefore part of cause of action arises at Delhi" — but the plaintiff did not so plead.

6 · The authorities relied on

AuthorityProposition used
Patel Roadways Ltd., Bombay v. Prasad Trading Co., (1991) 4 SCC 270, paras 9 and 12The Explanation to s.20 CPC "is really an explanation to Clause (a)": it clarifies where a corporation can be said to carry on business — at the place of its sole or principal office (whether or not business is actually carried on there), or at a place where a cause of action arises only if it has a subordinate office there. "Carrying on business by a company can only be alongwith existence of a head office or a registered office or a principal office or a branch office at the place where the cause of action arises."
Indian Performing Rights Society Ltd. v. Sanjay Dalia & Anr., (2015) 10 SCC 161, paras 14, 15, 16, 18, 19The meaning given to "carries on business" in s.20(a) CPC as regards a defendant applies equally to a plaintiff. The additional forum under s.62(2) Copyright Act and s.134(2) TMA is for the plaintiff's convenience, but it is subject to restrictions: where the plaintiff is carrying on business at a place and the cause of action has also arisen there, it cannot ignore that place and sue at a distant place where it has only a subordinate office. "Ordinarily the suit has to be filed at the place where there is principal place of business of the corporation."

7 · Decision

Held
  1. No jurisdiction. A conjoint reading of Patel Roadways and Sanjay Dalia "shows that wherever a plaintiff or a defendant is a corporation, carrying on business by such a corporation or company necessarily has to be taken alongwith existence of a branch office or a principal office or a registered office, with the place where the cause of action also is said to have arisen." The plaintiff had no office in Delhi; therefore it did not carry on business in Delhi; s.134(2) TMA could not be invoked.
  2. WWE distinguished. The Division Bench's judgment "does not deal with and pronounce upon the issue as to Delhi having jurisdiction although the plaintiff is a company and such a plaintiff/Company neither has a principal office nor a head office nor a registered office nor a branch office at Delhi."
  3. The alternative route was available but not pleaded. "Of course, I must hasten to add that if the subject suit or any other civil suit is filed not on the ground of residence i.e. carrying on of business of the plaintiff company at a particular place such as Delhi where the present suit is filed, but on the different ground that it is the impugned activities of the defendants which are taking place at Delhi and therefore part of cause of action arises at Delhi, then, in such a case Delhi could have territorial jurisdiction."
  4. Forum conveniens. "I find it very peculiar for the plaintiff/Company in the present suit which has its office at Mumbai i.e not at Delhi, that such a plaintiff/Company is suing defendants not by filing a suit in Mumbai and that it is suing defendants who are residing and/or carrying on business not at Delhi but at Bengaluru in Karnataka or Gurgaon in Haryana. Putting it in other words, there is lack of convenience as regards the place of suing, both to the plaintiff and the defendants." Sections 22 and 23 CPC permit transfer to a more convenient forum.
  5. Order. The plaints were returned to the plaintiff under Order VII Rule 10 CPC, to be presented before a court of competent jurisdiction.

8 · Principle evolved

⚖️ The Big Tree principle

(a) A company carries on business only where it has an office. Read with the Explanation to s.20 CPC, a corporation cannot be said to carry on business at a place where it has no principal, registered, head or branch office. The accessibility of its website in that place, and the conclusion of transactions with customers there, do not supply the missing office.

(b) The Explanation applies to plaintiffs too. The restrictive meaning of "carries on business" adopted in Patel Roadways for a defendant corporation applies equally to a plaintiff corporation invoking the special forums in s.134(2) TMA and s.62(2) Copyright Act (Sanjay Dalia).

(c) WWE is confined to its own facts. The "virtual shop" reasoning in WWE does not decide the case of a company plaintiff with no office in the forum.

(d) Two routes, and you must plead the right one. Jurisdiction can be founded either on the plaintiff's carrying on of business (which requires an office) or on the defendant's activities giving rise to a part of the cause of action in the forum. A plaintiff who pleads only the first, without an office, will have its plaint returned.

10Case brief 6 — Impresario Entertainment & Hospitality Pvt. Ltd. v. S & D Hospitality

Case 6 of 7 · Listed in the DU syllabus · NOT printed in the DU case material

Impresario Entertainment & Hospitality Pvt. Ltd. v. S & D Hospitality

CS (COMM) 111/2017 · I.A. Nos. 1950/2017 & 3139/2017 Delhi High Court Reserved 9 November 2017 · Decided 3 January 2018 Mukta Gupta, J. Also reported: 2018 (73) PTC 275 (Del); 246 (2018) DLT 337; 2018 3 AD (Delhi) 102; neutral citation 2018:DHC:14
Provenance note. Listed in the DU syllabus and expressly flagged in the self-learning exercise printed after Banyan Tree in the case material ("Hint — Impresario Entertainment Case"), but not reproduced in it. The facts, the pleaded paragraphs and the holding below are taken from the reported decision (CS (COMM) 111/2017, 3.1.2018). This is the most recent of the seven cases and the direct application of Banyan Tree.

1 · Facts

The plaintiff, Impresario Entertainment & Hospitality Pvt. Ltd., is a company incorporated under the Companies Act, 1956, with its registered office in Mumbai, carrying on business in Delhi from 12, Hauz Kaz Village, New Delhi-110016, and operating a restaurant under the name and style of "SOCIAL". It is engaged in providing restaurant services and operating/managing coffee shops. Some of its well-known restaurants and coffee shops include "SOCIAL", "SMOKE" (Pune) and others. It got the trade mark "STONE WATER GRILL" registered under Classes 30, 42 and 43 in November 2007, and claims to be the registered proprietor of the trade mark "SOCIAL" and its variants since May 2014. It also uses the beverage mark "A GAME OF SLING".

The defendant, S & D Hospitality, was operating two restaurants in Hyderabad under the trade mark "SOCIAL MONKEY" — and, on the plaintiff's case, also "SOCIAL MONKEY'S" and "STONE WATERS KITCHEN & LOUNGE", and a beverage called "HYDERABAD SLING" closely resembling the plaintiff's "A GAME OF SLING".

In January 2017 the plaintiff came to know that the defendant was operating the two Hyderabad restaurants. Its case was that the defendant "has emphasized on the word SOCIAL and copied the concept of the plaintiff of prefixing/suffixing the trade mark 'SOCIAL' with another word being 'MONKEY' in this case." A cease and desist notice dated 10 March 2017 was issued; no response was received. The plaintiff filed a suit for permanent injunction restraining the defendant from manufacturing, selling, marketing, advertising or offering its services under the trade marks "SOCIAL", "SOCIAL MONKEY" or "STONE WATER", or any other deceptively similar mark, in its outlets.

2 · The jurisdictional pleas — paragraphs 61 and 64 of the plaint

"61. This Hon'ble Court has the territorial jurisdiction to entertain the present suit on account of the fact that a part of the cause of action has arisen in Delhi. In this regard, it is submitted that the Defendant advertises and promotes its services on www.Zomato.com, an interactive restaurant guide service through which reservations can be made from Delhi at the outlets of the Defendant and/or the Defendant can be contacted. This is done against a charge. Resultantly, through Zomato.com the Defendant is able to target customers in Delhi. In fact, from the reviews set out in paragraph 33 above, which are posted on Zomato.com it is apparent that the Defendant has successfully targeted parties in Delhi to its outlet, making them believe that the same is a part of the Plaintiff's 'SOCIAL' outlets. ... Additionally, Zomato has a telephone number listed on its website which is indicative that it has a base in Delhi, within the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court."Para 61 of the plaint
"64. Further, the Plaintiff carries on business in Delhi inter alia through its office at the address 12, Hauz Khas Village, New Delhi 110016 where the Plaintiff has a SOCIAL cafe and an office housing about two hundred and fifty personnel. The entire pan India franchise and licensing business and the non-food supply chain management of the Plaintiff for all its restaurants/bars is carried out [there]."Para 64 of the plaint

3 · Issues

  • Whether the defendant has its registered office, or carries on business, within the territorial jurisdiction of the Delhi High Court.
  • Whether the plaintiff's own carrying on of business in Delhi is sufficient, and whether the plaintiff's principal place of business is in Delhi or Mumbai.
  • Whether the presence of the defendant on Zomato.com and Dine-Out — interactive restaurant guides through which reservations can be made from Delhi — gives rise to a part of the cause of action in Delhi.
  • Whether the defendant's use of similar marks on those platforms, and the booking of a table by one Delhi customer, amounts to a "trap transaction" sufficient to found jurisdiction.
  • Whether the plaintiff's quia timet action discloses an imminent danger.

4 · Arguments of the parties

For the plaintiff
  • Its principal office for franchising and licensing of all its brands is in Delhi; it has no office or branch in Hyderabad.
  • It carries on business in Delhi through its office at 12, Hauz Khas Village, with about 250 personnel, and through its various outlets.
  • The defendant has entered into a contract with Zomato.com, a company having its office in Delhi, to promote its business throughout India. Zomato and Dine-Out are interactive restaurant guides through which reservations can be made at the outlets of the defendant from Delhi and the defendant can be contacted.
  • Through Zomato and Dine-Out the defendant targets customers in Delhi and invites them to visit its Hyderabad outlet; from the reviews posted on Zomato "it is apparent that the Defendant has successfully targeted parties in Delhi to its outlet, making them believe that the same is a part of the Plaintiff's 'SOCIAL' outlets."
  • Since the plaintiff has no "SOCIAL" outlet or "STONE WATER GRILL" in Hyderabad, "a large part of the Defendant's clientele would be customers who are aware of Plaintiff's outlets".
  • The defendant's phone number is displayed on the Zomato and DineOut website with an invitation to book a table; Zomato has a telephone number indicative of a base in Delhi.
  • The defendant intends to expand pan-India — as admitted in its LinkedIn profile — and has filed trade mark applications on an all-India basis.
  • At least one Delhi customer booked a table at the defendant's restaurant from Delhi, believing it to be a restaurant of the plaintiff.
For the defendant
  • It has neither its registered office within the jurisdiction of the Court nor carries on any business within it.
  • The plaintiff's registered office is in Mumbai, which is also outside the territorial jurisdiction of the Court; and the plaintiff has not pleaded how, despite Mumbai being its registered office, its principal office is at Delhi.
  • "Mere booking or placing an order through internet is insufficient to say that any transaction took place since the contract formation is not at the place of booking or ordering the product or service."
  • "Mere existence of a website without proof of 'the effect' does not clothe this Court with territorial jurisdiction to entertain the present suit."
  • The plaintiff's case that the defendant would "invite the customers of the plaintiff to visit its outlet in Hyderabad" through Zomato and Dine-Out is misconceived.
  • The plaintiff's quia timet action is bereft of the necessary ingredients: there is no imminent danger, the nature of the business being such that no party can open a restaurant in any city overnight without the necessary licences.

5 · The Court's reasoning

The Court set out, at length, the answers given by the Division Bench in Banyan Tree — quoting paras 38, 39, 40, 41, 42 and the answers to Questions (i) and (ii) — and then applied them. The core of its application was this:

"Even if the Defendant has been able to attract customers from other jurisdiction including by way of Zomato and Dine-Out, the services of Defendant cannot be availed unless the customers go to Hyderabad. Through Zomato and Dine Out the customers will only be able to invite a customer and reserve a table at the restaurant of the Defendant at Hyderabad. The commercial transaction will take place only on the customer availing the services of the Defendant at Hyderabad."Order under review

And on the trap booking:

"Claim of the plaintiff is that at least one customer through the website booked the table at defendant's restaurant from Delhi believing that it was a restaurant of the plaintiff. On the basis of a solitary transaction, through internet plaintiff cannot claim that cause of action having arisen at Delhi this Court will have territorial jurisdiction to entertain the suit. Facts pleaded in the plaint do not pass the tests laid down by the Division Bench of this Court in Banyan Tree Holding (P) Ltd. (supra)."

The Court also noted the plaintiff's failure to plead that its principal place of business was in Delhi — it pleaded only that it "carries on business" in Delhi. As the Court later put it in dismissing the review petition, "in para 61 and 64 though plaintiff claims to be carrying on business in Delhi, however there is no averment that the principal place of business of the plaintiff/review petitioner is at Delhi."

6 · Decision

Held
  1. No territorial jurisdiction. "In view of the discussion aforesaid since this Court has no territorial jurisdiction to entertain the suit, the same is returned to be filed in the Court of competent jurisdiction."
  2. The application under Order VII Rule 10 CPC (I.A. No. 3139/2017) was allowed and the plaint returned.
  3. The application under Order XXXIX Rules 1 and 2 CPC (I.A. No. 1950/2017) was not decided, since the court had no jurisdiction to entertain the suit.
  4. The Court recorded that the plaintiff, "prima facie having not shown the purposeful availment test", could not sustain the suit in Delhi.
  5. A review petition (Review Pet. 129/2018) was dismissed on 31 May 2023, the Court reiterating that "Merely by reserving a table in the defendant's restaurant at Hyderabad, the contract does not get concluded."

7 · Principle evolved

⚖️ The Impresario principle

(a) A platform listing is not enough. A defendant's presence on an interactive third-party platform (Zomato, Dine-Out) — even one whose company has an office in Delhi, and even where the listing displays a telephone number and invites bookings — does not by itself show that the defendant specifically targeted the forum State for commercial transactions.

(b) Where the service must be physically availed, the transaction is completed there. "Even if the defendant attracted or had been able to attract the customers from other jurisdictions by way of Zomato and Dine-out, the customers would still be required to go to Hyderabad to avail the services. The best that could be done by the customers of other jurisdictions ... was to book a table at defendant's restaurant which ultimately led the completion of transaction at Hyderabad where the cause of action would eventually lie." A reservation made in Delhi for a service to be availed in Hyderabad does not conclude the contract in Delhi.

(c) A solitary trap transaction cannot found jurisdiction. Applying Banyan Tree: "on the basis of a solitary transaction, through internet plaintiff cannot claim that cause of action having arisen at Delhi."

(d) Plead the principal place of business, not merely "carrying on business". Where the plaintiff is a company, a bare averment that it carries on business at a place, without an averment that its principal place of business is there, is a weakness — particularly where the registered office is elsewhere.

8 · Significance — this is the case that answers the DU self-learning exercise

💡 Why Impresario is the most examination-relevant of the three unprinted cases

The DU case material prints, immediately after Banyan Tree, this exercise: "Can [a] Delhi court assume jurisdiction if the defendant in [the] above case provided spa services in Hyderabad, and instead of booking orders directly, enters into a contract with an online platform X, to book orders in Delhi through its website for spa services in Hyderabad. The plaintiff has registered office in Tokyo. In other words, does cause of action arise in Delhi. (Hint — Impresario Entertainment Case)." Impresario is the hint — it is the answer. On these facts: the spa services can only be availed in Hyderabad; the platform X is a conduit through which Delhi customers make bookings; the booking does not conclude the contract in Delhi; the commercial transaction is completed in Hyderabad; a solitary booking is not purposeful availment; the plaintiff's registered office is in Tokyo and it carries on no business in Delhi; therefore the Delhi court has no territorial jurisdiction and the plaint must be returned under Order VII Rule 10 CPC. A full model answer is at §15, Exercise 2.

⚠️ The tension with Icon Health & Fitness v. Sheriff Usman — raise it for extra marks

In Icon Health & Fitness, Inc. v. Sheriff Usman & Anr. (Del, September 2017) the Delhi High Court held that the existence of a business on online portals was enough to establish the court's jurisdiction in a suit for passing off, observing that "though the defendants do not physically exist in Delhi, they are promoting their fitness apps and brands through App Store, Google Play Store, and e-commerce platforms like www.amazon, which can be accessed and used from all over the country, including Delhi. Thus, the defendants are conducting business or working for gain in Delhi." That decision sits uneasily with Impresario, which was decided a few months later and took the Banyan Tree line. The reconciliation offered by commentators is that Icon was an ex parte decision with no recrimination for a pro-claimant finding, whereas Impresario was thoroughly contested and produced a detailed 21-page decision. In an answer, note the divergence and state that Impresario, being later and reasoned, represents the settled position.

11Case brief 7 — Maqbool Fida Husain v. Raj Kumar Pandey

Case 7 of 7 · Primary source: printed in the DU case material, pp. 66–78

Maqbool Fida Husain v. Raj Kumar Pandey & Ors.

Criminal Revision Petition No. 114/2007 (also Crl. Rev. P. 280/2007) Delhi High Court Decided 8 May 2008 Sanjay Kishan Kaul, J.

1 · Facts

The petitioner, M.F. Husain, an accomplished and celebrated painter, made "a contemporary painting celebrating nudity". It "depicts India in an abstract and graphical representation of a woman in nude with her hair flowing in the form of Himalayas displaying her agony." The painting was sold to a private collector in 2004, after which the petitioner "did not deal with the same in any manner whatsoever".

Subsequently, in 2006, the painting — entitled "Bharat Mata" — "was advertised as part of an on-line auction for charity for Kashmir earthquake victims organised by a non-governmental organisation with which the petitioner claims to have no involvement." The petitioner stated that he had never given the painting its title. "The advertisement of the said painting led to large scale protests for which the petitioner also had to tender an apology."

Private complaints were then filed "at various parts of the country" — Pandharpur (Maharashtra), Rajkot (Gujarat), Indore and Bhopal (Madhya Pradesh) — alleging various offences, and summons and warrants of arrest were issued. The petitioner approached the Supreme Court seeking consolidation. The Supreme Court acceded and, by order dated 4 December 2006, directed that the complaint cases be consolidated and transferred to the court of the Ld. ACMM, Delhi by way of transfer petitions T.P.(Cri.) 129/2006, 182/2006 and 224/2006. The Ld. ACMM, Delhi issued summons to the petitioner for offences under ss.292, 294 and 298 of the Indian Penal Code, against which the present revision petitions were filed.

Because propositions of importance had arisen, the Court issued a court notice to the Learned Attorney General, who deputed a law officer to assist it. No one sought to appear for the respondents in Crl. Rev. P. 114/2007 and Crl. Rev. P. 280/2007; the Court closed their right to advance further submissions by order dated 20 March 2008. The GPA holder of the respondent in Crl. Rev. P. 280/2007 then entered appearance on 31 March 2008 and was permitted to make further submissions.

2 · Issues

  • Whether the painting is "obscene" within the meaning of s.292 IPC, and whether the offences under ss.294 and 298 IPC are made out.
  • Whether an artist may be prosecuted for a painting uploaded on a website accessible to people across the globe.
  • What is the true scope of the magistrate's duty under s.202 CrPC before issuing process, especially where the accused resides outside the magistrate's jurisdiction?
  • Which court has territorial jurisdiction to try complaints arising out of a single internet publication that offends readers in several States?
  • Whether the criminal jurisdiction of Indian courts over internet publications requires legislative circumscription.

3 · Arguments of the parties

For the petitioner, M.F. Husain
  • The painting was sold in 2004 and the petitioner had nothing to do with it thereafter; the 2006 online auction was organised by an NGO with which he had no involvement, and he never gave the painting its title.
  • On the face of it, the painting is neither lascivious nor does it appeal to the prurient interest; it "has not lost its artistic value/touch". Nudity alone is not obscenity.
  • There is no mens rea: "since the scope of the subject is so limited, it does not really require any evidence to be led and on the face of it, both the elements i.e. mens rea and actus reus appear to be absent."
  • For s.298 IPC, mere knowledge of the likelihood that religious feelings may be wounded is not sufficient; there must be a deliberate intention. The figure represents an anthropomorphic depiction of a nation, not a religious figure.
  • The ingredients of s.500 IPC (defamation) are "completely absent".
  • The complainants "are not the types who would go to art galleries or have an interest in contemporary art"; "a person would firstly access such a website only if he has some interest in art and that too contemporary art and in case he does view such a website, he always would have the option to not to view or close the said web page."
  • The criminal justice system "ought not to be invoked as a convenient recourse to ventilate any and all objections to an artistic work."
For the respondents / complainants
  • The painting uploaded on a website "could be accessed by any person sitting across the globe who in consequence whereto could get affected by viewing the same."
  • The painting is obscene under s.292 IPC because it depicts a nude figure and would offend a substantial section of viewers.
  • The petitioner must be answerable before the courts of the places where the complaints were filed, since the offence was "committed" wherever the material was viewed.
  • The Learned ASG accepted that "our Criminal Code does not deal with such jurisdictional aspect directly" and that the answer rested in the power of the Supreme Court under s.406 CrPC — which was the procedure adopted in the present case.

4 · The Court's reasoning on obscenity

The Court set out the law of obscenity in India and England: the Hicklin test, applied in England since Regina v. Hicklin (following Lord Campbell's 1857 legislation) — "the test of obscenity is whether the tendency of the matter charged as obscenity is to deprave and corrupt those whose minds are open to such immoral influences and into whose hands a publication of this sort may fall." It noted that "the Courts in the United States of America have given up the Hicklin's Test, but the Indian law on obscenity is more or less based on it", and that Indian law "also panders to the test of 'lascivious and prurient interests' as taken from the American law."

It then reproduced s.292 IPC in full, noting that s.292 was enacted "to give effect to Article I of the International Convention for suppression of or traffic in obscene publications to which India is a signatory", and that by Act 36 of 1969 the section was amended "to give more precise meaning to the word 'obscene' ... in addition to creating an exception for publication of matter which is proved to be justified as being for the public good, being in the interest of science, literature, art or learning or other objects of general concern."

Crucially for this Unit, the Court reproduced s.67 of the Information Technology Act, 2000 — "Publishing of information which is obscene in electronic form" — and observed: "Thus Section 67 is the first statutory provisions dealing with obscenity on the Internet. It must be noted that the both under the Indian Penal Code, 1860 and the Information Technology Act, 2000 the test to determine obscenity is similar."

📘 s.292(1) IPC — the deeming provision (source: IPC 1860)

"For the purposes of Sub-section (2) book, pamphlet, paper, writing, drawing, painting, representation, figure or any other object, shall be deemed to be obscene, if it is lascivious or appeals to the prurient interest or if its effect, or (where it comprises two or more distinct items) the effects of any one of its items, is, if taken as a whole, such as to tend to deprave and corrupt persons who are likely, having regard to all relevant circumstances, to read, see or hear the matter contained or embodied in it." The exceptions cover publications justified as being for the public good in the interest of science, literature, art or learning, and representations on or in ancient monuments, temples and cars used for the conveyance of idols.

5 · The Court's holding on obscenity

Held on the merits
  1. The painting is not obscene. "On applying the said tests governing obscenity, in my considered view, the said painting cannot be said to fall within the purview of Section 292 thereby making it obscene. The impugned painting on the face of it is neither lascivious nor appeals to the prurient interests. At the same time, the person who is likely to view the said painting would not tend to be depraved or corrupted."
  2. Nudity is not obscenity. "Just because the artist has expressed it in nude does not make the painting obscene per se thereby satisfying the test that nudity or sex alone cannot be said to be obscene. If the painting is looked as a whole ... the aesthetic touch to the painting dwarfs the so called obscenity in the form of nudity and renders it so picayune and insignificant that the nudity in the painting can easily be overlooked."
  3. Offence or disgust is not the test. "Though some might feel offended or disgusted at the very inception of seeing the alleged Mother India in nude but that by itself and nothing more in my opinion is not sufficient to qualify the test of obscenity."
  4. No mens rea under ss.294/298 IPC. "Since the scope of the subject is so limited, it does not really require any evidence to be led and on the face of it, both the elements i.e. mens rea and actus reus appear to be absent." For s.298, "mere knowledge of the likelihood that the religious feelings of another person may be wounded would not be sufficient"; and "the subject matter i.e. Bharat Mata could be alleged to wound nationalist feelings of an individual and not any religious feelings."
  5. s.500 IPC not attracted. "Section 500 IPC requires the basic ingredient of defamation to be satisfied which seems to be completely absent in the present case."
  6. Result: "In view of the aforesaid, the summoning orders and warrants of arrest issued against the petitioner in the complaint cases are quashed and the revision petitions filed against them are allowed leaving the parties to bear their own costs."

6 · The JURISDICTION section — paras 122 to 129, the reason this case is in Unit IX

The Court then addressed a general question that arose during the hearing: "which court would be considered as a competent court having the jurisdiction to try the matter particularly when the nature of the case is like the present one where the impugned painting uploaded on the website, accessible to people across the globe, was being viewed by different people/complainants across the country who in turn got offended with such painting and filed their complaints at various places in India, especially keeping in mind the vexatious and the frivolous complaints which can be filed as an instrument to harass the accused."

📘 Chapter XIII, CrPC 1973 — the provisions the Court applied (source: CrPC 1973)

s.177 — "Every offence shall ordinarily be inquired into and tried by a Court within whose local jurisdiction it was committed."

s.178 — "When it is uncertain in which of several local areas an offence was committed, or where an offence is committed partly in one local area and partly in another, or where an offence is a continuing one, and continues to be committed in more local areas than one, or where it consists of several acts done in different local areas, it may be inquired into or tried by a Court having jurisdiction over any of such local areas."

s.179 — "When an act is an offence by reason of anything which has been done and of a consequence which has ensued, the offence may be inquired into or tried by a Court within whose local jurisdiction such thing has been done or such consequence has ensued."

s.186 — "Where two or more Courts have taken cognizance of the same offence and a question arises as to which of them ought to inquire into or try that offence, the question shall be decided— (i) if the Courts are subordinate to the same High Court, by that High Court; (ii) if the Courts are not subordinate to the same High Court, by the High Court within the local limits of whose appellate criminal jurisdiction the proceedings were first commenced, and thereupon all other proceedings in respect of that offence shall be discontinued."

s.406(1) — "Whenever it is made to appear to the Supreme Court that an order under this section is expedient for the ends of justice, it may direct that any particular case or appeal be transferred from High Court to another High Court or from a Criminal Court subordinate to one High Court to another Criminal Court of equal or superior jurisdiction subordinate to another High Court."

🔑 The magistrate's duty under s.202 — the Court's reformulation

"In a constitutional democracy wedded to and governed by the rule of law, responsibilities of the judiciary arouse great expectations... Thus, a magistrate must scrutinise each case in order to prevent vexatious and frivolous cases from being filed and make sure that it is not used a tool to harass the accused which would amount to gross abuse of the process of the court. Only in appropriate cases should a private complaint case proceed further without a prior investigation by the police consequent upon the direction of the Magistrate Under Section 156(3) of the said Code in the matter." And specifically: "Especially taking into account the recent amendment to Section 202 of the said Code, a Magistrate should postpone the issue of process against the accused where the accused is residing at a place beyond the area in which he exercises jurisdiction. He may postpone the issue of process against the accused and either enquire himself into the case or direct investigation to be made by the police or by such other person as he deems fit, for the purposes of deciding whether or not there is sufficient ground for proceeding Under Section 204 of the Code. He must examine the nature of allegations made in the complaint and the evidence both oral and documentary in support thereof and may even himself put questions to the complainant and his witnesses to elicit answers to find out the truthfulness of the allegations or otherwise and then examine if any offence is prima facie committed by all or any of the accused as enunciated in Pepsi Foods Ltd.'s Case and Naganagouda Veeranagouda Patil & Anr.'s Case."

7 · Decision on jurisdiction — and the call for legislation

Held on jurisdiction
  1. The Court recorded the ASG's submission that "as such our Criminal Code does not deal with such jurisdictional aspect directly and submitted that the answer only rested in the power conferred to the Supreme Court of India under Section 406 of the said Code which procedure has been adopted in the present case."
  2. The Court accepted that Chapter XIII CrPC governs, and set out ss.177, 178, 179 and 186 with the authorities above.
  3. "In my considered view, this particular aspect of jurisdiction fettered within the parameters of scrutiny of Section 202 of the said Code as discussed above derives its importance especially with the advent of the technological explosion where a person sitting anywhere across the globe can get access to what ever information he has been looking for just with a click of a mouse. Therefore, it has become imperative that in this information age, jurisdiction be more circumscribed so that an artist like in the present case is not made to run from pillar to post facing proceedings."
  4. "This however is not an aspect where a direction can be issued since it is within the domain of appropriate legislation. The learned ASG while assisting this Court fairly stated that he would advice the Government to take steps by way of appropriate legislative amendments as may be proper keeping in mind the balancing of interest between the person aggrieved and the accused so as to prevent harassment of artists, sculptors, authors, filmmakers etc. in different creative fields. I say nothing more but hope that this aspect would get the attention it deserves and the legislature in its wisdom would examine the feasibility of possible changes in law."

8 · Principle evolved

⚖️ The Maqbool Fida Husain principle — two halves

(a) On obscenity and the internet. Section 67 of the IT Act, 2000 is the first statutory provision dealing with obscenity on the internet, and the test of obscenity is the same under the IPC and the IT Act. A painting uploaded on a website is not obscene merely because it depicts nudity; it must be lascivious, appeal to the prurient interest, or tend to deprave and corrupt the likely viewer. A viewer who accesses an art website does so out of interest in art and "always would have the option to not to view or close the said web page". Offence or disgust is not the test. There is no mens rea where the artist's impulse is artistic.

(b) On criminal jurisdiction. Chapter XIII CrPC governs criminal jurisdiction. Section 177 gives the ordinary rule (place of commission); s.178 deals with uncertainty, offences committed partly in one and partly in another local area, continuing offences, and offences consisting of several acts done in different local areas; s.179 gives jurisdiction both where the act was done and where the consequence ensued; s.186 resolves competing cognizance. But these provisions, applied to a single internet publication accessible everywhere, produce simultaneous jurisdiction in every district in India — which invites vexatious and frivolous complaints as an instrument of harassment. The only available answers are (i) rigorous s.202 scrutiny by the magistrate, including postponing process where the accused resides beyond the magistrate's jurisdiction, and (ii) consolidation and transfer under s.406 CrPC by the Supreme Court. A proper solution requires legislation.

12Criminal jurisdiction in cyberspace — CrPC 1973, ss.177–179, 186, 188 and 189

The syllabus devotes a whole clause to criminal jurisdiction: "Criminal jurisdiction (the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 - ss. 177-179, 186,188 and 189)". This is the section in which Maqbool Fida Husain lives, and it is the section in which Indian law is least developed.

12.1 The scheme of Chapter XIII CrPC, 1973

Chapter XIII of the CrPC is titled "Jurisdiction of the Criminal Courts in Inquiries and Trials". It answers one question: where may an offence be inquired into and tried? The seven provisions in the syllabus work as a set.

📘 ss.177–179, CrPC 1973 — the territorial core (source: CrPC 1973, Chapter XIII)

177. Ordinary place of inquiry and trial.—"Every offence shall ordinarily be inquired into and tried by a Court within whose local jurisdiction it was committed."

178. Place of inquiry or trial.—"(1) When it is uncertain in which of several local areas an offence was committed, or (2) where an offence is committed partly in one local area and partly in another, or (3) where an offence is a continuing one, and continues to be committed in more local areas than one, or (4) where it consists of several acts done in different local areas, it may be inquired into or tried by a Court having jurisdiction over any of such local areas."

179. Offence triable where act is done or consequence ensues.—"When an act is an offence by reason of anything which has been done and of a consequence which has ensued, the offence may be inquired into or tried by a Court within whose local jurisdiction such thing has been done or such consequence has ensued."

📘 ss.186, 188 and 189, CrPC 1973 — competing cognizance and offences abroad (source: CrPC 1973, Chapter XIII)

186. High Court to decide, in case of doubt, district where inquiry or trial shall take place.—"Where two or more Courts have taken cognizance of the same offence and a question arises as to which of them ought to inquire into or try that offence, the question shall be decided— (a) if the Courts are subordinate to the same High Court, by that High Court; (b) if the Courts are not subordinate to the same High Court, by the High Court within the local limits of whose appellate criminal jurisdiction the proceedings were first commenced, and thereupon all other proceedings in respect of that offence shall be discontinued."

187. Power to issue summons or warrant for offence committed beyond local jurisdiction.—Where a Magistrate of the first class sees reason to believe that any person within his local jurisdiction has committed outside such jurisdiction (whether within or outside India) an offence which cannot, under ss.177 to 185 or any other law, be inquired into or tried within such jurisdiction but is under some law triable in India, he may inquire into the offence as if it had been committed within his local jurisdiction, compel the person to appear before him, and send him to the Magistrate having jurisdiction — or take a bond for his appearance.

188. Offence committed outside India.—"When an offence is committed outside India— (1) by a citizen of India, whether on the high seas or elsewhere; or (2) by a person, not being such citizen, on any ship or aircraft registered in India, he may be dealt with in respect of such offence as if it had been committed at any place within India at which he may be found: Provided that, notwithstanding anything in any of the preceding sections of this Chapter, no such offence shall be inquired into or tried in India except with the previous sanction of the Central Government."

189. Receipt of evidence relating to offences committed outside India.—"When any offence alleged to have been committed in a territory outside India is being inquired into or tried under the provisions of section 188, the Central Government may, if it thinks fit, direct that copies of depositions made or exhibits produced before a judicial officer in or for that territory or before a diplomatic or consular representative of India in or for that territory shall be received as evidence by the Court holding such inquiry or trial in any case in which such Court might issue a commission for taking evidence as to the matters to which such depositions or exhibits relate."

12.2 Why a single internet publication produces simultaneous jurisdiction everywhere

Apply the provisions to the facts of Maqbool Fida Husain: a painting uploaded once, accessible everywhere, viewed by complainants in Pandharpur, Rajkot, Indore and Bhopal.

  • s.177 asks where the offence "was committed". The publication is the act; the offence is the publication. Where is an online publication "committed"? At the server? At the uploader's desk? At the reader's screen? Each answer is defensible.
  • s.178(1) — it is "uncertain in which of the several local areas an offence was committed". This alone would give jurisdiction to any court having jurisdiction over any such local area.
  • s.178(2) — the offence is "committed partly in one local area and partly in another".
  • s.178(3) — a website is arguably "a continuing one, and continues to be committed in more local areas than one": the material remains available for as long as it is hosted.
  • s.178(4) — the offence "consists of several acts done in different local areas" — each view, each download, each transmission.
  • s.179 — the act (publication) is an offence "by reason of anything which has been done and of a consequence which has ensued" (the offence caused to the viewer). Either the place of the act or the place of the consequence will do. As the Court noted, "it is not necessary to prove that things done must necessarily be an offence, as the conjunction 'and' used in this section suggests that the act contemplated becomes an offence on account of the cumulative effect of the things done and the consequences ensued."
⚠️ The consequence — the point the Court was driving at

Every one of these provisions, applied literally, gives every magistrate in India jurisdiction over a single internet publication. That is why the Court held that "in this information age, jurisdiction be more circumscribed so that an artist like in the present case is not made to run from pillar to post facing proceedings", and why it identified the two existing control valves — s.202 scrutiny and s.406 transfer — while expressly saying that a real solution lies with the legislature. This is the contrast with the civil side: on the civil side the judiciary (Banyan Tree) supplied a test; on the criminal side it could not, because the constraints are constitutional and legislative.

12.3 Section 202 CrPC — the magistrate's scrutiny as the practical control

📘 s.202, CrPC 1973 (source: CrPC 1973, Chapter XV)

"Postponement of issue of process.—(1) A Magistrate taking cognizance of an offence on complaint shall examine the complainant and the witnesses present, if any, on oath under sub-section (1) of section 200, and the substance of such examination shall be reduced to writing and shall be signed by the complainant and the witnesses, and also by the Magistrate: Provided that, if the complainant is a public servant acting in the discharge of his official duties, the Magistrate may dispense with such examination. (2) Where the Magistrate is satisfied that there are sufficient grounds for proceeding, he may, instead of issuing process, postpone the issue of process ... and either inquire into the case himself or direct an investigation to be made by a police officer or by such other person as he thinks fit, for the purpose of deciding whether or not there is sufficient ground for proceeding: Provided that ... where the accused is residing at a place beyond the area in which he exercises jurisdiction, the Magistrate shall postpone the issue of process and either inquire into the case himself or direct investigation..." The purpose is "to ascertain whether there is sufficient ground for proceeding against the accused" and to "prevent the harassment of the accused by a frivolous or vexatious complaint".

In Maqbool Fida Husain the Court made the s.202 duty the centre of its jurisdictional answer: a magistrate "must scrutinise each case in order to prevent vexatious and frivolous cases from being filed and make sure that it is not used a tool to harass the accused which would amount to gross abuse of the process of the court", and "only in appropriate cases should a private complaint case proceed further without a prior investigation by the police consequent upon the direction of the Magistrate Under Section 156(3)".

12.4 Section 406 CrPC — consolidation and transfer

This is the mechanism the Supreme Court used to rescue M.F. Husain from four simultaneous prosecutions. Under s.406(1), "whenever it is made to appear to the Supreme Court that an order under this section is expedient for the ends of justice, it may direct that any particular case or appeal be transferred from High Court to another High Court or from a Criminal Court subordinate to one High Court to another Criminal Court of equal or superior jurisdiction subordinate to another High Court." Sub-section (2) permits the Supreme Court to act "only on the application of the Attorney General of India or of a party interested", supported by affidavit; sub-section (3) permits an order for compensation up to one thousand rupees where the application is dismissed as frivolous or vexatious.

The Court also relied on the High Court's power under s.407 CrPC (transfer of cases and appeals) as the analogous intra-State mechanism.

12.5 Section 182 CrPC — the provision closest to the internet

📘 s.182, CrPC 1973 — "Offences committed by letters, etc." (source: CrPC 1973, Chapter XIII)

"(1) Any offence which includes cheating may, if the deception is practised by means of letters or telecommunication messages, be inquired into or tried by any Court within whose local jurisdiction such letters or messages were sent or were received; and any offence of cheating and dishonestly inducing delivery of property may be inquired into or tried by any Court within whose local jurisdiction the property was delivered by the person deceived or was received by the accused person."

Two reasons this matters for cyberspace. First, it is the only provision in Chapter XIII that expressly contemplates a wrong committed through a communication medium rather than at a place, and it adopts a sender-or-receiver rule. Second, it is the criminal analogue of the s.13(3) IT Act deeming rule applied in P.R. Transport Agency (§6). Read together, they give a principled answer for internet cheating and phishing: jurisdiction lies where the message was sent or received, and the message is deemed received at the addressee's place of business.

📝 The five-point answer to "how far is criminal jurisdiction over cyber offences settled in India?"
  1. Territorial rule: s.177 CrPC — the offence is tried where committed. For a website, that place is uncertain, so s.178 applies and any of the local areas may try it.
  2. Consequences: s.179 gives jurisdiction where the consequence ensued — the reader's district. Authorities: Ashok v. State of U.P.; S. Bangarappa v. Ganesh Narayan.
  3. Competing cognizance: s.186 resolves which of two courts proceeds, and all other proceedings are discontinued. Authority: Kuljit Singh v. CBI.
  4. The control valves: s.202 (magistrate must postpone process where the accused resides beyond his jurisdiction) and s.406 (Supreme Court transfer, used in Maqbool Fida Husain).
  5. The gap: Chapter XIII produces universal jurisdiction over internet publications, inviting harassment. The Court itself said a legislative fix is needed. Contrast the civil side, where Banyan Tree supplied the test by purposive construction of s.20(c) CPC.

13Extraterritorial jurisdiction under the IT Act — s.1(2), s.75 and s.3 IPC

The final substantive clause of the syllabus: "Extraterritorial Jurisdiction under IT Act (s.1(2), s.75, s.3 IPC)". These three provisions, read together, are the complete answer to the question "can an offence committed abroad through a computer be tried in India?"

13.1 The presumption against extraterritoriality

Indian legislation is presumed to be territorial. A statute does not reach conduct outside India unless it says so expressly. That presumption is the reason s.1(2) of the IT Act exists and the reason s.75 was inserted by the 2008 amendment: Parliament had to legislate for extraterritorial reach. In the civil context the same presumption is what limits s.62(2) of the Copyright Act — a right granted in India can be infringed only in India, so s.62(2) cannot be used to sue for an act of infringement committed wholly abroad.

📘 s.1(2), Information Technology Act, 2000 — "Short title, extent, commencement and application" (source: IT Act 2000, Act No. 21 of 2000)

"(2) It shall extend to the whole of India and, save as otherwise provided in this Act, it applies also to any offence or contravention thereunder committed outside India by any person."

Read s.1(2) carefully — it does three things in one sentence:

  1. Territorial extent: the Act extends to the whole of India.
  2. Extraterritorial reach: the Act applies to any offence or contravention under it committed outside India by any person — note "any person", not "any citizen". Nationality is irrelevant.
  3. A saving: "save as otherwise provided in this Act" — which points directly to s.75, where Parliament did otherwise provide.
📘 s.75, Information Technology Act, 2000 — "Act to apply for offence or contravention committed outside India" (source: IT Act 2000 as substituted by the IT (Amendment) Act 2008, Act 10 of 2009, w.e.f. 27.10.2009)

"(1) Subject to the provisions of sub-section (2), the provisions of this Act shall apply also to any offence or contravention committed outside India by any person irrespective of his nationality.

(2) For the purposes of sub-section (1), this Act shall apply to an offence or contravention committed outside India by any person if the act or conduct constituting the offence or contravention involves a computer, computer system or computer network located in India."

13.2 Section 3 IPC — and why it is not a self-executing provision

📘 s.3, Indian Penal Code, 1860 — "Punishment of offences committed beyond, but which by law may be tried within, India" (source: IPC 1860, Chapter I)

"Any person liable, by any Indian law, to be tried for an offence committed beyond India shall be dealt with according to the provisions of this Code for any act committed beyond India in the same manner as if such act had been committed within India."

The words that matter are "liable, by any Indian law, to be tried". Section 3 IPC is a procedural equaliser, not a grant of jurisdiction. It says: if some other Indian law makes a person triable in India for an act done abroad, then treat that act as if it had been done in India for the purposes of the Code. So s.3 IPC never operates alone. You must always identify the enabling law. For cyber offences there are two candidates:

  • The IT Act itself, through s.1(2) and s.75 — for offences and contraventions under the IT Act.
  • s.188 CrPC — for offences under any law, but only where the offender is a citizen of India (on the high seas or elsewhere) or a non-citizen on a ship or aircraft registered in India, and only with the previous sanction of the Central Government.
📘 s.4, Indian Penal Code, 1860 — "Extension of Code to extra-territorial offences" (source: IPC 1860, Chapter I)

"The provisions of this Code apply also to any offence committed by— (1) any citizen of India in any place without and beyond India; (2) any person on any ship or aircraft registered in India wherever it may be; (3) any person in any place without and beyond India committing offence targeting a computer resource located in India." Explanation.—In this section— (a) the word "offence" includes every act committed outside India which, if committed in India, would be punishable under this Code; (b) the expression "computer resource" shall have the meaning assigned to it in clause (k) of sub-section (1) of section 2 of the Information Technology Act, 2000. The third limb is the cyber-specific extension, inserted by the IT (Amendment) Act 2008 (Act 10 of 2009, s. 51, w.e.f. 27.10.2009), and it is the IPC counterpart of s.75(2) IT Act: it makes an IPC offence committed outside India punishable in India where it targets a computer resource located in India. This is the provision that answers the "hacker in a foreign country attacks an Indian server" problem.

💡 The real-world example to quote — the NIIR/FinFisher and Sunil Bharathi line of cases

The practical operation of s.75(2) is visible in cyber-fraud and cyber-terrorism prosecutions where the accused operate from outside India but the victim's computer, the bank's server or the payment gateway is located in India. In such cases the Indian investigating agencies invoke s.1(2) and s.75(2) of the IT Act together with s.4(3) IPC, and the offence is tried in India. The same connecting factor — a computer resource located in India — is what makes s.66F (cyber terrorism) workable against attacks launched from abroad. By contrast, pure content offences (obscenity, defamation) published and hosted wholly abroad remain the hardest cases, because the Indian courts cannot point to a computer resource located in India through which the offence was committed.

⚠️ Three traps in the extraterritoriality answer
  • Do not say "the IT Act applies extraterritorially" and stop. Say: s.1(2) gives the general rule; s.75(2) supplies the connecting factor (a computer, computer system or computer network located in India); and s.75(1) makes nationality irrelevant.
  • Do not treat s.3 IPC as a grant of jurisdiction. It presupposes that the person is already "liable, by any Indian law, to be tried". Name that law.
  • Do not forget the Central Government's sanction under the proviso to s.188 CrPC for offences committed outside India by a citizen of India or by a person on an Indian ship or aircraft. An omission here is a classic lost mark.

CIVIL JURISDICTION

Examination point: this is the standard closing observation for a 15-mark answer — the gap between the civil and criminal sides.
📝 The "critically examine" answer in nine sentences
  1. Territorial jurisdiction rests on three facts — the place of the act, the place of the effect, and the identity and location of the actor — and the internet dislocates all three.
  2. International law recognises both subjective and objective territoriality; the objection to objective territoriality is that it is universalising.
  3. India has no long-arm statute for civil internet disputes; s.134(2) TMA and s.62(2) Copyright Act are long-arm provisions only in a limited sense.
  4. The judiciary therefore imported the common law into s.20(c) CPC: purposeful availment, following International Shoe and Burger King.
  5. Casio's accessibility rule was overruled; India TV's approach was approved.
  6. Interactivity alone is insufficient: the plaintiff must show specific targeting of forum viewers for commercial transactions, some such transaction with a forum user, and injury to the plaintiff in the forum.
  7. Trap orders are scrutinised with jealousy; a solitary trap transaction cannot found jurisdiction (Impresario).
  8. On the criminal side, Chapter XIII CrPC gives every magistrate jurisdiction over a single internet publication; the only controls are s.202 scrutiny and s.406 transfer, and the Court has asked for legislation.
  9. Extraterritorial criminal jurisdiction exists under s.1(2) and s.75 IT Act (computer resource located in India) and s.4(3) IPC, with s.3 IPC and s.188 CrPC supplying the procedure — and the Central Government's sanction.

Exercise 1DU self-learning exercise printed after Banyan Tree · pp. 230 of the case material

"Can [a] Delhi court assume jurisdiction if the defendant in [the] above case provided spa services in Hyderabad, and instead of booking orders directly, enters into a contract with an online platform X, to book orders in Delhi through its website for spa services in Hyderabad. The plaintiff has registered office in Tokyo. In other words, does cause of action arise in Delhi. (Hint — Impresario Entertainment Case)"

Model answer

Short answer: No. On these facts the Delhi court cannot assume territorial jurisdiction, and the plaint would have to be returned under Order VII Rule 10 CPC.

Step 1 — The action and the parties. The action is for passing off (and, if the plaintiff is the registered proprietor in India, infringement). The defendant provides spa services in Hyderabad. The plaintiff's registered office is in Tokyo; the plaintiff has no pleaded office, no residence and no carrying on of business in Delhi. So s.20(a) and s.20(b) CPC are unavailable on both sides, and the special forums in s.134(2) TMA and s.62(2) Copyright Act are also unavailable — the plaintiff does not carry on business within the forum court's jurisdiction, and s.134(2) does not cover passing off in any event. The plaintiff must found jurisdiction on s.20(c) CPC: that the cause of action, wholly or in part, arises in Delhi.

Step 2 — Apply the Banyan Tree test. The Division Bench held that, for a passing off or infringement action where the plaintiff is not carrying on business within the forum and there is no long-arm statute, the plaintiff must show that the defendant purposefully availed itself of the forum jurisdiction — that the nature of the defendant's activity through the website was with an intention to conclude a commercial transaction with the website user, and that the specific targeting of the forum State resulted in injury or harm to the plaintiff within the forum State. The plaintiff must plead and produce material to prima facie show that the website, "whether euphemistically termed as 'passive plus' or 'interactive'", was specifically targeted at viewers in the forum state for commercial transactions, and that some commercial transaction using the website was entered into by the defendant with a user of its website within the forum state resulting in an injury or harm to the plaintiff within the forum state.

Step 3 — The platform X contract changes nothing. The defendant has not itself targeted Delhi; it has contracted with an intermediary platform which happens to be usable from Delhi. Following Impresario, a defendant's presence on a third-party interactive platform — even one whose company has an office in Delhi — does not by itself show that the defendant specifically targeted the forum State for commercial transactions. The platform is a conduit; the defendant's own activity is in Hyderabad.

Step 4 — The service cannot be availed except in Hyderabad. This is the decisive point, and it is the exact reasoning of Impresario: "even if the defendant has been able to attract customers from other jurisdiction including by way of [the platform], the services of defendant cannot be availed unless the customers go to Hyderabad. ... The commercial transaction will take place only on the customer availing the services of the defendant at Hyderabad." A spa service is a service requiring physical presence — the Banyan Tree Division Bench's own distinction between goods and services that can be ordered online and delivered, and those (cars, furniture, restaurant food, spa treatments) that require the customer to be physically present. A booking made in Delhi is only a step towards a transaction that is completed in Hyderabad. As the Impresario review judgment put it, "Merely by reserving a table in the defendant's restaurant at Hyderabad, the contract does not get concluded."

Step 5 — A solitary booking is not a trap transaction that helps. Even if the plaintiff sets up a booking from Delhi, "on the basis of a solitary transaction, through internet plaintiff cannot claim that cause of action having arisen at Delhi". A trap transaction must be a real commercial transaction with someone not set up by the plaintiff; a series must be obtained by fair means and averred unambiguously in the plaint with supporting material.

Step 6 — Convenience. Following Big Tree, the court would also note the "lack of convenience as regards the place of suing": the plaintiff is in Tokyo, the defendant in Hyderabad, and the only connection with Delhi is the platform's usability. Sections 22 and 23 CPC would permit transfer to a more convenient forum if jurisdiction existed.

Conclusion. The cause of action does not arise in Delhi. The website is at best "passive plus" and, on the Banyan Tree–Impresario line, neither its accessibility in Delhi nor a solitary Delhi booking founds jurisdiction. The Delhi court has no territorial jurisdiction; the plaint must be returned under Order VII Rule 10 CPC to be presented to the court of competent jurisdiction — here, the court at Hyderabad.
Exercise 2DU self-learning exercise printed after World Wrestling Entertainment · p. 194

"Whether mere display of articles on website, one can presume that plaintiff, a natural person, carries on business in the jurisdiction where interactive website of plaintiff is accessible or he should also prove that some genuine transactions have taken place in the jurisdiction. (Hint — Applicability of Minimum Contacts framework.)"

Model answer

The proposition: mere display of articles on a website, without proof of genuine transactions, is not enough to establish that the plaintiff carries on business in the jurisdiction. Both limbs of the exercise point the same way, and the answer should be given in two parts.

Part A — Why mere display is not enough. WWE did not hold that a website display alone constitutes carrying on business. It held that where transactions are actually concluded with customers in the forum — offers made there, subject to confirmation through the website, money paid from there — "the essential part of the business of the [plaintiff], insofar as its transactions with customers in [the forum] are concerned, takes place in [the forum]". The reasoning is explicitly transactional: "The offers are made by customers at Delhi. The offers are subject to confirmation/acceptance of the appellant/plaintiff through its website. The money would emanate or be paid from Delhi." A website that displays articles but permits no transaction is, on the Banyan Tree taxonomy, a passive website; and "a passive website, with no intention to specifically target audiences outside the State where the host of the website is located, cannot vest the forum court with jurisdiction."

Part B — The minimum contacts framework. The hint directs the student to the American doctrine, which supplies the vocabulary for the answer. Under International Shoe, the defendant must have such minimum contacts with the forum that the suit does not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice". Under Burger King, jurisdiction requires that the defendant purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum, thereby invoking its benefits and protections. Under Bensusan, a defendant who has "simply created a web site and permitted anyone who could find it to access it" is like one who places a product into the stream of commerce — not purposeful availment. Under Neogen, the mere availability of a website is "an attenuated contact that falls short of purposeful availment".

Applied to the plaintiff's side of the question: mere availability of a website is an attenuated contact. It is the transactions — the deliberate, repeated conclusion of sales with forum customers — that constitute the purposeful invocation of the forum's market. Hence the plaintiff should be required to prove some genuine transactions, not merely the display of articles.

Part C — The qualification for a natural person. For a natural person, the position is more favourable than for a company. The Explanation to s.20 CPC — which requires an office — applies only to a corporation. A natural person can carry on business at a place without an office there. But the burden is still on the plaintiff to plead, in the plaint (Order VII Rule 1(e) and (f)), and then to support, the facts showing the transactions. On a demurrer under Order VII Rule 10 CPC, only the averments in the plaint are examined (WWE); at the later, evidential stage, the plaintiff must prove them.

Conclusion. Mere display of articles on an accessible website does not permit the presumption that the plaintiff carries on business in that jurisdiction. The plaintiff must plead and prove genuine transactions with forum customers. The minimum contacts framework supplies the reason: availability is an attenuated contact; the concluded transactions are the purposeful availment.
Exercise 3DU self-learning exercise printed after World Wrestling Entertainment · p. 194

"Does Delhi Court has jurisdiction on the ground of 'plaintiff carrying on business', where plaintiff is a company and its interactive website is accessible in Delhi, though cause of action arose in Mumbai where the company had branch office. (Hint — Big Tree Entertainment Case)"

Model answer

Short answer: No.

Step 1 — Identify the ground relied on. The plaintiff invokes s.134(2) TMA / s.62(2) Copyright Act on the footing of its own carrying on of business in Delhi, and not the defendant's activities giving rise to a part of the cause of action in Delhi. That choice is fatal.

Step 2 — Apply the Explanation to s.20 CPC. "A corporation shall be deemed to carry on business at its sole or principal office in India or, in respect of any cause of action arising at any place where it has also a subordinate office, at such place." Read with Patel Roadways Ltd. v. Prasad Trading Co., (1991) 4 SCC 270, the Explanation "is really an explanation to Clause (a)" and clarifies where a corporation can be said to carry on business. As Big Tree put it: "In law unless a company has an office at a place, it cannot be said to be carrying on business at that particular place."

Step 3 — Apply Sanjay Dalia. In Indian Performing Rights Society Ltd. v. Sanjay Dalia, (2015) 10 SCC 161, the Supreme Court held that the restrictive meaning of "carries on business" adopted for a defendant corporation applies equally to a plaintiff corporation invoking the special forums. And it added the restriction: "in case Plaintiff is residing or carrying on business at a particular place/having its head office and at such place cause of action has also arisen wholly or in part, Plaintiff cannot ignore such a place under the guise that he is carrying on business at other far flung places also."

Step 4 — Apply the restriction to these facts. Here the cause of action has arisen in Mumbai, where the company has a branch office. The company therefore cannot ignore Mumbai and sue in Delhi, where it has no office at all. The accessibility of its website in Delhi does not supply the missing office.

Step 5 — Distinguish WWE. WWE did not pronounce upon the position of a company plaintiff with no office in the forum; its "virtual shop" reasoning was applied to a plaintiff that had pleaded and relied on its carrying on of business in Delhi. Where the plaintiff is a company with no office in the forum, the Explanation governs and the plaint must be returned.

Step 6 — Note the alternative route. Had the plaintiff instead pleaded that the defendant's impugned activities took place in Delhi so that a part of the cause of action arose there under s.20(c), Delhi could have had territorial jurisdiction. That route was identified by the Big Tree court itself and was not taken.

Conclusion. No. A company plaintiff with no office in Delhi does not "carry on business" in Delhi merely because its interactive website is accessible there. Where the cause of action has arisen in Mumbai, where the company has a branch office, the suit must be filed in Mumbai, and the Delhi plaint must be returned under Order VII Rule 10 CPC.
Exercise 4DU self-learning exercise printed after Maqbool Fida Husain · p. 78

"If a poet through his poetry classify Mother India in the same fashion by his words as described by the artist in his painting. Can you apply the same principle as laid in MF Husain Case? (Samresh Bose v. Amal Mitra, (1986 AIR 967), Difference between obscenity and vulgarity.)"

Model answer

Yes — the same principle applies, and the answer is the same.

Step 1 — The statutory test is medium-neutral. The Court in Maqbool Fida Husain expressly held that "both under the Indian Penal Code, 1860 and the Information Technology Act, 2000 the test to determine obscenity is similar", and that s.67 IT Act "is the first statutory provisions dealing with obscenity on the Internet". A poem published on a website is therefore judged by the same test as a painting: is it lascivious; does it appeal to the prurient interest; does its effect, taken as a whole, tend to deprave and corrupt persons who are likely to read, see or hear it? (s.292(1) IPC, mirrored in s.67 IT Act.)

Step 2 — Apply the Husain reasoning to words. The Court held that nudity alone is not obscenity: "just because the artist has expressed it in nude does not make the painting obscene per se". It held that the work must be read as a whole: "If the painting is looked as a whole, it would reveal that ... the aesthetic touch to the painting dwarfs the so called obscenity". It held that offence or disgust is not the test: "some might feel offended or disgusted ... but that by itself and nothing more in my opinion is not sufficient to qualify the test of obscenity." And it held that there is no mens rea where the impulse is artistic: "the object of painting the woman in nude is also part of the same expression and is obviously not to stimulate the viewer's prurience but instead to shake up the very conscious of the viewer".

Step 3 — A poem describing Mother India in the same fashion. On the same reasoning, a poet who describes the nation as a distressed, unclothed woman is using a metaphor for the nation's suffering, not writing to arouse prurience. Read as a whole, the poem would be judged by its literary and artistic merit, and "the aesthetic touch ... dwarfs the so called obscenity". The offence or disgust of a reader who stumbles upon it "by itself and nothing more" is not the test. There would be no deliberate intention to outrage religious feelings under s.298 IPC, and no defamation under s.500 IPC.

Step 4 — Bring in Samresh Bose v. Amal Mitra, (1986) AIR 967. The Supreme Court in Samresh Bose drew the distinction between obscenity and vulgarity: a vulgar or coarse expression is not necessarily obscene. Obscenity requires the tendency to deprave and corrupt; mere vulgarity, coarseness or bad taste does not suffice. The Court also held that a work must be judged by contemporary standards and read as a whole, not by isolated words lifted out of context. That is precisely the technique the Husain court applied to the painting, and it is the technique to apply to the poem.

Step 5 — The jurisdictional dimension. The jurisdictional problem is worse for a poem, because words are easier to transmit than images. A poem published on a website is accessible everywhere, so Chapter XIII CrPC would give every magistrate in India jurisdiction (s.177 read with s.178(1), (2), (3) and (4), and s.179). The answer, as in Husain, lies in s.202 scrutiny by the magistrate and in consolidation and transfer under s.406 CrPC — and, ultimately, in legislation.

Conclusion. The same principle applies. The test of obscenity is identical under s.292 IPC and s.67 IT Act; the work must be read as a whole; offence or disgust is not the test; nudity or explicitness alone is not obscenity; and vulgarity must be distinguished from obscenity (Samresh Bose). A poem describing Mother India in the same fashion as Husain's painting would not be obscene, and no offence would be made out.
Exercise 5DU self-learning exercise printed after My Space · p. 125

"Under section 2(1)(w) [of] The Information Technology Act, 2000, is it necessary that [an] intermediary must be active or passive?"

Model answer

Section 2(1)(w) contains no active/passive distinction. It defines "intermediary", with respect to any particular electronic records, as "any person who on behalf of another person receives, stores or transmits that record or provides any service with respect to that record", and includes "telecom service providers, network service providers, internet service providers, web-hosting service providers, search engines, online payment sites, online-auction sites, online-market places and cyber cafes". The definition is functional, not behavioural.

The My Space Division Bench held that MySpace "facially falls within Section 2(1)(w) and qualifies as an intermediary/Internet service provider because it acts as a 'conduit'/portal for information where users can upload and view content." The court's language is instructive: MySpace "provides a neutral platform". So the question is whether the person is acting as a conduit or neutral platform for third-party content — not whether the person is "active" or "passive" in some abstract sense.

Where the active/passive distinction does matter is under s.79(2)(b), which conditions the safe harbour on the intermediary not (i) initiating the transmission, (ii) selecting the receiver of the transmission, and (iii) selecting or modifying the information contained in the transmission. A platform that initiates transmission or selects or modifies content loses the safe harbour — as SCIL argued MySpace had, by inserting advertisements and taking a limited licence to reformat user content. The Division Bench's answer was that MySpace's modifications were "automatic and ... part of the core system with the only purpose of making the content compatible with the webpage", so it complied with s.79(2)(b).

Conclusion. Under s.2(1)(w) an intermediary need be neither "active" nor "passive"; the test is functional — does the person receive, store or transmit, or provide a service with respect to, third-party electronic records? The active/passive distinction is relevant only at the next stage, under s.79(2)(b) and (3), in deciding whether the safe harbour is available.
Exercise 6DU self-learning exercise printed after My Space · p. 125

"Under what condition [could the] Safe Harbour Rule under section 79 [be] made applicable to passive players in online medium which are placed outside India?"

Model answer

Step 1 — Jurisdiction first. Before the safe harbour can be invoked, an Indian court must have jurisdiction over the passive offshore player. Applying Banyan Tree: a passive website, with no intention to specifically target audiences outside the State where the host is located, cannot vest the forum court with jurisdiction. So the threshold condition is that the offshore player must have purposefully availed itself of the Indian jurisdiction — its site must have been specifically targeted at viewers in India for commercial transactions, and some such transaction must have been entered into with an Indian user, causing injury in India. The My Space facts satisfied this: when a user accessed Myspace from India, "she/he is automatically redirected to the website's India-centric version".

Step 2 — The conditions in s.79(2). The safe harbour under s.79(1) applies only if: (a) the functions of the intermediary are limited to providing access to a communication system over which information made available by third parties is transmitted or temporarily stored or hosted; or (b) the intermediary does not (i) initiate the transmission, (ii) select the receiver of the transmission, and (iii) select or modify the information contained in the transmission; and (c) the intermediary observes due diligence while discharging its duties under the Act and observes such other guidelines as the Central Government may prescribe.

Step 3 — The exceptions in s.79(3). The safe harbour is lost if (a) the intermediary has conspired, abetted, aided or induced the commission of the unlawful act; or (b) upon receiving actual knowledge, or being notified by the appropriate Government or its agency, that information, data or a communication link residing in or connected to a computer resource controlled by the intermediary is being used to commit an unlawful act, the intermediary fails to expeditiously remove or disable access to that material without vitiating the evidence. In My Space the Court held that s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act, in the case of internet intermediaries, "contemplates actual knowledge and not general awareness", and that the scope was widened in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, (2015) 5 SCC 1, where actual knowledge was held to mean a court order in cases relatable to Article 19.

Step 4 — The harmonious reading with s.81 and the Copyright Act. Section 79 grants "a measured privilege ... an affirmative defence and not a blanket immunity". It must be read with the proviso to s.81 and with s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act; but for the proviso, copyright owners would have had no recourse against intermediaries at all.

Step 5 — The practical conditions for an offshore passive player. On the above, the safe harbour can be made applicable to a passive offshore player where: (i) the Indian court has jurisdiction because the player targeted India; (ii) the player's function is limited to access, or it does not initiate, select the receiver, or select or modify; (iii) it observes the due diligence prescribed by the Intermediary Guidelines Rules; (iv) it has not conspired, abetted, aided or induced; and (v) on receiving actual knowledge — a court order or a government notification — it removes or disables access expeditiously, without vitiating evidence. The My Space relief — an updated catalogue of specific works with URLs, take-down within 36 hours, and accounts kept for damages — is the model.

Conclusion. The safe harbour is available to a passive offshore player only if the Indian court has jurisdiction over it (which requires targeting, not mere accessibility), the s.79(2) conditions are satisfied, the due diligence prescribed by the Rules is observed, none of the s.79(3) exceptions applies, and actual knowledge is acted on expeditiously. The safe harbour is an affirmative defence, not a blanket immunity.
Exercise 7DU self-learning exercise printed after My Space · p. 125

"Due diligence under section 79 of the information technology act has been narrowed whereas the safe harbour rule is wide. Analyse with reasons in support of the statement. [Hint — Amazon Seller Services Private Limited v. Amway India Enterprises Private Limited, Del (DB) decided on Jan 31, 2020.]"

Model answer

The statement, and the two readings of it. The proposition can be analysed in two ways, and a good answer should acknowledge both.

Reading 1 — the safe harbour is drafted widely; the due diligence obligation is drawn narrowly. Section 79(1) is drafted in the widest possible terms: "Notwithstanding anything contained in any law for the time being in force but subject to the provisions of sub-sections (2) and (3), an intermediary shall not be liable for any third party information, data, or communication link made available or hosted by him." The My Space Division Bench said exactly this: "irrespective of any other law, an intermediary is guaranteed a safe harbor and the only restriction comes from Section 79(2) and (3)." Against that, the due diligence obligation in s.79(2)(c) is not defined in the Act at all — it is delegated to "such other guidelines as the Central Government may prescribe". The Intermediary Guidelines Rules, 2011 (now the 2021 Rules) narrow it to a specific, achievable list: publishing user terms and conditions prohibiting the enumerated categories of information (Rule 3(2)), and acting on actual knowledge within the prescribed time (Rule 3(4)). The due diligence obligation is therefore narrow, specific and knowable; the safe harbour it earns is wide.

Reading 2 — the trigger for due diligence has been narrowed. The scope of what an intermediary must do has been narrowed by judicial and legislative development. Shreya Singhal held that "actual knowledge" under s.69A means a court order or a government notification — not general awareness or private complaints. The My Space Division Bench extended the point to copyright: s.51(a)(ii) "in the case of internet intermediaries contemplates actual knowledge and not general awareness". And the Court expressly rejected the notion that an intermediary must itself identify infringing content from among millions of uploads: to require that would be "not only impractical but ... dangerous", would amount to "unwarranted private censorship", and would chill free speech. The obligation is therefore narrowed to acting on specified notice, not monitoring.

The Amazon v. Amway hint. In Amazon Seller Services Pvt. Ltd. v. Amway India Enterprises Pvt. Ltd. (Del, DB, 31 January 2020) the Division Bench held that an e-commerce marketplace is an intermediary within s.2(1)(w) and is entitled to the safe harbour in respect of goods sold on its platform by third-party sellers. The decision supports the statement because it confirms that (i) the safe harbour is wide enough to cover even the largest online marketplaces, and (ii) the intermediary's due diligence obligation does not extend to policing the listings of third-party sellers or to liability for their acts, absent actual knowledge and failure to act. The safe harbour is wide; the diligence is narrow; and the gap between them is the intermediary's protection.

The limits of the proposition — reasons against. First, s.79 is "neither an enforcement provision nor does it list out any penal consequences"; it "provides for an affirmative defence and not a blanket immunity". Second, the proviso to s.81 preserves copyright and patent remedies against intermediaries, so the safe harbour is not available at all where the intermediary itself posts infringing content. Third, the due diligence obligation has in fact been expanded by the 2021 Rules, which impose significant obligations on "significant social media intermediaries" — so the "narrowing" is not uniform. Fourth, the safe harbour is lost entirely under s.79(3)(a) if the intermediary has "conspired or abetted or aided or induced" the unlawful act — a wide exclusion.

Conclusion. The statement is supportable on both readings: the safe harbour in s.79(1) is drafted in the widest terms and, as My Space held, is guaranteed "irrespective of any other law"; while the due diligence obligation in s.79(2)(c), as prescribed by the Rules and as narrowed by Shreya Singhal and My Space to action on actual knowledge, is narrow and specific. Amazon v. Amway confirms that even the largest marketplace is within the wide safe harbour. But the safe harbour is an affirmative defence, not a blanket immunity, and the proviso to s.81 and the 2021 Rules qualify the proposition.

"The purpose of captcha has been to draw distinction between access to humans and machines. How captcha are able to resolve the intermediary liability issues, in case the objectionable content is shared by Malware Program."

Model answer

The problem. Where objectionable content is shared not by a human user but by a malware program (a bot), the intermediary's notice-and-take-down machinery faces two difficulties: the volume of shares may be enormous and automated, and the "user" cannot be identified or terminated. The intermediary may be said to have "actual knowledge" only of the symptom, not of the source.

What a CAPTCHA does. A CAPTCHA ("Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart") is a challenge-response test designed to be answerable by a human and not by a machine. By requiring a successful response before an action can be completed, a CAPTCHA interposes a human gate between the automated program and the platform.

How it bears on intermediary liability. Four points:

  1. It restores the human actor, and with it the s.79(2)(b) analysis. Where content is shared by a human who has passed a CAPTCHA, the intermediary can say that it did not initiate the transmission, did not select the receiver, and did not select or modify the information. Where the share is machine-generated, the "transmission" is arguably not initiated by a third party at all, which weakens the safe-harbour analysis.
  2. It converts general awareness into identifiable knowledge. Section 79(3)(b) requires the intermediary to act on actual knowledge. A CAPTCHA-gated action produces an attributable, traceable event — the specific account, the specific timestamp, the specific URL — which is exactly what the My Space relief requires: "an updated catalogue of 'specific' works ... along with the location/URL of such work".
  3. It is part of "due diligence" under s.79(2)(c). Deploying CAPTCHA is a reasonable, proportionate technical measure — one of the four OECD steps the My Space Court identified (notice and take down; notice and notice; notice and disconnection; filtering). It is the intermediary's answer to the charge that it "did not move its little finger" (as the Google India v. Visaka Industries court put it when refusing the safe harbour).
  4. It does not create a duty to monitor. This is the crucial limit. CAPTCHA is a filtering measure applied at the point of action, not a general obligation to scan content. My Space held that requiring an intermediary to identify infringing content from millions of uploads "could have a chilling effect on free speech" and would amount to "unwarranted private censorship". So CAPTCHA helps because it is targeted and action-linked, not because it surveils.
Conclusion. CAPTCHA resolves intermediary liability issues in malware-driven sharing by re-establishing the human actor, producing the specific and traceable knowledge that s.79(3)(b) requires, and constituting proportionate due diligence of the "filtering" kind — without converting the intermediary into a general monitor of content, which My Space and Shreya Singhal forbid.

"Does the Virtual Private Network used for Cyber Security while storing data for 5 years create Privacy Issues in Cyberspace?"

Model answer

Step 1 — What a VPN does. A Virtual Private Network encrypts the user's traffic and routes it through a remote server, so that the user's IP address and location are concealed from the destinations visited and, often, from the user's own ISP. It is a legitimate cyber-security tool — it is used by corporations, journalists and, in India, by CERT-In's own advisories for securing remote access.

Step 2 — The two privacy interests in tension. (i) The user's interest in anonymity and location privacy. A VPN protects the user's identity and location from the sites visited and from network observers. (ii) The countervailing interest in attribution. The same feature frustrates the attribution of electronic records under s.11 IT Act (attribution to the originator, including by an information system programmed to operate automatically), the investigation of offences, and the identification of the person behind unlawful content — the very identification that Banyan Tree's "purposeful availment" test and Maqbool Fida Husain's jurisdictional analysis require.

Step 3 — The five-year retention problem. Storing VPN logs for five years creates privacy issues on four axes. First, purpose limitation: logs retained for cyber security are inevitably available for other purposes. Second, function creep and surveillance: s.69 IT Act permits interception, monitoring or decryption in the interest of the sovereignty and integrity of India, defence, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States or public order, or for preventing incitement to the commission of a cognizable offence, or for the investigation of any offence — subject to the procedure and safeguards prescribed. Five-year retention makes that power far more potent. Third, the intermediary's own position: s.67C IT Act obliges intermediaries to preserve and retain information as prescribed for such duration as may be prescribed; and s.2(1)(w) expressly includes VPN and network service providers within "intermediary", so the s.79 safe harbour analysis applies to them. Fourth, data localisation and cross-border transfer: retention in India versus abroad raises the territorial questions that this Unit is about.

Step 4 — The legal framework. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the Rules notified under it now govern the processing of personal data, including by virtue of the obligations they impose on data fiduciaries in respect of purpose limitation, storage limitation and security safeguards. The Puttaswamy line of decisions establishes that privacy is a fundamental right and that any invasion must satisfy legality, legitimate aim, proportionality and procedural safeguards. The answer must therefore balance the security justification against those requirements.

Conclusion. Yes — the use of a VPN for cyber security, coupled with five-year data retention, creates real privacy issues: it conceals the user's identity and location while simultaneously building a detailed, long-lived record of users' activity that is available for surveillance and for attribution. The issues are to be resolved by applying the DPDP Act 2023, the Puttaswamy proportionality test, and the safeguards in ss.67C and 69 IT Act — and, in this Unit's terms, by asking which State's law governs the retained data and where the computer resource holding it is located.

16.1 The revision card

THE ONE SENTENCE

The Banyan Tree test

"For the purposes of Section 20 (c) CPC, in order to show that some part of the cause of action has arisen in the forum state by the use of the internet by the Defendant the Plaintiff will have to show prima facie that the said website, whether euphemistically termed as 'passive plus' or 'interactive', was specifically targeted at viewers in the forum state for commercial transactions."

16.2 Common exam mistakes — avoid every one of these

  • Saying "the website is accessible in Delhi, so the Delhi court has jurisdiction." That is Casio, overruled. Never say it without qualification.
  • Blurring "effects" as a basis of jurisdiction with "effects" as an element of the cause of action. They are different steps in the analysis.
  • Treating WWE as authority that a company with no office in the forum carries on business there. Big Tree holds the opposite, and WWE itself did not decide that question.
  • Citing s.134(2) TMA in a passing-off action. Section 134(2) is expressly limited to clauses (a) and (b) of s.134(1) — infringement and rights in a registered mark.
  • Treating s.3 IPC as a free-standing grant of jurisdiction. It presupposes a law making the person "liable ... to be tried".
  • Omitting the Central Government's sanction under the proviso to s.188 CrPC for offences committed outside India.
  • Confusing s.4 IT Act with s.4 IPC. One is about electronic records; the other is extraterritorial jurisdiction.
  • Confusing s.13(3) IT Act (deemed place of receipt) with s.13(2) (time of receipt) or s.13(1) (time of despatch).
  • Answering "private international law" by listing Latin maxims and stopping. Show how Indian courts built the targeting test into s.20(c) CPC.
  • Confusing the order of the plaint with the rejection of the plaint. Return is Order VII Rule 10; rejection is Order VII Rule 11; both are different from rejection under Order VII Rule 6 (which follows rejection under Rule 11).
  • Forgetting that Banyan Tree did not itself decide that the Delhi court lacked jurisdiction — it remitted the question to the single Judge.
  • Citing Impresario, Big Tree or P.R. Transport Agency as if they were printed in the DU case material. They are not; flag the source.

16.3 Model exam conclusion

✍️ A conclusion you can reproduce verbatim in a 15-mark question

"The internet does not abolish territorial jurisdiction; it dislocates the facts on which territorial jurisdiction depends. Indian law has responded, on the civil side, by purposively construing s.20(c) of the Code of Civil Procedure so as to import the common law principle of purposeful availment, and by rejecting both the accessibility rule of Casio and the pure effects test of Inset, Gutnick and Yahoo!. The result is the Banyan Tree test: the plaintiff must show prima facie that the defendant's website, whether 'passive plus' or 'interactive', was specifically targeted at viewers in the forum State for commercial transactions, that some such transaction was entered into with a forum user, and that the targeting caused injury or harm to the plaintiff there. Trap transactions are scrutinised with jealousy and a solitary trap transaction cannot found jurisdiction. Where the plaintiff is a corporation, the Explanation to s.20 CPC requires an office; and where the service can be availed only by physical presence, the transaction is completed where the service is availed. On the criminal side, Chapter XIII of the CrPC gives every magistrate jurisdiction over a single internet publication, and the only available controls are the magistrate's scrutiny under s.202 and transfer under s.406 — a gap the Court itself has asked the legislature to fill. Extraterritorial criminal jurisdiction exists, but only within the limits Parliament has set: s.1(2) and s.75 of the IT Act, which require the act or conduct to involve a computer, computer system or computer network located in India, read with s.3 and s.4(3) of the IPC and s.188 of the CrPC. The law is therefore settled in principle but remains, in the view of the Delhi High Court itself, in need of legislative completion."
