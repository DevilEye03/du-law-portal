🎯 THE ONE IDEA THAT CARRIES THIS ENTIRE UNIT

Chapter XI of the IT Act, 2000 is India's cyber‑crime code. Its central architectural device is that s.66 is textually parasitic on s.43: the ten civil acts of unauthorised interference enumerated in s.43 become criminal the moment they are done "dishonestly or fraudulently". Everything else in Chapter XI is a set of specialised, escalating offences layered on top of that base:

  • s.65 — tampering with computer source code (the intellectual‑property offence).
  • s.66 — hacking: any s.43 act done dishonestly or fraudulently.
  • ss.66C, 66D — identity theft and cheating by personation (credential and identity misuse; phishing and e‑mail spoofing).
  • s.66E — violation of privacy (capturing/publishing images of a private area without consent).
  • s.66F — cyber terrorism — the gravest offence in the Act, punishable with imprisonment for life.
  • ss.67, 67A, 67B — a graduated obscenity ladder: general obscenity → sexually explicit acts → child sexual exploitation and abuse material (CSEAM).

If you remember one sentence for the exam: ask (1) which enumerated act was done, (2) with what mental element, (3) upon what subject matter, and (4) to whom it was published — and then locate the correct provision on the ladder. The same facts can simultaneously attract civil compensation (ss.43/43A), IT Act offences (Chapter XI) and ordinary IPC/BNS offences, and all three tracks run concurrently.

📍 Three things to keep straight before you start
  • Civil and criminal run in parallel — they are not alternatives. The same conduct can attract compensation under ss.43/43A before the Adjudicating Officer and punishment under Chapter XI before a magistrate. The AO has no criminal jurisdiction; the magistrate has no jurisdiction to award compensation. A finding of contravention under s.43 is not a finding of guilt, and vice versa.
  • Chapter XI is a special criminal code — read it with the general law. Every IT Act offence is charged alongside IPC (now BNS, 2023) provisions: cheating (s.318 BNS), forgery (ss.336/338 BNS), defamation (s.356 BNS), stalking (s.78 BNS), outraging modesty (s.79 BNS), obscenity (ss.294/296 BNS). Examiners now expect the IPC → BNS cross‑reference in every criminal answer.
  • Currency. All sections are cited in their post‑2008‑amendment form. The 2008 Amendment inserted ss.66A–66F and 67A–67C and rewrote ss.66 and 67. s.66A was struck down in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, (2015) 5 SCC 1 — never cite it as an available charge. The BNS, 2023 replaced the IPC with effect from 1 July 2024; the Bhartiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (s.63) governs the electronic evidence on which every prosecution depends.

1The mischief: cyber crimes as a distinct offence category

Syllabus item 1  Unit IV covers offences — conduct visited with imprisonment and/or fine — as distinct from Unit III's compensatory civil wrongs. The syllabus's parenthetical, "s.43 read with s.66; ss.65–67B", is a map of the whole Unit: s.43 supplies the acts, s.66 supplies the mental element that converts them into crimes, and ss.65–67B supply the specialised offences that stand independently.

1.1 Why a separate cyber‑crime code?

🔍 The mischief the 2000 Act addressed

The Indian Penal Code, 1860 was drafted in 1860 for a physical world of hands, paper and witnesses. It could reach cheating by personation (s.419 IPC) but only where a person personated another in flesh and blood; it could reach forgery (s.463 IPC) but only of a document; it could reach theft (s.378 IPC) but only of movable property taken out of possession. A thief who copies a database and leaves the original untouched, or a forger who alters a ledger through a terminal with a valid but unauthorised login, or an impersonator who opens a Yahoo account in a woman's name, all fall outside the classic offences. Chapter XI supplies the missing acts, the missing mental elements and the missing punishments — and, critically, the definitional bridge (s.2(1)) that makes a mobile phone a "computer" and an ESN "computer source code".

1.3 The civil / criminal overlap — the point examiners test most

⚠️ The single most important structural point in Unit IV

The same primary conduct frequently attracts both regimes simultaneously. Unauthorised access is civilly actionable under s.43 (compensation before the Adjudicating Officer, up to ₹5 crore) and may separately constitute the offence of hacking under s.66 (up to 3 years / ₹5 lakh, before a magistrate). In Sanjay Kumar v. State of Haryana the accused was convicted under ss.65 and 66 IT Act and ss.420, 467, 468, 471 IPC on the same facts. In Syed Asifuddin the Court expressly noted that the same ESN tampering attracted s.65 and could, if alleged, attract s.66 as well as s.63 of the Copyright Act. Never treat the tracks as alternatives.

✅ Real examples — the mental element decides the charge
  • A journalist who accesses a publicly available government database with a password that was inadvertently left in a press release — no dishonesty, no fraudulence: at most civil liability under s.43, and even that is doubtful. No s.66 offence.
  • A call‑centre agent who, using a customer's accidentally‑visible OTP, books a flight in the customer's name — dishonest (wrongful gain to self, wrongful loss to other): s.66 + s.66D, and IPC s.415 / BNS s.318 cheating.
  • A developer who deliberately inserts a backdoor into a client's payroll software and conceals it — intentional concealment and alteration of source code: s.65 (see Sanjay Kumar).
  • A teenager who downloads a file shared in a group chat without knowing its content, which turns out to be CSEAM — the mens rea question is unresolved; see the critique at §19.2.

⚠️ Two provisions that are dead or rarely used — say so in the exam
  • s.66A — struck down. In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, (2015) 5 SCC 1 (Supreme Court, 24 March 2015), the Supreme Court struck down s.66A as unconstitutionally vague and overbroad — it criminalised sending "grossly offensive" or "menacing" information through a computer resource. 2.2 The other statutes that routinely accompany a Chapter XI charge

📚 Companion statutes
  • Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 (POCSO) — s.15 criminalises storage/possession of child sexual abuse material for commercial or transmission purposes; read with s.67B IT Act. Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish (2024) held that mere possession is independently punishable under both.
  • Copyright Act, 1957 — s.51(a)(ii) (permitting a place to be used for communication of a work to the public); s.63 (offence of infringement); s.63B (knowing use of an infringing copy of a computer programme). The IPR limb of the syllabus.
  • Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 — the money‑laundering limb of the syllabus; s.43 of the IT Act supplies the "scheduled offence" predicate.
  • Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 — the terrorism limb; runs alongside s.66F.
  • Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 — s.63 (the successor to Evidence Act s.65B): the dual certificate (device custodian + expert) plus hash value is a condition precedent to admissibility.
  • Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, as amended in 2026 — labelling and takedown duties for synthetically generated information (deepfakes), directly bearing on ss.66D, 67, 67A, 67B prosecutions.
🔍 The international comparator

The Budapest Convention on Cybercrime, 2001 — to which India is not a signatory — is the global template. Its offence taxonomy closely parallels ss.65–74 of the IT Act: (i) illegal access; (ii) illegal interception; (iii) data interference; (iv) system interference; (v) misuse of devices; (vi) computer‑related forgery; (vii) computer‑related fraud; (viii) offences related to child pornography; (ix) offences related to infringements of copyright and related rights. Use this in a comparative paragraph: "India's Chapter XI anticipates the Budapest taxonomy, though India has declined to accede to the Convention, citing sovereignty concerns over cross‑border access and mutual assistance."

3Tampering with computer source code — s.65

Syllabus item 1  The IPR limb of the syllabus. Section 65 is the Act's original offence — present in the unamended 2000 Act — and it protects the intellectual property invested in computer source code beyond what copyright law provides.

s.65 · TAMPERING WITH COMPUTER SOURCE DOCUMENTS · VERBATIM

Tampering with computer source documents

Whoever knowingly or intentionally conceals, destroys or alters or intentionally or knowingly causes another to conceal, destroy, or alter any computer source code used for a computer, computer programme, computer system or computer network, when the computer source code is required to be kept or maintained by law for the time being in force, shall be punishable with imprisonment up to three years, or with fine which may extend up to two lakh rupees, or with both.
Note the disjunctive "or": the code must be "required to be kept or maintained by law". Syed Asifuddin held that this is a disjunctive test — either situation suffices, and they are "different situations". This is the single most important textual point in the section.
📜 Explanation to s.65 — the definition

For the purposes of this section, "computer source code" means the listing of programmes, computer commands, design and layout and programme analysis of computer resource in any form.

3.2 What is "computer source code"? The four limbs

⚖️ Syed Asifuddin on the four limbs of the definition

"By the very definition of 'computer source code,' a) list of programmes; b) computer commands; (c) design and layout and d) programme analysis of computer resource in any form, is a 'computer source code' for the purpose of Section 65 of I.‑T. Act." On that basis the Court held that the ESN (Electronic Serial Number) of a Samsung N191 or LG‑2030 handset and the SID (System Identification Code) of the service provider come within the definition of computer source code.

✅ Real examples — what counts as computer source code
  • ESN and SID in a mobile handset — Syed Asifuddin (expressly held to be computer source code).
  • The payroll module's source code of a company's software — Sanjay Kumar (altering the bank's software and interest entries).
  • Firmware and bootloader of a device — the "listing of programmes ... in any form".
  • Design and layout documents and programme analysis — e.g. the schema and log files of a banking software, which Sanjay Kumar's trial court found the accused had tampered with and deleted using SRF files.
  • NOT merely a data entry or a record of a transaction — that is "information residing in a computer resource" (s.43(i) / s.66), not source code.

3.3 Section 65 and the Copyright Act, 1957 — the IPR limb

🔍 Two overlapping regimes — plead both

In Syed Asifuddin the Court held that a computer programme is by very definition an original literary work under s.2(o) of the Copyright Act, 1957, protected by ss.13 and 14(b), and that infringement is punishable under s.63. "Therefore, prima facie, if a person alters computer programme of another person or another computer company, the same would be infringement of the copyright." The Court was careful to add that "the entire issue in this regard is subject to the evidence that may be led by the complainant at the time of trial" — the observations were not intended to decide the question one way or the other. Note also s.63B of the Copyright Act, inserted in 1994, which makes knowing use of an infringing copy of a computer programme an offence punishable with up to 3 years' imprisonment and fine up to ₹2 lakh. A complete answer on IPR‑related cyber crime must address both s.65 of the IT Act and ss.51/63/63B of the Copyright Act.

4Hacking and computer‑related offences — s.66

Syllabus item 1  The syllabus's "Hacking (s.43(i) read with s.66)" describes the whole architecture. Post‑2008, s.66 no longer independently defines "hacking" — it is drafted as a pure mental‑element gateway onto s.43.

s.66 · COMPUTER RELATED OFFENCES · VERBATIM (POST‑2008)

Computer related offences

If any person, dishonestly or fraudulently, does any act referred to in section 43, he shall be punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three years or with fine which may extend to five lakh rupees or with both.
Explanation. For the purposes of this section— (i) the word "dishonestly" shall have the meaning assigned to it in section 24 of the Indian Penal Code; (ii) the word "fraudulently" shall have the meaning assigned to it in section 25 of the Indian Penal Code. (Read with the BNS, 2023: s.2(7) and s.2(9) respectively.)
⚠️ The pre‑2008 version — know the difference

Before the 2008 Amendment, s.66 defined hacking itself: "Whoever with the intent to cause or knowing that he is likely to cause wrongful loss or damage to the public or any person destroys or deletes or alters any information residing in a computer resource or diminishes its value or utility or affects it injuriously by any means, commits hacking" — punishable with up to 3 years or ₹2 lakh. The pre‑2008 version is the one Syed Asifuddin (2005) quotes and analyses. In the exam, note the amendment expressly: "Section 66 as it stood at the time of Syed Asifuddin independently defined hacking; the 2008 Amendment recast it as a gateway onto the s.43 acts, importing the IPC definitions of 'dishonestly' and 'fraudulently'."

4.3 The investigative chain — how a hacking prosecution is actually proved

⚖️ The chain Prabhakar Sampath illustrates — memorise the six steps
  1. IP address identified from the complainant's server logs — here 61.95.152.145.
  2. ISP subscriber records obtained — the DSP addressed a letter to Bharti Airtel's manager, who verified and disclosed that the IP was allotted to M/s Frost & Sullivan Pvt. Ltd., Chennai.
  3. Search warrant obtained from the court; search conducted at the accused's residence in the presence of mediators (panchas).
  4. Seizure — a pen drive containing six .pdf files (Anticoagulants, Bulk_Paclitaxel, CNS_Therapeutics, Human_Vaccines, Microplate_instrumentation_Supplies, Therapeutic_Aphersis), and later the accused's office computer system S605.
  5. Arrest and disclosure statement — the accused was arrested on 29.12.2008 and his statement recorded.
  6. Forensic analysis — the pen drive and CPU were sent to APFSL, Hyderabad, whose expert (PW‑8) opined that both contained the six downloaded files and data pertaining to the IP address.
✅ Real examples — s.66 in everyday Indian fact patterns
  • An ex‑employee logs into the company CRM with a still‑active account and exports the client list — s.43(a)+(b) done dishonestly → s.66; if he used the credentials as if they were his own, s.66C.
  • A fraudster gains access to a net‑banking account using a duplicated SIM and transfers money — s.43(a) dishonestly → s.66, plus s.66D and BNS s.318.
  • A competitor's employee uses a packet sniffer on office Wi‑Fi to capture a rival's e‑mails — s.43(b) → s.66 (and, in the Rakesh cyber‑terrorism variant, potentially s.66F).
  • A student defaces a college website and deletes its database — s.43(d)+(e)+(i) → s.66; if the motive is ideological, consider s.66F.
  • A person sends an e‑mail bomb to a former employer's server, causing a denial of service — s.43(e)+(f) → s.66 (email bombing is expressly a syllabus item).
⚠️ Common mistake — confusing s.66 with s.66C, and forgetting the s.43 clause

Section 66 requires an act referred to in s.43 — it is not a free‑standing "unauthorised access" offence. Always name the s.43 clause. And s.66C is a different offence: it requires fraudulent or dishonest use of another person's electronic signature, password or other unique identification feature — it does not require any of the s.43 acts. A person who uses a stolen password to log in has committed s.66C even if he does nothing further.

5Identity theft and cheating by personation — ss.66C, 66D

Syllabus item 1  Both sections were inserted by the 2008 Amendment. They are the credential and identity offences — the statutory home of phishing, e‑mail spoofing and password theft, which the syllabus expressly names.

s.66C · PUNISHMENT FOR IDENTITY THEFT · VERBATIM

Punishment for identity theft

Whoever, fraudulently or dishonestly makes use of the electronic signature, password or any other unique identification feature of any other person, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three years and shall also be liable to fine which may extend to rupees one lakh.
Three things to note: (i) the object is another person's credential — using your own is not identity theft; (ii) the mental element is fraudulent or dishonest use; (iii) the offence is complete on the use — no further act of damage, disclosure or gain is required. This is the provision that catches the duplicate‑SIM fraudster and the password thief.
s.66D · PUNISHMENT FOR CHEATING BY PERSONATION BY USING COMPUTER RESOURCE · VERBATIM

Punishment for cheating by personation by using computer resource

Whoever, by means of any communication device or computer resource cheats by personation, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three years and shall also be liable to fine which may extend to one lakh rupees.
"Personation" means pretending to be another person, by word or conduct, with intent to deceive. Read with IPC s.416 (now BNS s.330) for the definition and IPC s.419 (now BNS s.331) for the aggravated offence of cheating by personation. The distinguishing feature of s.66D is the medium: the personation is effected "by means of any communication device or computer resource".

🔍 "Unique identification feature" — how wide is it?

The phrase is deliberately open‑textured. It plainly covers passwords, PINs, OTPs, e‑mail credentials, digital signatures and private keys, Aadhaar numbers, biometric templates and device identifiers. In practice, the courts and the police treat it as covering anything that authenticates a person to a system — which is why the same provision catches a stolen banking password and a misappropriated Aadhaar number. In an essay, note the overlap with s.3A of the Act (electronic authentication, including biometrics) and with s.42 (control of the private key) from Unit II: the integrity of attribution in Unit II is the same fact that grounds identity‑theft liability in Unit IV.

5.2 Phishing — the anatomy, and the law that now catches it

⚖️ Nandrajog J.'s definition — NAASCOM v. Ajay Sood, 119 (2005) DLT 596

"'Phishing' is a form of internet fraud. In a case of 'Phishing', a person pretending to be a legitimate association such as a bank or an insurance company in order to extract personal data from a user such as access codes, passwords etc. which are then used to his own advantage, misrepresents on the identity of the legitimate party. Typically 'Phishing' scams involve persons who pretend to represent online banks and siphon cash from e‑banking accounts after conning consumers into handing over confidential banking details." The Court added: "The internet these days is full of scams. E‑mail that form the basis of phishing attacks and pose as a security check. These messages trick users into handing over their account details and passwords. The quoted details are subsequently used for fraudulent transfers."

🔍 The historical point — why this case matters so much

In 2005 the Court found — and said expressly — "I find no legislation in India on 'phishing'", holding that such an act "would be a mis‑representation made in the course of trade leading to confusion as to the source and origin of the e‑mail causing immense harm not only to the consumer but even the person whose name, identity or password is misused", and would also be an act of passing off. The 2008 Amendment then inserted ss.66C and 66D. NAASCOM is therefore the doctrinal ancestor of India's statutory phishing offences — a strong "historical development" opener for any question on identity theft. Use it to show the evolution from common‑law passing‑off remedies to codified cyber‑offences.

✅ Real examples — which section bites
  • An SMS says "Your KYC is expired; share OTP to reactivate" — the recipient shares the OTP and money is transferred: the sender commits s.66C (using the OTP — the "unique identification feature") and s.66D, plus s.66 and BNS s.318.
  • A fake NASSCOM‑branded recruitment e‑mail harvests CVs: s.66D; the sender commits passing off and, if trade marks are used, offences under the Trade Marks Act, 1999.
  • A person opens an account in a relative's name using the relative's Aadhaar and PAN without consent: s.66C (use of another's unique identification features) plus forgery under BNS ss.336/338.
  • A colleague logs into a shared server using a co‑worker's password because his own is locked out, with no dishonest purpose: no s.66C offence — the use is neither fraudulent nor dishonest.
  • A deepfake video call impersonating a CFO instructs a clerk to transfer funds: s.66D (cheating by personation through a communication device), read with the 2026 Intermediary Guidelines Amendment on synthetically generated information.

5.3 Financial frauds — the money‑laundering and credit‑card limb

🔍 The statutes that travel with a s.66/66C/66D charge

The syllabus expressly names financial frauds (money laundering, credit card frauds). A complete answer must note: (i) the IT Act offences (ss.66, 66C, 66D); (ii) BNS s.318 (cheating) and BNS ss.336/338 (forgery); (iii) the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002, under which the IT Act offence supplies the scheduled offence predicate for attachment and confiscation of proceeds; (iv) the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007 and RBI's card‑fraud and customer‑liability circulars; and (v) where a card is used, ss.66C and 66D plus the debit/credit card misuse provisions. In Sanjay Kumar the trial court also relied on the RBI's KYC/AML Master Circular to establish the standard of care.

Syllabus item 1  The syllabus's "Obscenity and Pornography (ss.66E, 67, 67A, 67B read with POCSO Act) s.292 IPC / s.294 Bharatiya Nyaya Samhita, 2023" is a graduated ladder. Getting the rung right is most of the answer.

s.66E · PUNISHMENT FOR VIOLATION OF PRIVACY · VERBATIM

Punishment for violation of privacy

Whoever, intentionally or knowingly captures, publishes or transmits the image of a private area of any person without his or her consent, under circumstances violating the privacy of that person, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may not extend to three years or with fine not exceeding two lakh rupees, or with both.
Explanation. For the purposes of this section— (a) "transmit" means to cause to be sent to any person; (b) "capture" means to include taking a photograph, video or other visual representation, either analog or digital, of the private area of a person; (c) "private area" means the naked or undergarment clad genitals, pubic area, buttocks or anus of a person or, in the case of a female, her exposed nipples or the naked or undergarment clad breasts; (d) "publish" means reproduction in the printed or electronic form and making it available for public.
🔍 The key structural point — s.66E has no publication requirement for the core offence

Section 66E punishes capturing, publishing OR transmitting an image of a private area without consent. Capturing alone (e.g. a hidden camera in a changing room) is enough. This is what distinguishes s.66E from ss.67/67A/67B, which all require publication or transmission of material that is obscene or sexually explicit. It is also gender‑neutral — unlike IPC s.354C (voyeurism), which protects only women. Compare the point with the DU syllabus's express inclusion of s.66E in the obscenity group.

Syllabus items 2–3  Two very different offences: one common‑law/statutory hybrid with no dedicated IT Act provision, and one the gravest offence in the entire Act.

8.1 Cyber stalking — ss.354D and 509 IPC (now BNS ss.78 and 79)

🔍 The doctrinal point — there is no dedicated IT Act provision for stalking

After the fall of s.66A in Shreya Singhal (2015), the IT Act has no general online‑harassment offence. Cyber stalking is prosecuted through a cluster: IPC s.354D (now BNS s.78), which expressly includes a man "monitoring a woman's use of the internet, email or any other form of electronic communication"; read with IPC s.509 (now BNS s.79) for words, gestures or acts intended to insult the modesty of a woman; and, where applicable, s.66E (privacy), s.67/s.67A (if obscene or sexually explicit material is involved) and s.43 civilly. The Ritu Kohli case (Delhi) — one of India's earliest reported cyber‑stalking complaints, pre‑dating the 2008 Amendment — remains the illustrative foundational reference, though it was prosecuted at a time when the statutory toolkit was far thinner.

8.2 Cyber terrorism — s.66F

s.66F · PUNISHMENT FOR CYBER TERRORISM · VERBATIM

Punishment for cyber terrorism

(1) Whoever—
(A) with intent to threaten the unity, integrity, security or sovereignty of India or to strike terror in the people or any section of the people, by—
  (i) denying or causing the denial of access to any person authorised to access a computer resource; or
  (ii) attempting to penetrate or access a computer resource without authorisation or exceeding authorised access; or
  (iii) introducing or causing to introduce any computer contaminant,
and by means of such conduct causes or is likely to cause death or injuries to persons or damage to or destruction of property or disrupts or is likely to disrupt the supplies or services essential to the life of the community; or
(B) knowingly or intentionally penetrates or accesses a computer resource without authorisation or exceeding authorised access, and by means of such conduct obtains access to information, data or computer database that is restricted for reasons of the security of the State or foreign relations; or any restricted information, data or computer database, with reasons to believe that such information, data or computer database so obtained may be used to cause or likely to cause injury to the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, or in relation to contempt of court, defamation or incitement to an offence or to the advantage of any foreign nation,
commits the offence of cyber terrorism.
(2) Whoever commits or conspires to commit cyber terrorism shall be punishable with imprisonment for life.
The gravest offence in the Act. Cognizable and non‑bailable. Note the two limbs: limb (A) requires intent to threaten the unity, integrity, security or sovereignty of India or to strike terror, plus one of three enumerated means, plus the consequence (death, injury, property damage or disruption of essential supplies or services — actual or likely). Limb (B) is an information‑acquisition offence with no consequence requirement, but requires knowledge/intention and "reasons to believe" the information may be used to injure India's interests.
🔍 The "computer contaminant" — a defined term you must know

Section 66F(iii) refers to a "computer contaminant", defined in s.43 Explanation (b) as "any set of computer instructions that is designed—(a) to modify, destroy, record or transmit data or information residing within a computer, computer system or computer network without the intent of the owner of the data; or (b) by some other means to usurp the normal operation of the computer, computer system or computer network." Viruses, worms, Trojan horses, ransomware, logic bombs and botnet agents are all computer contaminants. Introducing one is civilly actionable under s.43(c), criminally under s.66, and — if the intent and consequence limbs are met — s.66F.

⚠️ Common mistake — the breadth of limb (B)

Limb (B) has been criticised as vague and overbroad, echoing in miniature the vagueness concerns that led to s.66A's invalidation in Shreya Singhal. The phrase "reasons to believe that such information ... may be used to cause or likely to cause injury to the interests of" a very long list of State interests, including "decency or morality" and "defamation", sweeps in conduct that would otherwise be ordinary hacking. Flag this in a critical answer: "Limb (B) of s.66F imports a consequence‑free, belief‑based standard that sits uneasily with the constitutional requirement of definiteness in criminal law, and its interaction with the Shreya Singhal vagueness doctrine remains a live tension."

2Admission of Facts

The crime was registered on a written complaint given by the Head of the Sales and Marketing Wing of M/s. Reliance Infocomm Ltd., Hyderabad. The complaint alleged that vested elements of the mobile telephone trade had begun to woo Reliance's subscribers away, damaging its image and revenues.

The commercial background: Reliance Infocomm, under the Dhirubhai Ambani Pioneer Offer, launched "Reliance India Mobile" (RIM) to make communication affordable. The scheme offered a third‑generation digital handset worth ₹10,500 together with a service bundle for three years on an initial payment of ₹3,350 and a monthly outflow of ₹600, with a one‑year warranty and insurance for three years. The market response in the twin cities was phenomenal — and this had an impact on the business of other service providers, who, it was alleged, "attempted unethical and illegal practices for weaning away the subscribers".

The modus operandi: subscribers of Reliance were attracted by phone calls from unknown persons in Abids, Begumpet, Koti, Himayatnagar and Malakpet, who represented that the tariff plans of other providers were better and that they could shift providers by paying ₹3,000 towards plan charges and deposits of ₹540 towards activation fee. Once a subscriber agreed, he was asked to meet a business associate of the rival provider, who would take the handset to an undisclosed location in Secunderabad for a "conversion process" lasting 45 minutes to an hour and a half. During this time, the ESN number of the Reliance instrument was hacked by reprogramming. The handset was then returned with instructions to switch it off and on later in the day and start enjoying the new services.

Investigation: the crime was registered on 31 May 2003. Investigation revealed that Reliance handsets were being migrated to the TATA Indicom network at the behest of TATA Indicom staff. A raid was conducted at the TATA Indicom head office at Khan Lathif Khan Estate, Hyderabad, and later at a TATA Indicom centre opposite Harihara Kala Bhavan, Secunderabad, where one Raj Naren, an officer of TATA Indicom, disclosed that the General Manager (Marketing), Madhavan, and Anil Ambati, Manager (Marketing), were accepting Reliance handsets and re‑programming them with their network with different tariff packages. Syed Asifuddin, Patlay Navin Kumar, Khaja/Gareed Nawaj and Manoj were arrested, and two Samsung N191 handsets co‑branded with Reliance, re‑programmed with distinct ESN and serial numbers, were seized, along with 63 application forms of persons who had migrated from Reliance to TATA Indicom, together with affidavits. One handset recovered from Raj Naren was a Samsung N191 co‑branded with Reliance with ESN No. 3F7AB 832, migrated to TATA Indicom with No. 56376361 allotted by TATA Indicom, its original Reliance number being 31086523.

Investigation further revealed an agreement between the manufacturers of the Reliance handsets (Samsung and LG) and Reliance Infocomm regarding their exclusive models Samsung N191 and LG‑2030, which were to be used exclusively by Reliance India Mobile only. The remand report recorded that the TATA Indicom staff were "tampering with pre‑programmed CDM‑A digital handsets belonging to Reliance Infocomm and activating with their network with all dubious means which is an offence under Section 65, I.T. Act".

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether a telephone handset is a "computer" or a "computer system containing a computer programme" within the IT Act.
  2. Whether the manipulation of the 32‑bit Electronic Serial Number (ESN) programmed into the handset amounts to altering computer source code within s.65.
  3. Whether, in the absence of any law requiring the maintenance of "computer source code", the allegation that the petitioners concealed, destroyed or altered any computer source code is devoid of substance.
  4. Whether the same alteration amounts to hacking with a computer system (s.66, as it then stood).
  5. Whether, in the absence of any allegation that the second respondent had a copyright in the source code of the computer programme in the handsets, infringement of copyright arises (s.63, Copyright Act).
  6. Whether the FIR discloses the commission of cognizable offences under ss.409, 420 and 120B IPC at all.

4Examination of Reasoning — the arguments of both sides

Arguments of the petitioners (Sri C. Padmanabha Reddy, learned Senior Counsel)
  • It is always open to a subscriber to change service providers, and a subscriber who wants to change always takes his handset to BSNL or Reliance to get service connected and to give up the services of the previous provider.
  • The CDMA handsets brought by subscribers of other providers are capable of accommodating two separate lines and can be activated on principal assignment mobile (NAM 1 or NAM 2). The mere activation of NAM 1 or NAM 2 does not amount to any crime.
  • An offence under s.409 IPC is not made out: there is no entrustment of property to TTSL or its employees, and no dishonest appropriation or conversion to their own use.
  • An offence under s.420 IPC does not arise: there is no allegation that the petitioners deceived the second respondent fraudulently and dishonestly to deliver or retain property; the subscriber was never induced to deliver property to TTSL.
  • Since s.120B IPC is relatable only to ss.409 and 420 IPC, the charge under s.120B is misconceived.
  • As to s.65: a telephone handset is not a computer nor a computer system containing a computer programme; alternatively, in the absence of any law in force requiring the maintenance of "computer source code", the allegation that the petitioners concealed, destroyed or altered any computer source code is devoid of substance, and therefore the offence of hacking is absent.
  • As to s.63 of the Copyright Act: there is no allegation that the second respondent has a copyright in the source code of the computer programme in the handsets supplied by it, and therefore infringement does not arise.
  • TTSL has a subscriber base of one lakh customers in Andhra Pradesh and there was no necessity to woo Reliance's customers.
Arguments of the State and the second respondent
  • (Sri H. Prahlad Reddy, Additional Public Prosecutor; Sri D. Seshadri Naidu for the second respondent.)
  • When a cognizable offence under various provisions of different statutes is registered and investigation is pending, the Court cannot quash the FIR at the stage of investigation.
  • After appropriate preliminary investigation and examination of witnesses, the police have concluded that the petitioners have committed offences involving highly technical aspects, and unless and until proper evidence is let in before the criminal Court, on mere assertions of the accused a crime cannot be quashed.
  • The cell phone handsets with CDMA technology supplied by the second respondent are dedicated to Reliance Infocomm Ltd., and by interfering with the computer programme and converting the handsets to be responsive to the technology adopted by TTSL is itself an offence.

5Matrix of Holding

(a) ss.409, 420 and 120B IPC — quashed. "The submission of the learned Senior Counsel that even if the allegations in F.I.R., are taken to be true, an offence under Sections 409, 420 and 120B of IPC, is not made out has force." A subscriber was given a phone and connection with an understanding that the subscriber has exclusive right to use the phone; if the accused induced the subscriber to opt for TTSL's services, s.409 is not made out because entrustment is absent. Similarly, cheating under s.415 is not made out because the subscriber was never induced to deliver property to TTSL, and there was no dishonest or fraudulent inducement of the second respondent or its subscribers — "indeed the delivery of the property as such is not present in the case". As s.120B is relatable only to ss.409 and 420, the petitioners could not be prosecuted under it either. Citing State of West Bengal v. Swapan Kumar and State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal, 1992 Cri LJ 527 (SC), the Court held that where the FIR does not disclose the commission of a cognizable offence, the police have no power to investigate, and the Court would be justified in quashing.

(b) A cell phone is a computer. The Court read s.2(1)(i) ("computer"), s.2(1)(l) ("computer system") and s.2(1)(j) ("computer network") of the IT Act together, and held: "any electronic, magnetic or optical device used for storage of information received through satellite, microwave or other communication media and the devices which are programmable and capable of retrieving any information by manipulations of electronic, magnetic or optical impulses is a computer which can be used as computer system in a computer network." Then, decisively:

⚖️ The holding that decided the "cell phone is a computer" question — quote this

"Every cell phone service provider like Tata Indicom and Reliance India Mobile have special codes dedicated to them and these are intended to identify the phone, the phone's owner and the service provider. To understand how the cell phone works, we need to know certain terms in cell phone parlance. System Identification Code (SID) is a unique 5‑digit number that is assigned to each carrier by the licensor. Electronic Serial Number (ESN) is a unique 32‑bit number programmed into the phone when it is manufactured by the instrument manufacturer. Mobile Identification Number (MIN) is a 10‑digit number derived from cell phone number given to a subscriber. ... In the cell technology, a person using a phone in one cell of the division will be plugged to the central transmitter, which will receive the signals and then divert the signals to the other phone to which the same are intended. When the person moves from one cell to other cell in the same city, the system i.e., Mobile Telephone Switching Office (MTSO) automatically transfers signals from tower to tower when the telephone user moves from one division to another. ... The essential functions in the use of cell phone, which are performed by the MTSO, is the central antenna/central transmitter and other transmitters in other areas well coordinated with the cell phone functions in a fraction of a second. All this is made possible only by a computer, which simultaneously receives, analyses and distributes data by way of sending and receiving radio/electrical signals. ... So as to match with the system of the cell phone provider, every cell phone contains a circuit board, which is the brain of the phone. It is a combination of several computer chips programmed to convert analog to digital and digital to analog conversion and translation of the outgoing audio signals and incoming signals. This is a micro processor similar to the one generally used in the compact disk of a DeskTop computer. Without the circuit board, cell phone instrument cannot function. Therefore, it is not possible to accept the submission that a cell phone is not a computer. Even by the very definition of the computer and computer network as defined in IT Act, a cell phone is a computer which is programmed to do among others the function of receiving digital audio signals, convert it into analog audio signal and also send analog audio signals in a digital form externally by wireless technology."

(c) ESN and SID are computer source code — s.65 is attracted.

⚖️ The s.65 holding — the most quotable passage in the case

"By the very definition of 'computer source code,' a) list of programmes; b) computer commands; (c) design and layout and d) programme analysis of computer resource in any form, is a 'computer source code' for the purpose of Section 65 of I.‑T. Act. Going by the definition, ESN of Samsung N191 model cell phone handset or ESN of LG‑2030 model cell phone handset exclusively used by the second respondent as well as SID of second respondent come within the definition of computer source code. Every cell phone operator is required to obtain SID from the licensor i.e., Government of India. Further, ESN is a permanent part of the phone whereas MIN and SID are programmed into phone when one purchases a service plan and have the phone activity. When a customer of second respondent opts for its services, the MIN and SID are programmed into the handset. If some one manipulates and alters ESN ... Samsung/LG handsets which are exclusively used by them become usable by other service providers like TATA Indicom. Therefore, prima facie, when the ESN is altered, the offence under Section 65 of I.T. Act is attracted because every service provider like second respondent has to maintain its own SID code and also gives a customer specific number to each instrument used to avail the services provided. The submission that as there is no law which requires a computer source code to be maintained, an offence cannot be made out, is devoid of any merit. The disjunctive word 'or' is used by the Legislature between the phrases 'when the computer source code is required to be kept' and the other phrase 'maintained by law for the time being in force' and, therefore, both the situations are different. This Court, however, hastens to add that whether a cell phone operator is maintaining computer source code, is a matter of evidence. So far as this question is concerned, going by the allegations in the complaint, it becomes clear that the second respondent is in fact maintaining the computer source code."

The Court also set out the elements: "The offence of tampering with computer source documents under Section 65 of the IT Act is made out when a person, (i) intentionally conceals, destroys or alters a computer source code used for a computer, computer programme, computer system or computer network; (ii) intentionally or knowingly causes another to conceal, destroy or alter any computer source code ...; and (iii) (a) However, the offence is made out only when computer source code is required to be kept or (b) when computer source code is maintained by law for the time being in force."

(d) Hacking — s.66 left open. The Court expressly flagged the possibility without deciding it: "If the query answered in the affirmative, it is always open to the police to alter the F. I. R., or it is always open to the criminal Court to frame a charge specifically with regard to hacking with computer system, which is an offence under Section 66 of the IT Act."

(e) Copyright — s.63 survives. Reading ss.2(o), 2(ffc), 13 and 14(b) of the Copyright Act, 1957 together, "it becomes clear that a computer programme is by very definition original literary work and, therefore, the law protects such copyright. Under Section 63 of the Copyright Act, any infringement of the copyright in a computer programme/source code is punishable. Therefore, prima facie, if a person alters computer programme of another person or another computer company, the same would be infringement of the copyright." But: "the entire issue in this regard is subject to the evidence that may be led by the complainant at the time of trial. This Court, however, examined the submission of the learned senior counsel for the petitioners in the background of the provisions of the Copyright Act and observations made herein are not intended to decide the question one way or the other. The trial Court has to deal with these aspects."

(f) Result. "Crime No. 20 of 2003 insofar as it is under Sections 409, 420 and 120‑B of Indian Penal Code, 1860 is quashed and insofar as the crimes under Section 65 of the Information Technology Act, 2000 and Section 63 of the Copyright Act, 1957, the criminal petitions are dismissed. The C.I.D. Police ... is directed to complete investigation and file a final report before the Metropolitan Magistrate competent to take cognizance of the case within a period of three months."

Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. A cell phone is a "computer" within s.2(1)(i) of the IT Act, and part of a "computer system" and "computer network" — because it is a programmable electronic device that performs logical, arithmetic and memory functions by manipulating electronic, magnetic or optical impulses, and because its operation depends on a circuit board and microprocessor coordinated with a Mobile Telephone Switching Office.
  2. The Electronic Serial Number (ESN) and the System Identification Code (SID) are "computer source code" within the Explanation to s.65, because they fall within "the listing of programmes, computer commands, design and layout and programme analysis of computer resource in any form".
  3. The "required to be kept or maintained by law" condition is disjunctive. Either limb suffices, and the two situations "are different". A service provider that must obtain a SID from the licensor and give a customer‑specific number to each instrument is, on the allegations, maintaining computer source code. But whether it does so is a matter of evidence.
  4. Section 63 of the Copyright Act, 1957 applies to the alteration of another's computer programme, because a computer programme is an original literary work under s.2(o) protected by ss.13 and 14(b). Section 65 and s.63 are parallel and cumulative, not alternative.
  5. An FIR that discloses no cognizable offence under the IPC can be quashed at the investigation stage (Bhajan Lal), but a technical offence under a special statute cannot be quashed on the accused's bare assertions.
  6. The Court will not pre‑judge evidence at the s.482 stage. The observations on copyright were expressly "not intended to decide the question one way or the other".

6Application & Significance

Syed Asifuddin is the doctrinal source for "tampering with computer source code", which straddles s.65 (criminal) and s.43(i) (civil) — the same conduct can attract parallel liability, a key overlap point examiners test. It is also the case that established that the IT Act's enlarged definition of "computer" reaches devices nobody in 2000 would have called a computer, a point it shares with (and is distinguished from) Diebold Systems Pvt. Ltd. v. Commissioner, ILR 2005 KAR 2210, where the Karnataka High Court held that the IT Act's enlarged definition is not imported into a fiscal/taxation Entry, and an ATM is not a "computer" for sales‑tax classification. Note the distinction carefully: the IT Act's definitions govern the IT Act; they do not travel to other statutes.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Cite Syed Asifuddin for four propositions: (1) "A cell phone is a computer within s.2(1)(i) of the IT Act"; (2) "The ESN and SID of a mobile handset are computer source code within the Explanation to s.65"; (3) "The 'required to be kept or maintained by law' condition in s.65 is disjunctive — either limb suffices"; and (4) "Altering another's computer programme is also an infringement of copyright punishable under s.63 of the Copyright Act, 1957 — s.65 and s.63 run cumulatively."

10Case brief 2: Sanjay Kumar v. State of Haryana

DU CASE‑MAT 2 · ss.65, 66  The case that shows ss.65 and 66 applied concurrently to a single course of conduct, and that sharply distinguishes s.72 (disclosure) from ss.65/66 (source‑code and data integrity).

CASE‑MAT 2 · TIER 1 · ss.65, 66 READ WITH IPC FORGERY AND CHEATING

Sanjay Kumar v. State of Haryana

CRM No. 1353 of 2013 CRR No. 66 of 2013 (O&M) Punjab & Haryana High Court Decided 10 January 2013 Paramjeet Singh, J. Conviction under ss.420, 467, 468, 471 IPC and ss.65, 66 IT Act

1Citation & Bench

Sanjay Kumar v. State of Haryana, CRM No. 1353 of 2013; CRR No. 66 of 2013 (O&M), Punjab & Haryana High Court, decided 10 January 2013 by Paramjeet Singh, J. A criminal revision against the judgment dated 21.08.2012 of the learned Sessions Judge, Faridabad, dismissing the petitioner's appeal against the judgment of conviction dated 01.09.2011 and order of sentence dated 03.09.2011 of the Judicial Magistrate First Class, Faridabad. Printed in full in the DU case material at pages 31–35.

2Admission of Facts

The Senior Branch Manager of Vijay Bank, NIT, Faridabad moved a complaint dated 11.02.2003 before the police stating that the petitioner had been deputed by M/s Virmati Software and Telecommunication Ltd. to maintain the software system supplied by them to the bank, and was also looking after the software systems of certain other banks. In connection with rendering such services, he had access to the bank's accounting system, which was computerized, and was in a position to enter into ledgers and various other accounts.

While reconciling the accounts, certain discrepancies were pointed out by the bank's officials, and in that process it was revealed that the accused, who had SB Account No. 21499 in his own name in that bank, manipulated the entries by forging and fabricating certain entries from one account to another, from the computer system by handling the software, and withdrew the amounts from the bank on various dates by issuing cheques in his own favour, as well as from the cash counter and through transfer/clearing transactions.

As per the enquiry, the accused, by carrying out forgery, fabricating the entries in the computer system of the bank, illegally and wrongfully withdrew ₹17,67,409/‑ from the bank, thus causing wrongful gain to himself and wrongful loss to the bank. The bank came to know of the fraud on 07.02.2003. The accused was called to the bank and confronted with the details, but he gave evasive replies and only admitted having embezzled a sum of ₹17 lacs without giving further information or revealing the exact amount of fraud or the modus operandi, and assured to pay back the amount.

FIR No. 165 dated 11.02.2003 was registered under ss.406, 420, 467, 468, 469, 471 IPC and ss.65, 66 and 72 of the IT Act. After investigation, a challan was presented. The petitioner pleaded not guilty and claimed trial. The prosecution examined PW1 A. Siridhar, PW2 Girish Kumar Verma, PW3 Maheshwar Rath, PW4 Ramesh Kumar Malik and PW5 Dalip Singh, DSP. The accused's statement was recorded under s.313 Cr.P.C.; he denied the incriminating circumstances and pleaded innocence.

The trial court's detailed findings (reproduced in the High Court's judgment):

  • All prosecution witnesses supported the prosecution case. Ex.P36 corroborated that the accused was an employee of M/s Virmati Software deputed to the branch (Team No. 7, with his residence and pager numbers). Ex.P37 was the account‑opening form for Account No. 21499, with Ex.P38 the specimen signature card and Ex.P39 the cheque‑book issue register.
  • On 1.8.2001, ₹2,00,000 was deposited into the accused's account "by clearing" and ₹1 as interest. PW‑3 Maheshwar Rath found no supporting document or voucher for the clearing. Ex.PW1/D showed that even the ₹2,00,000 had been transferred from the interest account on 1.8.2001. Ex.P34 recorded that the accused had increased the interest portion in his own account through first‑time creation to the extent of ₹2,00,000, and then applied interest along with other SB accounts, and to mislead the branch employees, split the transaction into two parts — one shown as "by interest credit" of ₹1.00 and the other "by clearing" of ₹2,00,000.
  • Using the same modus operandi, the accused forged the interest entries on 1.8.2002 and 2.8.2000 and got ₹3,00,000 and ₹4,20,000 deposited in his account.
  • He then used the fixed deposit account of Sardar Jeet Singh, opened on 8.3.2002 with ₹1,05,00,000 deposited (Ex.P3). An interest of ₹8,46,489 was deposited on 29.4.2002 — an inflated interest calculated by forging entries, as if the account had been opened on a prior date. On 30.4.2002 the accused transferred ₹8,46,489 to the account of Anil Kumar Sharma, Account No. 22618 — an account already closed on 1.9.2001 — by forging entries to the effect that it was changed in the bank records from closed to open. On 8.5.2002, 13.5.2002 and 18.5.2002 he transferred ₹2,50,000, ₹2,50,000 and ₹3,47,409 respectively from Anil Kumar's account to his own. PW‑3 stated, and Ex.P34 recorded, that the transaction rooted through the accounts of Jeet Singh and Anil Kumar had been deleted by the accused using SRF files which were later recovered during audit.
  • Ex.P15 to Ex.P24 showed that the accused had withdrawn the amount through cheques.
  • The accused admitted his guilt vide letter Ex.P44, whose signature matched the specimen signatures on Ex.P38 and Ex.P37. Ex.P47 and Ex.P48 showed that he had tendered ₹3,50,000 to the bank in respect of the fraudulently withdrawn amount — further admission of guilt.
  • On the defence that no specific password was allotted to Sanjay, the trial court held: "However, no doubt password is given to an employee but it has surfaced in the testimonies of the prosecution witnesses that for the purpose of maintaining the software and in this manner had access to all those files to which only the employee of the bank could have. Moreover, as the amount was deposited in the account of accused and he has withdrawn it, there is no force in said argument."
  • And critically on s.72: "Furthermore, clearly the accused has tampered with the computer source document and he has also altered in the information which resided in the computer resource and by doing so he committed the offences under Sections 65 and 66 of the Information & Technology Act, 2000. At the same time, it is pertinent to mention that although accused was having secured assess to electrical record of the bank and he forged the entries and cheated to cause wrongful gain to himself but there is no such breach of confidentiality by disclosing the information to any other person and as such he is acquitted of offence under Section 72 of the Information & Technology Act, 2000."

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the petitioner's acts — forging electronic records, altering computer source documents, and unauthorised alteration of information residing in a computer resource — satisfy ss.65 and 66 of the IT Act read with the IPC forgery and cheating provisions.
  2. Whether the elements of ss.420, 467, 468 and 471 IPC were proved beyond reasonable doubt.
  3. Whether the offence under s.72 of the IT Act was made out.
  4. Whether the petitioner was falsely implicated and whether there was no direct evidence connecting him with the offence, so that no prima facie case was made out.

4Examination of Reasoning — the arguments of both sides

Arguments of the petitioner (in revision)
  • He was falsely implicated: the complainant and the eye witness were inimical to him, having had a dispute about 15/16 years prior to the occurrence.
  • There is no direct evidence to connect him with the alleged offence, and therefore no prima facie case was made out.
  • (Implicitly, and as recorded by the trial court:) no specific password was allotted to Sanjay, so unauthorised access could not be attributed to him.
Position of the prosecution / the Courts below
  • The documentary evidence — bank statements, the account‑opening form, the specimen signature card, the enquiry report Ex.P34, the cheque register, and the confessional letter Ex.P44 with matching handwriting — established the forgery and the withdrawals.
  • The same modus operandi — splitting a fraudulent interest credit into two entries to disguise it — was used on three separate dates, showing deliberation.
  • The accused had reopened a closed account by forging database entries and deleted the transaction trail using SRF files later recovered in audit — deliberate concealment.
  • The tender of ₹3,50,000 to the bank and the assurance to repay amounted to admission of guilt.
  • The accused had access to all those files to which only an employee of the bank could have had, by reason of his deputation to maintain the software.

5Matrix of Holding

The High Court dismissed the revision in limine:

⚖️ The operative holding

"From perusal of the judgments of both the Courts below, it transpires that the allegations against the petitioner are that the petitioner has manipulated the computerized Bank account i.e. the interest entries and thereby cheated the complainant bank by forging electronic record in order to cause wrongful loss to the bank and wrongful gain to himself to the tune of Rs.17,67,409/‑. It has come in the documentary evidence on record that the petitioner has forged the entries in the bank record and had thereby withdrawn a sum of Rs.17,67,409/‑. ... The learned Trial Court was wholly justified in convicting the accused‑petitioner and the learned Appellate court, as can be clearly seen, had not committed any error in upholding the conviction of the accused petitioner. Learned counsel for the petitioner failed to point out any misreading or non‑reading of any evidence and could not point out any infirmity in the judgments of the Courts below. The findings of guilt, reached against the accused‑petitioner does not, thus, suffer from any infirmity, legal or factual and does not therefore, warrant interference by this Court in exercise of this Court's revisional jurisdiction. In view of the above, there is no merit in the contentions raised by the learned counsel for the petitioner. Dismissed in limine."

Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. An insider's authorised access does not authorise the use made of it. A person deputed to maintain a bank's software has access to files, but that access does not extend to forging interest entries and transferring funds into his own account. Authorised access is not a defence to s.66 where the act done is one referred to in s.43 and is done dishonestly.
  2. Sections 65 and 66 apply concurrently to a single course of conduct. Tampering with the computer source document (s.65) and dishonestly altering information residing in a computer resource (s.66 via s.43(i)) are distinct offences arising from the same facts, and both may be charged.
  3. Section 72 requires unauthorised disclosure, not mere unauthorised access. Where the accused had secured access to the electronic record, forged entries and cheated, but did not disclose the information to any other person, he must be acquitted of s.72. This is the sharp distinction examiners test.
  4. The absence of an individually allotted password is not a defence where the accused, by reason of his deputation, had access to files that only an employee could access, and where the benefit of the fraud was deposited in and withdrawn from his own account.
  5. A confessional letter whose signature matches the specimen signature card, together with a partial tender of the defrauded amount, is powerful evidence of guilt — the Court relied on the manner in which the word "Sanjay" was written, compared with the accused's own writing in his s.313 Cr.P.C. statement.
  6. Revisional jurisdiction under s.401 Cr.P.C. is narrow. Where counsel cannot point to any misreading or non‑reading of evidence or any legal or factual infirmity, the revision will be dismissed in limine.

6Application & Significance

Sanjay Kumar is the authority for the concurrent application of ss.65 and 66 and for the s.65/s.66/s.72 triangle. It is also a valuable illustration of the evidentiary method in a computer‑related fraud prosecution: enquiry reports, account‑opening forms, specimen signature cards, audit‑recovered SRF files and a confessional letter, all read together. Use it whenever a problem question presents an insider — a bank employee, a software vendor, a payroll administrator — who abuses authorised access.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Cite Sanjay Kumar for three propositions: (1) "Sections 65 and 66 of the IT Act apply concurrently to a single course of conduct — tampering with the computer source document and dishonestly altering information residing in a computer resource"; (2) "An insider's authorised access to a system is no defence to s.66 where the act done is a s.43 act performed dishonestly"; and (3) "Section 72 requires unauthorised disclosure to another person; unauthorised access without disclosure does not attract s.72".

11Case brief 3: State of A.P. v. Prabhakar Sampath

DU CASE‑MAT 3 · s.66  The syllabus's "Hacking (s.43(i) read with s.66)" case. Its value is the investigative chain it documents — the six‑step method by which a hacking prosecution is actually proved.

CASE‑MAT 3 · TIER 2 · THE HACKING INVESTIGATIVE CHAIN

State of A.P. through Inspector of Police, Cyber Crimes P.S., C.I.D., Hyderabad v. Prabhakar Sampath

C.C. No. 489 of 2010 VI Addl. Chief Metropolitan Magistrate, Hyderabad Decided 31 March 2015 Sri P. Bhaskara Rao, Addl. CMM s.66 IT Act

1Citation & Bench

State of A.P. through Inspector of Police, Cyber Crimes P.S., C.I.D., Hyderabad v. Prabhakar Sampath, C.C. No. 489 of 2010, decided by Sri P. Bhaskara Rao, VI Addl. Chief Metropolitan Magistrate, Hyderabad on 31 March 2015. Charge sheet in Cr. No. 18/2008. Available at www.prashantmali.com/cyber‑law‑cases. Printed in the DU case material at pages 36–38.

2Admission of Facts

PW.1, Arcot K. Balraj, Chief Manager (Administrative Affairs) of M/s. SIS Infotech Private Limited, situated in Lakshmi Cyber Centre, Road No. 12, Banjara Hills, Hyderabad, lodged a complaint (Ex.P1) on 23‑12‑2008 in the Cyber Crimes P.S., C.I.D., Hyderabad.

The complainant company was one of the largest market research firms headquartered in the USA with substantial operations at Hyderabad, having 300 employees, and carried on the business of research, station and support to its parent company in the USA, M/s. Global Industry Analysts Inc. (GIA). In the course of its day‑to‑day operations the company created substantial data and content, hosted on the Adobe Content Server of GIA, accessible only by registered users with permissions. The data was in the form of analysed documents called "Research Reports" carrying high pecuniary value, of which the complainant company held proprietary copyright.

It was alleged that the content — the Research Reports — was hacked by somebody by hacking their server www.strategyR.com and downloaded several e‑Reports through some free public sites. PW.1 furnished the information that the hacker had worked out from their server as [email protected] and that the IP address was 61.95.152.145, and requested police action.

The investigation, step by step:

  1. LW.11, M. Ganapathi Rao, Dy. Superintendent of Police, Cyber Crimes P.S. Hyderabad registered the complaint as a case in Cr. 18/2008 under s.66 of the IT Act and took up investigation. He secured PW.1 and PW.2, examined them and recorded their detailed statements, and came to know that the accused had hacked the content web server through IP address 61.95.152.145 provided by Bharti Airtel Ltd.
  2. He addressed a letter to Airtel's Manager to furnish the end‑user details of that IP address. On verification, Airtel gave the information that the IP address was allotted to M/s. Frost & Sullivan Private Limited, 101, Santhome High Road, Foreshore Estate, Chennai, Tamil Nadu, with the contact details of Mr. M. Kirbakaram, email [email protected], mobile 978993031.
  3. On that information, the DSP obtained a search warrant from the court and conducted a search at the residence of the accused at House No. 35A, Magazine Road, St. Thomas Mount, Chennai, in the presence of mediators PW.7 and LW.9 Jakhir Hussain, and seized one pen drive.
  4. The pen drive contained six files in .pdf format: Anticoagulatns.pdf, Bulk_Paclitaxel.pdf, CNS_Therapeutics.pdf, Human_Vaccines.pdf, Microplate_instrumentation_Supplies.pdf and Therapeutic_Aphersis.pdf — all downloaded by the accused from www.StrategyR.com of the complainant company using the computer system installed at his office, M/s. Frost & Sullivan Pvt. Ltd., Chennai.
  5. The DSP arrested the accused on 29‑12‑2008 at 2.00 PM, recorded his confessional statement, and on his information seized one computer system S605 allotted to the accused in his company, which contained the same six downloaded documents.
  6. He examined PW.3 to PW.5 and LW.6 Kirthi Thimmanagoudar, working at Frost & Sullivan, and forwarded the material objects — the pen drive and the CPU — to APFSL, Hyderabad for analysis and report. PW.9, Inspector of Police, Cyber Crimes P.S. C.I.D. Hyderabad collected the FSL report from PW.8, Computer Forensic Expert, APFSL, Hyderabad, who analysed the material objects and opined that the pen drive contained the six files, and that the hard disk of the CPU contained the six files and data pertaining to the IP address.

After completion of the investigation, PW.9 filed the charge sheet under s.66 of the IT Act for hacking the content server of the complainant's company.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the accused hacked the content web server of the complainant's company and downloaded proprietary Research Reports without permission.
  2. Whether such conduct amounts to an act referred to in s.43 — specifically s.43(b) (downloading, copying or extracting data or information) — done dishonestly or fraudulently, so as to attract s.66.
  3. Whether the chain of electronic evidence — IP trace, ISP subscriber records, seizure, and forensic analysis matching the downloaded files to the seized media — establishes the offence beyond reasonable doubt.

4Examination of Reasoning

The judgment records the procedural sequence in the orthodox form: copies of all documents on which the prosecution relied were furnished to the accused under s.207 Cr.P.C.; the accused was examined under s.239 Cr.P.C. and denied the accusations; charges under s.66 were framed, read over and explained; the accused pleaded not guilty and claimed to be tried. The prosecution then proved, by the chain set out above, that the accused accessed the complainant's content server without authorisation and downloaded six proprietary research reports onto a pen drive and onto his office computer. The DU case material reproduces the judgment up to the framing of the charge; the conviction followed on the charge sheet filed under s.66.

5Matrix of Holding

  • The accused was charged under s.66 of the IT Act, 2000 for hacking the content server of the complainant company.
  • The prosecution established the unauthorised downloading of proprietary data — an act referred to in s.43(b) — done dishonestly (wrongful gain to the accused, wrongful loss to the complainant), thereby attracting s.66.
  • The case is the standard illustration of the investigative methodology for hacking prosecutions: IP address → ISP subscriber records → search warrant → seizure of devices → forensic (APFSL) analysis matching downloaded files to the seized media.
Principle evolved
  1. A hacking prosecution under s.66 is proved by a chain, not by direct evidence of the keystrokes. The IP address, the ISP's subscriber records, the seizure under a warrant, and the forensic match between the downloaded files and the seized media together establish the offence.
  2. The ISP's subscriber records are the pivot of attribution. It was the Airtel letter identifying the IP as allotted to M/s. Frost & Sullivan that made the search warrant possible.
  3. Forensic matching of files to seized media is the link between the accused and the act — here, the same six .pdf files on both the pen drive and the hard disk, plus IP‑address data on the hard disk.
  4. An employee's misuse of his employer's computer system is not excused by his possession of the device — the system S605 was allotted to the accused at his office, yet the offence was made out.

6Application & Significance

Prabhakar Sampath is the case to cite for the evidentiary and investigative chain in a hacking prosecution. It also demonstrates the IPR dimension of the syllabus: the downloaded Research Reports were "of high pecuniary value, of which complainant company holds proprietary Copy Right", so the same facts would support civil claims and, potentially, Copyright Act remedies.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Use Prabhakar Sampath whenever a question asks how hacking is proved or asks you to describe the investigative process. The correct formulation: "State of A.P. v. Prabhakar Sampath (Addl. CMM Hyderabad, 31.03.2015) illustrates the six‑step investigative chain in a s.66 hacking prosecution: identification of the IP address from the server logs; obtaining ISP subscriber records; obtaining a search warrant; seizure of the pen drive and CPU; arrest and disclosure statement; and forensic analysis by APFSL matching the six downloaded research reports to the seized media."

🎯 The self‑learning exercise printed with the case — model answer

"Does 'Hacking' continue to be a criminal offence under IT Act 2000. Is it possible to claim damages for hacking."

Model answer: Yes, hacking continues to be a criminal offence. Although the 2008 Amendment deleted the pre‑2008 definition of "hacking" in s.66(1), the offence survives in a recast form: s.66 now provides that "if any person, dishonestly or fraudulently, does any act referred to in section 43, he shall be punishable with imprisonment ... up to three years or with fine which may extend to five lakh rupees or with both", importing the IPC definitions of "dishonestly" (s.24) and "fraudulently" (s.25). The ten acts of s.43 — including unauthorised access, downloading and extracting data, introducing a virus, damaging, disrupting and denying access — therefore remain criminal when done dishonestly or fraudulently. Separately, s.65 punishes tampering with computer source code, s.66C punishes identity theft, and s.70 punishes unauthorised access to a protected system. As to damages: yes. The same conduct is civilly actionable under s.43, which provides that a person who does any of the enumerated acts without permission "shall be liable to pay damages by way of compensation to the person so affected". The claim lies before the Adjudicating Officer under s.46, subject to the ₹5 crore ceiling, with appeal to TDSAT under s.57 and then to the High Court under s.62. However, per Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi, the claimant must independently plead and prove the damage suffered — a breach of s.43 does not automatically yield compensation.

12Case brief 4: NAASCOM v. Ajay Sood & Ors.

DU CASE‑MAT 4 · phishing  The doctrinal ancestor of ss.66C and 66D — and the case that defined "phishing" in Indian law at a time when no statute did.

CASE‑MAT 4 · TIER 1 · THE DOCTRINAL ANCESTOR OF ss.66C AND 66D

National Association of Software and Service Companies v. Ajay Sood and Others

119 (2005) DLT 596 2005 (30) PTC 437 Del Delhi High Court Pradeep Nandrajog, J. C.S. (OS) No. 285/2005 Order dated 2 March 2005

1Citation & Bench

National Association of Software and Service Companies v. Ajay Sood and others, 119 (2005) DLT 596; 2005 (30) PTC 437 Del, Delhi High Court, Pradeep Nandrajog, J. A suit (C.S. (OS) No. 285/2005) for a permanent injunction, rendition of accounts and damages, decided in the context of an application (I.A. 2351/2005) under Order 23 Rule 3 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 recording a compromise. The order granting the ex parte ad interim injunction is dated 2 March 2005; the reasoned order decreeing the suit in terms of the compromise follows. Printed in the DU case material at pages 39–41.

2Admission of Facts

NASSCOM is India's premiere software association, representing 850 members of which nearly 150 are global companies, with a wide range of activities. The plaintiff filed the suit inter alia praying for a decree of permanent injunction restraining the defendants or any person acting under their authority from circulating fraudulent e‑mails purportedly originating from the plaintiff or using the trade mark "NASSCOM" or any other mark confusingly similar in relation to goods or services, together with a prayer for rendition of accounts and damages.

As per the plaint, the defendants, masquerading as NASSCOM, in order to obtain personal data from various addresses which they could then use for head‑hunting, went on the website as if they were a premiere selection and recruitment firm.

The procedural sequence:

  • On 2 March 2005, the Court granted an ex parte ad interim injunction restraining the defendants from using the trade name NASSCOM or any name deceptively similar thereto, and from holding themselves out as associates or part of NASSCOM. The Court also directed the execution of a commission to visit the premises of the defendants and take into custody the hard disc, "as it was reasonably to be expected that the fraudulent e‑mails sent by the defendants to various parties would be located on the hard‑disc".
  • The commission was executed on 2.3.2005 itself. Two hard discs were recovered, on which the offending e‑mails were found. One e‑mail dated 10.1.2003 written by defendant No. 3 and another e‑mail dated 11.1.2005 written by defendant No. 2 (both fictitious persons) were downloaded from the hard disc.
  • There were four defendants to the suit. Defendants 2 and 3 — Ms. Shweta Ganguli and Mr. Preeti Malotra — were, per the averments in the plaint, the authors of the offending e‑mails.
  • As per the compromise application, it transpired that a lady, Tithypoorna Ganguli, an employee of defendant No. 4, created fictitious e‑mail IDs in the names of defendants No. 2 and 3 and sent the e‑mails in the name of NASSCOM to third parties with a view to extract personal data. "In other words, head hunting was on. May be, head hunting was on behalf of defendant No. 4 but the truth would never surface in the present case for the reason parties have entered into a compromise."
  • Mr. Ajay Sood and Mr. Shiv Agrawal (a Director of defendant No. 4) were present in court and affirmed the settlement; their statements were recorded. The application was signed on behalf of defendant No. 1 in person and, on behalf of the plaintiff, by Mr. Mohan Khanna, whose affidavit was enclosed.
  • Under the compromise, defendants 1 and 4 agreed to suffer a decree in terms of paras 35(a), (b) and (g) of the plaint, agreed that the hard disc seized from their office by the Local Commissioner could be delivered up to the plaintiff, and agreed that defendants 2 and 3 — fictitious identities created by Tithypoorna Ganguli — be deleted from the array of parties.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the conduct of the defendants — masquerading as NASSCOM and sending fraudulent recruitment e‑mails to extract personal data — is actionable at all, at a time (2005) when India had no specific legislation on phishing.
  2. Whether it amounts to a misrepresentation in the course of trade leading to confusion as to the source and origin of the e‑mail.
  3. Whether it amounts to passing off — affecting or tarnishing the image of the plaintiff.
  4. Whether it amounts to cheating or criminal breach of trust.
  5. What relief is appropriate — injunction, rendition of accounts, damages, and delivery up of the hard discs.

4Examination of Reasoning — the arguments of both sides

Arguments of the plaintiff (NASSCOM)
  • The defendants masqueraded as NASSCOM and sent fraudulent e‑mails purportedly originating from the plaintiff, using the trade mark NASSCOM without authorisation.
  • Their object was to obtain personal data from various addresses which they could then use for head‑hunting — a commercial advantage obtained by deception.
  • The conduct caused immense harm not only to the consumer but even to the person whose name, identity or password was misused.
  • It constituted passing off — affecting and tarnishing the image of the plaintiff.
  • Prayer: permanent injunction, rendition of accounts, and damages.
Position of the defendants
  • The offending e‑mails were sent by defendants 2 and 3, who were fictitious identities created by an employee of defendant No. 4 — not by the corporate defendants themselves.
  • The corporate defendants learnt of the offending acts through the medium of the present suit and identified Ms. Tithypoorna Ganguli as the person responsible.
  • They acknowledged their employee's illegal action as violative of the plaintiff's right and recognised the plaintiff in a sum of ₹16,00,000.
  • They consented to suffer a decree as recorded in the application under Order 23 Rule 3 CPC, and to deliver up the hard discs.

5Matrix of Holding

(a) The compromise was recorded and accepted. "I.A. 2351/2005 brings on record a settlement which in the opinion of the court is a bona‑fide settlement and does not suffer from any illegality. Settlement is taken on record and is accepted."

(b) The definition of phishing — the enduring contribution of the case.

⚖️ Nandrajog J.'s definition — quote this in full

"Internet has spawned novel and interesting methods to defraud individuals and companies, 'Phishing' is a form of internet fraud. In a case of 'Phishing', a person pretending to be a legitimate association such as a bank or an insurance company in order to extract personal data from a user such as access codes, passwords etc. which are then used to his own advantage, misrepresents on the identity of the legitimate party. Typically 'Phishing' scams involve persons who pretend to represent online banks and siphon cash from e‑banking accounts after conning consumers into handing over confidential banking details."

"The internet these days is full of scams. E‑mail that form the basis of phishing attacks and pose as a security check. These messages trick users into handing over their account details and passwords. The quoted details are subsequently used for fraudulent transfers. It was only towards the end of 2003 that phishing e‑mails were spotted. Unfortunately, these are becoming increasingly sophisticated. It appears that the expression 'phishing' comes from the word fishing whereby a bait is set in the hope that someone will bite."

(c) No Indian legislation on phishing — but the conduct was actionable.

⚖️ The holding that makes the case a landmark

"I find no legislation in India on 'phishing'. An act which amounts to phishing, under the Indian law would be a mis‑representation made in the course of trade leading to confusion as to the source and origin of the e‑mail causing immense harm not only to the consumer but even the person whose name, identity or password is misused. It would also be an act of passing off as is affecting or tarnishing the image of the plaintiff, if an action is brought by the aggrieved party."

"Whether law should develop on the lines suggested by Robert Louis B Stevenson in his article noted above is left by this Court for future development in an appropriate case." (The article — "Plugging the Phishing Hole: Legislation v. Technology", dated 17 March 2005 — described the US Anti‑Phishing Act, which would criminalise sending a phishing e‑mail and creating a phishing website regardless of whether any recipient suffered actual damages, and which appropriated $10,000,000 to the Attorney General for prosecuting spyware and phishing.)

(d) The decree. "Suit would stand decreed in terms of the compromise effected between the parties and as contained in IA No. 2351/2005. Said application shall form part of the decree to be drawn." And: "Hard‑discs seized for the defendant's premises by the Local Commissioner on 2.3.2005 are hereby ordered to be turned over to the plaintiff who would be the owner of the hard‑discs. Defendants 1 and 4, their servants and agents would be injuncted from circulating fraudulent e‑mails purportedly originating from the plaintiff or using the trade name NASSCOM or any other name/mark and address of the plaintiff amounting to passing off and tarnishment." No costs.

Principle evolved
  1. "Phishing" is defined in Indian law as a form of internet fraud in which a person, pretending to be a legitimate association such as a bank or an insurance company, extracts personal data such as access codes and passwords from a user, which are then used to his own advantage, by misrepresenting the identity of the legitimate party.
  2. Phishing is actionable at common law even without a dedicated statute. It is a misrepresentation in the course of trade leading to confusion as to the source and origin of the e‑mail, and it is an act of passing off affecting or tarnishing the image of the party whose identity is misused.
  3. Delivery up of the instrumentality is an available remedy. The hard discs on which the offending e‑mails were found were ordered to be turned over to the plaintiff — an early recognition that the physical medium of electronic wrongdoing can be forfeited to the victim.
  4. An employer is answerable for its employee's phishing. The corporate defendants acknowledged the illegal action of an employee of defendant No. 4, recognised the plaintiff in ₹16,00,000 and consented to a decree — illustrating vicarious liability for cyber wrongdoing done in the course of employment.
  5. Fictitious online identities do not defeat the action. Where the real author is identified (here, Ms. Tithypoorna Ganguli, who created fictitious e‑mail IDs in the names of defendants 2 and 3), the fictitious parties can be deleted and the real actors proceeded against.

6Application & Significance

NAASCOM is the foundational case on phishing and the doctrinal ancestor of ss.66C and 66D, both inserted by the 2008 Amendment — three years after this judgment. It is a strong "historical development" opener for any question on identity theft: "When Nandrajog J. decided NAASCOM in 2005, he found 'no legislation in India on phishing' and had to construct the wrong out of misrepresentation in the course of trade and passing off. The 2008 Amendment then inserted s.66C (identity theft) and s.66D (cheating by personation by using computer resource), which now supply the statutory home for precisely this conduct." Note that the case was decided before s.66C and s.66D existed — so do not describe it as a prosecution under those sections.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Cite NAASCOM for three propositions: (1) the definition of phishing (a person pretending to be a legitimate association in order to extract personal data such as access codes and passwords, by misrepresenting the identity of the legitimate party); (2) "At the time of the decision, India had no legislation on phishing; the wrong was constructed from misrepresentation in the course of trade and passing off"; and (3) "The case is the doctrinal ancestor of ss.66C and 66D, inserted by the 2008 Amendment — the statutory analogue of what was previously only a common‑law wrong."

13Case brief 5: Aveek Sarkar v. State of West Bengal

DU CASE‑MAT 5 · obscenity test  The controlling authority on obscenity in India, equally applicable to IPC s.292 and to ss.67, 67A and 67B of the IT Act. Printed in full in the DU case material at pages 42–50.

CASE‑MAT 5 · TIER 1 · THE CONTROLLING OBSCENITY TEST

Aveek Sarkar v. State of West Bengal

Criminal Appeal No. 902 of 2004 Supreme Court of India Decided 3 February 2014 K.S. Radhakrishnan, J. (2014) 4 SCC 257

1Citation & Bench

Aveek Sarkar v. State of West Bengal, Criminal Appeal No. 902 of 2004, Supreme Court of India, decided 3 February 2014 by K.S. Radhakrishnan, J. Reported at (2014) 4 SCC 257. An appeal against the judgment of the Calcutta High Court declining to quash proceedings under s.482 Cr.P.C. in Case No. C.796 of 1993 (corresponding to T.R. No. 35 of 1994) pending before the Judicial Magistrate's Court, Alipore. Printed in the DU case material at pages 42–50.

2Admission of Facts

A German magazine, "STERN", having worldwide circulation, published an article with a picture of Boris Becker, a world‑renowned tennis player, posing nude with his dark‑skinned fiancée Barbara Feltus, a film actress, which was photographed by none other than her father. The article stated that, in an interview, both Boris Becker and Barbara Feltus spoke freely about their engagement, their lives and future plans, and the message they wanted to convey to the people at large, for posing to such a photograph. The article pictured Boris Becker as a strident protester of the pernicious practice of "Apartheid", and stated that the purpose of the photograph was also to signify that love champions over hatred.

"Sports World", a widely circulated magazine published in India, reproduced the article and the photograph as a cover story in its Issue 15 dated 05.05.1993, with the caption "Posing nude dropping out of tournaments, battling Racism in Germany. Boris Becker explains his recent approach to life" — "Boris Becker Unmasked". Anandabazar Patrika, a newspaper having wide circulation in Kolkata, also published the photograph and the article on the second page on 06.05.1993.

A lawyer practising at Alipore Judge's Court, Kolkata, claiming to be a regular reader of both publications, filed a complaint under s.292 IPC against the appellants — the Editor and the Publisher and Printer of the newspaper, and the Editor of Sports World, the former Captain of the Indian Cricket Team, late Mansoor Ali Khan of Pataudi. The complaint stated that, as an experienced advocate and an elderly person, he could vouchsafe that the nude photograph would corrupt young minds, both children and youth of this country, and is against the cultural and moral values of our society, and that unless such photographs were censured and banned and the accused punished, the dignity and honour of our womanhood would be in jeopardy. He deposed that the photograph was published "in an inter‑twined manner wherein Boris Becker placed the hand upon the breast of Miss Barbara", was "absolutely obscene and lascivious in nature", and had "a very bad impact" on the minds of persons of different age groups.

The complainant also urged that the accused be prosecuted under s.4 of the Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986, since the photograph prima facie gave "a sexual titillation and its impact moral degradation" and would encourage people to commit sexual offences. The accused filed an application on 5.3.1993 for dropping the proceedings, submitting that there was no illegality in reproducing the news item and photograph from "STERN", that the magazine was never banned for entry into India and was never considered obscene, and that s.79 IPC (nothing is an offence done by a person justified by law, or who by reason of a mistake of fact and not of law in good faith believes himself justified by law) applied.

The learned Magistrate, on 10.5.1993, held that a prima facie case was made out under s.292 IPC and issued summons. He later held: "until evidence comes in it will not be proper to give any opinion as to the responsibility of the accused persons. But I feel it pertinent to mention that though the Section 292 does not define word 'obscene', but my [minds] of precedents have clustered round on this point and being satisfied with the materials on record, pernicious effect of picture in depraving and debauching the mind of the persons into whose hands it may come and also for other sufficient reasons to proceed further this Court was pleased to issue process against the accused persons under Section 292 I.P.C. At present having regard to the facts of the case, I find the matter merits interference by not dropping the proceedings as prayed for. It is too early to say that the accused persons are entitled to get benefit of Section 79 I.P.C." The accused were then put to face trial for the offence punishable under s.292 IPC alternatively under s.4 of the Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986. The High Court declined to quash the proceedings.

3Statutory Issues

  1. What is the correct legal test for "obscenity" under s.292 IPC — the Victorian Hicklin test (isolated passage / most susceptible reader) or the contemporary community standards test?
  2. Whether the photograph, taken as a whole and in the context of the article, is obscene — i.e. whether it is lascivious, appeals to the prurient interest, or tends to deprave and corrupt persons likely to read, see or hear it.
  3. Whether nudity per se is obscene.
  4. Whether the context and message of the publication are relevant to the determination.
  5. Whether the Magistrate and the High Court properly exercised their jurisdiction in issuing process and declining to quash.

4Examination of Reasoning — the arguments of both sides

Arguments of the appellants (Shri Pradeep Ghosh, learned senior counsel)
  • The publication and photograph, taken as a whole and in the background of the facts and circumstances, cannot be said to be per se "obscene" within the meaning of s.292(1) IPC so as to remand the appellants for trial.
  • Obscenity has to be judged in the context of contemporary social mores, current socio‑moral attitude of the community and the prevalent norms of acceptability/susceptibility of the community in relation to matters in issue. Reliance was placed on the Constitution Bench in Ranjit D. Udeshi v. State of Maharashtra, AIR 1965 SC 881 and on Chandrakant Kalyandas Kakodar v. State of Maharashtra, 1969 (2) SCC 687.
  • The Magistrate and the High Court completely overlooked the context in which the photograph was published and the message it gave to the public at large — a protest against apartheid in Germany.
  • The photograph is in no way vulgar or lascivious; it is neither suggestive nor provocative and would have no effect on the minds of the youth or the public in general.
  • The Courts below did not properly appreciate the scope of s.79 IPC: the appellants were justified in law in publishing the photograph and article borrowed from a German magazine which was never banned in India and never found obscene by the State authorities; no FIR was ever lodged; and a private complaint of such a nature should not have been entertained without appreciating the facts and the law.
  • The High Court ought to have exercised jurisdiction under s.482 Cr.P.C.
Arguments of the respondents (Shri Mohit Paul, learned counsel)
  • The Courts below were justified in holding that it would not be proper to give an opinion as to the culpability of the accused at this stage, and that the same has to be judged from the point of view of an average person by applying contemporary community standards.
  • The photograph was obscene and lascivious, would corrupt young minds, and was against the cultural and moral values of society; the dignity and honour of womanhood were in jeopardy.
  • The photograph prima facie gave sexual titillation and its impact was moral degradation, engaging s.4 of the Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986 as well.

5Matrix of Holding

(a) The comparative survey. The Court traced the evolution of obscenity law across jurisdictions:

  • England — Regina v. Hicklin (1868): "The test of obscenity is whether the tendency of the matter charged as obscenity is to deprave and corrupt those whose minds are open to such immoral influences and into whose hands a publication of this sort may fall." This came to be known as the Hicklin test. Lord Campbell enacted the first legislative measure against obscene books etc. in 1857; his successor in the office of Chief Justice interpreted his statute in Hicklin.
  • Canada — Brodie v. The Queen (1962) SCR 681: the majority held that D.H. Lawrence's novel "Lady Chatterley's Lover" was not obscene within the meaning of the Canadian Criminal Code.
  • Canada — Regina v. Butler (1992) 1 SCR 452: the dominant test is the "community standards of tolerance test". The Court held that explicit sex that is not violent and neither degrading nor dehumanizing is generally tolerated in Canadian society and will not qualify as the undue exploitation of sex unless it employs children in its production. For material to qualify as "obscene", the exploitation of sex must not only be a dominant characteristic, but such exploitation must be "undue".
  • Canada — Towne Cinema Theatres Ltd. v. The Queen (1985) 1 SCR 494: the Court applied the community standard test and not the Hicklin test.

(b) The Hicklin test is rejected; the community standards test is adopted.

⚖️ The ratio — the single most important passage in the case

"We are also of the view that Hicklin test is not the correct test to be applied to determine "what is obscenity". Section 292 of the Indian Penal Code, of course, uses the expression 'lascivious and prurient interests' or its effect. Later, it has also been indicated in the said Section of the applicability of the effect and the necessity of taking the items as a whole and on that foundation where such items would tend to deprave and corrupt persons who are likely, having regard to all the relevant circumstances, to read, see or hear the matter contained or embodied in it. We have, therefore, to apply the "community standard test" rather than "Hicklin test" to determine what is "obscenity"."

"A bare reading of Sub‑section (1) of Section 292, makes clear that a picture or article shall be deemed to be obscene (i) if it is lascivious; (ii) it appeals to the prurient interest, and (iii) it tends to deprave and corrupt persons who are likely to read, see or hear the matter, alleged to be obscene. Once the matter is found to be obscene, the question may arise as to whether the impugned matter falls within any of the exceptions contained in Section. A picture of a nude/semi‑nude woman, as such, cannot per se be called obscene unless it has the tendency to arouse feeling or revealing an overt sexual desire. The picture should be suggestive of deprave mind and designed to excite sexual passion in persons who are likely to see it, which will depend on the particular posture and the background in which the nude/semi‑nude woman is depicted. Only those sex‑related materials which have a tendency of "exciting lustful thoughts" can be held to be obscene, but the obscenity has to be judged from the point of view of an average person, by applying contemporary community standards."

(c) Message and context. "We have to examine the question of obscenity in the context in which the photograph appears and the message it wants to convey." The Court relied on Bobby Art International & Ors. v. Om Pal Singh Hoon, (1996) 4 SCC 1 (the Bandit Queen case), where the Court had held that the so‑called objectionable scenes must be considered in the context of the message the film sought to transmit: "The object of doing so was not to titillate the cinemagoer's lust but to arouse in him sympathy for the victim and disgust for the perpetrators. The revulsion that the Tribunal referred to was not at Phoolan Devi's nudity but at the sadism and heartlessness of those who had stripped her naked to rob her of every shred of dignity. Nakedness does not always arouse the baser instinct. ... We do not censor to protect the pervert or to assuage the susceptibilities of the over‑sensitive." The Court also relied on Ajay Goswami v. Union of India, (2007) 1 SCC 143, where it was held that "the commitment to freedom of expression demands that it cannot be suppressed, unless the situations created by it allowing the freedom are pressing and the community interest is endangered".

(d) Application to the facts. "Applying the community tolerance test, we are not prepared to say such a photograph is suggestive of deprave minds and designed to excite sexual passion in persons who are likely to look at them and see them, which would depend upon the particular posture and background in which the woman is depicted or shown. Breast of Barbara Fultus has been fully covered with the arm of Boris Becker, a photograph, of course, semi‑nude, but taken by none other than the father of Barbara. Further, the photograph, in our view, has no tendency to deprave or corrupt the minds of people in whose hands the magazine Sports World or Anandabazar Patrika would fall." And: "We should, therefore, appreciate the photograph and the article in the light of the message it wants to convey, that is to eradicate the evil of racism and apartheid in the society and to promote love and marriage between white skinned man and a black skinned woman. When viewed in that angle, we are not prepared to say that the picture or the article which was reproduced by Sports World and the Anandabazar Patrika be said to be objectionable so as to initiate proceedings under Section 292 IPC or under Section 4 of the Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986." Boris Becker's own words were quoted: "the nude photos were supposed to shock, no doubt about it....... What I am saying with these photos is that an inter‑racial relationship is okay."

(e) The result, with a rebuke to the lower courts. "We have found that no offence has been committed under Section 292 IPC and then the question whether it falls in the first part of Section 79 IPC has become academic. We are sorry to note that the learned Magistrate, without proper application of mind or appreciation of background in which the photograph has been shown, proposed to initiate prosecution proceedings against the Appellants. Learned Magistrate should have exercised his wisdom on the basis of judicial precedents in the event of which he would not have ordered the Appellants to face the trial. The High Court, in our view, should have exercised powers under Section 482 Cr.P.C. to secure the ends of justice. We are, therefore, inclined to allow this appeal and set aside the criminal proceedings initiated against the Appellants. The Appeal is allowed as above."

Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. The Hicklin test is no longer the correct test for obscenity in India. The Victorian standard — isolated passages judged by their tendency to deprave and corrupt those whose minds are open to immoral influences and into whose hands the publication may fall — has been replaced by the contemporary community standards test.
  2. Obscenity has three cumulative indicia under s.292(1): the material must be (i) lascivious, or (ii) appeal to the prurient interest, or (iii) tend to deprave and corrupt persons likely to read, see or hear it. Once found obscene, the exceptions in s.292 may be considered.
  3. Nudity per se is not obscenity. A picture of a nude or semi‑nude person cannot be called obscene unless it has a tendency to arouse feeling or reveal an overt sexual desire, is suggestive of a depraved mind and designed to excite sexual passion, judged by the particular posture and the background in which the person is depicted.
  4. Only material with a tendency of "exciting lustful thoughts" is obscene, and it must be judged from the point of view of an average person applying contemporary community standards — not the most susceptible reader.
  5. Context and message are decisive. The photograph must be examined in the context in which it appears and the message it wants to convey; a photograph published to protest apartheid and to promote inter‑racial love is not obscene merely because it depicts a nude body.
  6. The court does not censor to protect the pervert or to assuage the susceptibilities of the over‑sensitive (Bobby Art International).
  7. The test is transposable to the IT Act. Section 67 uses materially identical language — "lascivious", "prurient interest", "deprave and corrupt" — so the Aveek Sarkar test governs ss.67, 67A and 67B as well.

6Application & Significance

Aveek Sarkar is the controlling test for obscenity in India today. It is directly applicable to IPC s.292, to ss.67/67A/67B of the IT Act, and to the Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986. It is the answer to every problem question presenting online sexual content, and it is the doctrinal foundation of the companion case M.F. Husain v. Raj Kumar Pandey (§14 below), which applies the same approach to a painting auctioned online. In a comparative paragraph, note that India's community‑standards test draws on Roth v. United States, 354 U.S. 476 (1957) (average person, contemporary community standards, dominant theme) and on Regina v. Butler's "undue exploitation of sex", and may be contrasted with the more elaborate three‑prong Miller v. California, 413 U.S. 15 (1973) test (prurient interest + patently offensive + lacks serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value).

🎓 Exam takeaway — the four‑point formula

Always state: (1) the Hicklin test is abandoned; (2) the community standards test is adopted; (3) the work must be judged as a whole, in context, and by the message it conveys; and (4) the standard is that of an average, reasonable person, not the most susceptible reader. Add: (5) nudity per se is not obscenity.

🎯 The two self‑learning exercises printed with the case — model answers

Exercise 1: "Obscenity involves within its ambit many other social connotations like decency, morality, etc. which makes it a rather complex issue. Courts have from time and again introduced test/standard which have evolved from society to society. Do you think time has come to give definite meaning to word obscenity. Does section 67, 67A and 67B should satisfy the test laid down in Aveek Sarkar."

Model answer: Yes, and they do. Section 67 reproduces the s.292 IPC formula almost verbatim — "lascivious or appeals to the prurient interest or ... tend to deprave and corrupt persons who are likely, having regard to all relevant circumstances, to read, see or hear the matter" — so the Aveek Sarkar community‑standards test applies to it without modification. Sections 67A and 67B operate at a higher threshold ("sexually explicit act"; children), and the community‑standards analysis is engaged before that threshold is reached, because s.67A and s.67B are parasitic on the notion of sexual explicitness, which is a stricter and more objective standard than obscenity. Whether the legislature should now define "obscenity" and "sexually explicit" — neither is defined in the IT Act — is a fair question for a critical answer: the absence of a definition forces courts to import the IPC jurisprudence wholesale, which produces uncertainty at the charging stage. The better view is that a statutory definition would risk freezing a standard that must, by its nature, evolve with society; the community‑standards test performs that function better than a fixed definition. For s.67B, however, the position is different: because the subject matter is children, a per se rule is appropriate, and the Supreme Court in Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish has adopted a relaxed prima facie test for identifying a child in a depiction, precisely because the material's purpose is prosecutorial screening rather than final adjudication.

Exercise 2: "Can a person maintaining a commercial website featuring sexually explicit content be said to be publishing obscene material which is punishable under the Act."

Model answer: Yes. The word "publication" is not defined in the IT Act, but s.67 punishes whoever "publishes or transmits or causes to be published or transmitted in the electronic form" obscene material. Making content available on a website accessible to the public is publication in electronic form — a proposition supported by the English decision in R v. Graham Waddon (2000) WL 491456, which treated the making available of material on a website as publication. Where the website is maintained commercially, the case is stronger: the operator publishes for gain, the audience is the public at large, and the aggravating features of commercial exploitation and scale are present. Section 67A is engaged where the content contains a sexually explicit act, and s.67B where it depicts children. The operator cannot shelter behind s.79's intermediary safe harbour, because a person who creates, selects and hosts the content is not an intermediary "whose function is limited to providing access" — he is the primary publisher. Note the parallel with the DU syllabus's Nov–Dec 2025 problem question on a creator who uploads sexually explicit videos that go viral, where the creator is the primary publisher and the platform's liability is analysed separately under s.79.

14Case brief 6: Maqbool Fida Husain v. Raj Kumar Pandey

DU CASE‑MAT 6 · obscenity + online publication + jurisdiction  The companion case to Aveek Sarkar, and the case that addresses the cyberspace‑specific jurisdiction problem created by a website accessible across the globe.

CASE‑MAT 6 · TIER 2 · ARTISTIC FREEDOM AND ONLINE PUBLICATION

Maqbool Fida Husain v. Raj Kumar Pandey

Criminal Revision Petition No. 114/2007 Delhi High Court Decided 8 May 2008 Sanjay Kishan Kaul, J. ss.292, 294, 298, 500 IPC

1Citation & Bench

Maqbool Fida Husain v. Raj Kumar Pandey, Criminal Revision Petition No. 114/2007, Delhi High Court, decided 8 May 2008 by Sanjay Kishan Kaul, J. Revision petitions challenging the summoning orders passed by the learned ACMM, Delhi, on the transfer of consolidated complaint cases from Pandharpur (Maharashtra), Rajkot (Gujarat), Indore and Bhopal (Madhya Pradesh) under the Supreme Court's directions in T.P. (Cri.) No. 129/2006, 182/2006 and 224/2006, pursuant to the order dated 04‑12‑2006. Printed in the DU case material at pages 66–78.

2Admission of Facts

The petitions challenged the summoning orders against the petitioner, an accomplished painter, arising from a contemporary painting celebrating nudity. The painting depicted India in an abstract and graphical representation of a woman in nude with her hair flowing in the form of the Himalayas, displaying her agony.

The painting was sold to a private collector in 2004, and the petitioner did not deal with it in any manner whatsoever after the sale. Subsequently, in 2006, the painting, entitled "Bharat Mata", was advertised as part of an on‑line auction for charity for Kashmir earthquake victims organised by a non‑governmental organisation with which the petitioner claimed to have no involvement. The petitioner stated that at no point in time had he given a title to the painting.

The advertisement led to large‑scale protests, for which the petitioner had to tender an apology. In this background, private complaints were filed at various parts of the country — Pandharpur (Maharashtra), Rajkot (Gujarat), Indore and Bhopal (Madhya Pradesh) — alleging various offences against the petitioner on account of the painting, consequent to which summons and warrants of arrest were issued. The petitioner approached the Supreme Court seeking consolidation, which acceded to the request and directed that the complaint cases be consolidated and transferred to the court of the ACMM, Delhi. The ACMM issued summons to the petitioner for offences under ss.292, 294 and 298 IPC, against which the present revision petitions were filed.

The Court issued notices and granted the petitioner exemption from personal appearance. In view of the propositions having arisen, the Court deemed it appropriate to issue court notice to the Attorney General in order to depute a law officer for assistance; the learned ASG assisted the Court on the jurisdictional question. In Crl. Rev. P. 114/2007 and 280/2007, none sought to appear and argue for the respondents, so the Court closed their right to advance further submissions by order dated 20‑03‑2008 (though the GPA holder of the respondent in Crl. Rev. P. 280/2007 later entered appearance and was permitted to make further submissions).

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the painting is "obscene" within s.292 IPC — i.e. whether it is (i) lascivious, (ii) appeals to the prurient interest, or (iii) tends to deprave and corrupt persons likely to see it — read with the exceptions.
  2. Whether an offence under s.294 IPC (obscene acts and songs, to the annoyance of others) is made out where the only act of the petitioner said to have a nexus with the alleged annoyance is the uploading of the painting on the website.
  3. Whether an offence under s.298 IPC (uttering words etc. with deliberate intent to wound religious feelings) is made out, absent deliberate intention.
  4. Whether an offence under s.500 IPC (defamation) is made out, absent an imputation capable of harming the complainant's reputation.
  5. Which court has jurisdiction to try the matter where the painting, uploaded on a website accessible to people across the globe, was viewed by different complainants across the country who filed complaints at various places in India.
  6. Whether the summoning orders and warrants of arrest should be quashed, having regard to the harassment of persons in the creative fields.

4Examination of Reasoning — the arguments of both sides

Arguments of the petitioner
  • To make out a case under s.294, the impugned act must be performed in a public place, must be obscene, and must cause annoyance to others. Relying on Narendra H. Khurana v. Commissioner of Police, 2004 Cri.L.J. 3393, there was no prima facie case because there was no disclosure of any immediate, proximate nexus between the alleged annoyance of the complainants and any act done in a public place by the petitioner. The alleged annoyance could have been caused by viewing the painting on the internet, and the only alleged act of the petitioner having a nexus with it is the uploading of the painting on the website. Further, there could have been no "annoyance" because the complainants could have easily chosen not to view the website any further.
  • For an offence under s.298, the accused must have a deliberate intention of wounding the religious feelings of the complainant by uttering a word, making a sound or gesture, or placing an object in the sight of the complainant. The petitioner had done no such act. Reliance was placed on Narayan Das v. State, Shalibhadra Shah v. Swami Krishna Bharati, 1981 Cri. L.J. 113 and Acharya Rajneesh v. Naval Thakur, 1990 Cri.L.J. 2511 for the proposition that mere knowledge of the likelihood that religious feelings may be wounded is not sufficient.
  • As to s.500, the basic ingredient of defamation — some imputation capable of harming the reputation of the complainant — is absent.
  • The impugned painting cannot form the basis of any deliberate intention to wound religious feelings: the figure represents an anthropomorphic depiction of the nation, is not a religious depiction capable of offending Hindu religious feelings, and the concept of Bharat Mata is not the sole premise or belief of Hindus alone.
  • On obscenity: the painting is not lascivious, does not appeal to the prurient interest, and does not tend to deprave or corrupt.
  • The painting was sold in 2004, the petitioner did not deal with it thereafter, and the 2006 auction was organised by an NGO with which he had no involvement; he never gave the painting its title; he withdrew it from the auction and apologised — all showing artistic impulse, not insult.
Arguments of the complainants / respondents
  • The painting was not put up for display in some art gallery or private exhibition; instead it was uploaded on the artist's own website, which could be accessed by any person, and any common man who is a patriot would get affected by the picture. Hence the yardstick should be the mindset of society as a whole and not of a particular "class".
  • The painting was obscene and lascivious, offended religious feelings, and damaged the complainant's reputation.
  • The summons were rightly issued and the revision petitions should be dismissed.

5Matrix of Holding

(a) Section 292 — the test, stated precisely.

⚖️ The three conditions and the exceptions

"On a bare reading of Sub‑section (1) of Section 292 it is obvious that a book etc. shall be deemed to be obscene (i) if it is lascivious; (ii) it appeals to the prurient interest, and (iii) it tends to deprave and corrupt persons who are likely to read, see or hear the matter alleged to be obscene. It is only once the impugned matter is found to be obscene that the question of whether the impugned matter falls within any of the exceptions contained in the section would arise." And: "the relevance of exceptions arises in excluding otherwise obscene matter from the ambit of the criminal offence of obscenity and such exceptions has no role to play in determination of the obscenity of the impugned matter."

The Court also set out the exceptions — the section does not extend to any book, pamphlet, paper, writing, drawing, painting, representation or figure the publication of which is proved to be justified as being for the public good on the ground that it is in the interest of science, literature, art or learning or other objects of general concern, or which is kept or used bona fide for religious purposes; nor to representations on ancient monuments or temples. The Court noted that s.292 was enacted by the Obscene Publications Act to give effect to Article I of the International Convention for the Suppression of Traffic in Obscene Publications, to which India is a signatory, and was amended by Act 36 of 1969 to give more precise meaning to "obscene" and to create the public‑good exception.

(b) The legal test — at the extreme end of the spectrum, and the artist's perspective.

⚖️ The formulation to quote

"To fall within the scope of 'obscene' under Section 292 & 294 IPC, the ingredients of the impugned matter/art must lie at the extreme end of the spectrum of the offensive matter. The legal test of obscenity is satisfied only when the impugned art/matter can be said to appeal to a unhealthy, inordinate person having perverted interest in sexual matters or having a tendency to morally corrupt and debase persons likely to come in contact with the impugned art."

"It must also be remembered that a piece of art may be vulgar but not obscene. In order to arrive at a dispassionate conclusion where it is crucial to understand that art from the perspective of the painter, it is also important to picture the same from a spectator's point of view who is likely to see it."

(c) Application — the painting is not obscene.

⚖️ The reasoning — read the whole passage, it is examinable

"On applying the said tests governing obscenity, in my considered view, the said painting cannot be said to fall within the purview of Section 292 thereby making it obscene. The impugned painting on the face of it is neither lascivious nor appeals to the prurient interests. At the same time, the person who is likely to view the said painting would not tend to be depraved or corrupted. In other words, the said painting would not arouse sexual interest in a perverted inordinate person or would not morally corrupt and debase a person viewing the said painting. Though some might feel offended or disgusted at the very inception of seeing the alleged Mother India in nude but that by itself and nothing more in my opinion is not sufficient to qualify the test of obscenity. The said painting depicting India in a human form in no manner has that tendency to make an average person feel embarrassed by naked portrayal of a concept which has no particular face to it since the painting has not lost its artistic value/touch."

"An attempt to understand the said painting from the artist's/petitioner's perspective would show how the painter by way of an abstract expression has tried to elucidate the concept of a nation in the form of a distressed woman. No doubt, the concept of a nation has had a long association with the idea of motherhood but just because the artist has expressed it in nude does not make the painting obscene per se thereby satisfying the test that nudity or sex alone cannot be said to be obscene. If the painting is looked as a whole, it would reveal that the revulsion referred to by learned Counsel for the respondents of patriotic nationals would not arise for the reason that except the fact that it is in nude, there is nothing which can be considered as pinching to the eye. As a matter of fact, the aesthetic touch to the painting dwarfs the so called obscenity in the form of nudity and renders it so picayune and insignificant that the nudity in the painting can easily be overlooked."

"One of the tests in relation to judging nude/semi nude pictures of women as obscene is also a particular posture or pose or the surrounding circumstances which may render it to be obscene but in the present painting, apart from what is already stated above, the contours of the woman's body represent nothing more than the boundaries/map of India. There can be a numbers of postures or poses that one can think of which can really stimulate a man's deepest hidden passions and desires. To my mind, art should not be seen in isolation without going into its onomatopoetic meaning and it is here I quote Mr. Justice Stewart of the US Supreme Court in Jacobellis v. Ohio, 378 U.S. 184 (1964) who defined 'obscenity' as, 'I will know it when I see it'."

On the artist's intent: "The object of painting the woman in nude is also part of the same expression and is obviously not to stimulate the viewer's prurience but instead to shake up the very conscious of the viewer and to invoke in him empathy for India and abhorrence for the culprits. The person who may view the painting is likely to react in tears, silence or analogous to the same but no way near the feelings of lust." And: "It would not be proper to hold that the painter/petitioner had a deliberate intention to manifestly insult Bharat Mata which is clear from his various interviews and reports placed on record where he has consistently maintained that he actually celebrates nudity and considers it as the purest form of expression. It also cannot be lost sight of that he had immediately withdrawn the said painting from the auction and apologised to those offended, thus making it clear that his is only an artistic impulse. Under the criminal jurisprudence, for an offence to be made out against an accused, the ingredients of means rea and actus reas need to be proved. In the present case, since the scope of the subject is so limited, it does not really require any evidence to be led and on the face of it, both the elements i.e. means rea and actus reas appear to be absent."

(d) Section 294 — no annoyance, because viewing was voluntary.

⚖️ The s.294 holding — the online‑specific point

"I am unable to accept the plea raised by the learned Counsel for the respondents that the said painting uploaded on a website could be accessed by any person sitting across the globe who in consequence whereto could get affected by viewing the same. There can be no exasperation caused by viewing such painting on the website for the reason that a person would firstly access such a website only if he has some interest in art and that too contemporary art and in case he does view such a website, he always would have the option to not to view or close the said web page. It seems that the complainants are not the types who would go to art galleries or have an interest in contemporary art, because if they did, they would know that there are many other artists who embrace nudity as part of their contemporary art. Hence, the offence alleged Under Section 294 IPC can not be made out."

(e) Section 298 and s.500 — no deliberate intention, no defamatory imputation. "Similarly, the ingredients of Section 298 IPC as alleged are not met since there seems to be no deliberate intention on the part of the petitioner to hurt feelings of Indians as already stated and as a matter of fact, the subject matter i.e Bharat Mata could be alleged to wound nationalist feelings of an individual and not any religious feelings. I am in agreement with the contention raised by the learned Counsel for the petitioner that the impugned painting cannot form the basis of any deliberate intention to wound the religious feelings of the complainants since the figure, on the basis of the identity alleged, represents an anthropomorphic depiction of a nation as also that to hold a person liable under the above said section, mere knowledge of the likelihood that the religious feelings of another person may be wounded would not be sufficient." And: "Section 500 IPC requires the basic ingredient of defamation to be satisfied which seems to be completely absent in the present case."

(f) The result, with a plea for tolerance. "In view of the aforesaid, the summoning orders and warrants of arrest issued against the petitioner in the complaint cases are quashed and the revision petitions filed against them are allowed leaving the parties to bear their own costs." The Court added: "Every time an artist portrays something different, something which is an unpopular view point, it may accompany discomfort and unpleasantness but that in itself cannot be a ground to curb the artistic freedom and quickly go on to label it as obscene. There might be people who may actually get offended by those of Hussain's paintings or others but the right course of action for them, is to simply shrug it off or protest peacefully. ... But criminal Justice system ought not to be invoked as a convenient recourse to ventilate any and all objections to an artistic work. It should not be used as a mere tool in the hands of unscrupulous masters which in the process can cause serious violations of the rights of the people especially taking into consideration the people in the creative fields. Such a pernicious trend represents a growing intolerance and divisiveness within the society which pose a threat to the democratic fabric of our nation."

(g) The jurisdictional discussion — a whole treatise in miniature. The Court set out the problem: "which court would be considered as a competent court having the jurisdiction to try the matter particularly when the nature of the case is like the present one where the impugned painting uploaded on the website, accessible to people across the globe, was being viewed by different people/complainants across the country who in turn got offended with such painting and filed their complaints at various places in India, especially keeping in mind the vexatious and frivolous complaints which can be filed as an instrument to harass the accused."

The learned ASG accepted that the Criminal Code does not deal with such jurisdictional aspects directly, and that the answer rested in s.406 Cr.P.C. (the power of the Supreme Court to transfer cases) — which was the procedure adopted here. The Court then analysed:

  • s.177 — every offence shall ordinarily be inquired into and tried by a court within whose local jurisdiction it was committed.
  • s.178 — where it is uncertain in which of several local areas an offence was committed, or where an offence is committed partly in one local area and partly in another, or is a continuing one continuing in more than one local area, or consists of several acts done in different local areas, it may be tried by a court having jurisdiction over any of such local areas.
  • s.179 — "When an act is an offence by reason of anything which has been done and of a consequence which has ensued, the offence may be inquired into or tried by a Court within whose local jurisdiction such thing has been done or such consequence has ensued." The Court held that the conjunction "and" shows the act becomes an offence on account of the cumulative effect of the things done and the consequences ensued.
  • Illustrations: Ashok v. State of U.P, 2005 Cri L.J. 2324 — where a political leader made insulting remarks in an interview to a weekly magazine having all‑India circulation, the Courts either in the place where the interview was given or where the consequences ensued had jurisdiction; and S. Bangarappa v. Ganesh Narayan, 1984 Cri L.J. 1618 — where a defamatory statement made at a press conference was published in a newspaper in the same place and, the next day, in a newspaper in a different place, the Court in the second place had jurisdiction.
  • s.186 — where two or more courts have taken cognizance of the same offence, the High Court resolves the jurisdictional doubt; and Kuljit Singh v. CBI, 2000 CriLJ 3681 (the high‑sea tragedy case) applied s.186 to hold that the CMM, Delhi alone was competent.
  • s.202 — the Magistrate should postpone the issue of process where the accused resides beyond his jurisdiction, and either enquire himself or direct investigation, for the purpose of deciding whether there is sufficient ground for proceeding under s.204.

The Court concluded that in the information age, "jurisdiction be more circumscribed so that an artist like in the present case is not made to run from pillar to post facing proceedings", but that "this is not an aspect where a direction can be issued since it is within the domain of appropriate legislation", and recorded the ASG's undertaking to advise the Government to take legislative steps.

Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. A piece of art may be vulgar but not obscene. To be obscene, the matter must lie at the extreme end of the spectrum of the offensive, and appeal to an unhealthy, inordinate person having perverted interest in sexual matters, or have a tendency to morally corrupt and debase persons likely to come in contact with it.
  2. Nudity or sex alone is not obscenity. The artist's perspective must be understood, and the work must also be pictured from the spectator's point of view. Where the aesthetic touch "dwarfs" the nudity, the work is not obscene.
  3. Offence or disgust at a depiction is not enough. "Some might feel offended or disgusted at the very inception ... but that by itself and nothing more ... is not sufficient to qualify the test of obscenity."
  4. Both mens rea and actus reus must appear on the face of the record before a criminal prosecution can proceed — here both were absent, and the petitioner's withdrawal of the painting from the auction and his apology showed artistic impulse, not insult.
  5. Section 294 requires an immediate, proximate nexus between an act done in a public place and the annoyance caused. Where the only act is the uploading of a painting on a website, and the viewer accessed the site by choice and could have closed the page, no annoyance is caused.
  6. Mere knowledge that religious feelings may be wounded is not enough for s.298 — deliberate intention is required, and the depiction of the nation is not a religious depiction capable of offending religious feelings.
  7. The criminal justice system must not be used as a convenient recourse to ventilate objections to artistic work. A magistrate must scrutinise each case to prevent vexatious and frivolous complaints from harassing persons in the creative fields, and should postpone the issue of process under s.202 where the accused resides beyond his jurisdiction.
  8. Cyberspace jurisdiction: where content is uploaded on a website accessible across the globe and complainants in multiple places are offended, ss.177–179 and 186 Cr.P.C. govern, with the place where the act was done or where the consequence ensued both competent — a problem the Court said requires legislative, not judicial, solution.

6Application & Significance

M.F. Husain is the direct precedent for the "auctioned online painting" hypothetical that appears in DU problem questions. It applies the same community‑standards/context‑and‑message approach that Aveek Sarkar later crystallised, and it adds two things Aveek Sarkar does not: the artist's‑perspective test and the online‑publication‑and‑jurisdiction analysis. It is the case to cite for the proposition that nudity in art is not obscenity and for the harassment‑of‑artists critique of private complaints.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Use M.F. Husain for four propositions: (1) "A piece of art may be vulgar but not obscene; the ingredients of the impugned art must lie at the extreme end of the spectrum of the offensive matter"; (2) "Nudity or sex alone is not obscenity — the artist's perspective and the spectator's viewpoint must both be considered"; (3) "Section 294 IPC requires an immediate, proximate nexus between an act done in a public place and the annoyance caused; uploading on a website, which the viewer could have closed, causes no annoyance"; and (4) "The criminal justice system ought not to be invoked as a convenient recourse to ventilate objections to an artistic work."

🎯 The self‑learning exercise printed with the case — model answer

"If a poet through his poetry classify Mother India in the same fashion by his words as described by the artist in his painting. Can you apply the same principle as laid in MF Hussain Case? (Samresh Bose v. Amal Mitra, (1986 AIR 967), Difference between obscenity and vulgarity)"

Model answer: Yes, in substance. The M.F. Husain principle is medium‑neutral: what matters is not whether the expression is visual or verbal, but whether it is lascivious, appeals to the prurient interest, or tends to deprave and corrupt persons likely to read, see or hear it. A poem that depicts Mother India in the nude is subject to exactly the same test — and, read in context, would very likely pass it, for the same reasons the painting did: the depiction is an abstract expression of national anguish, the aesthetic and literary qualities dwarf the nudity, and the object is to "shake up the very conscious of the viewer and to invoke in him empathy for India", not to stimulate prurience. But note the important difference drawn in Samresh Bose v. Amal Mitra, (1986) AIR 967 (and applied in M.F. Husain): a work may be vulgar but not obscene. Vulgarity is a matter of taste; obscenity is a matter of the three statutory conditions. The Court in Samresh Bose held that a story which is vulgar and written in bad taste is not thereby obscene, and that the test is whether the material tends to deprave and corrupt. The poet would additionally have the benefit of the s.292 exception for matter justified as being for the public good as being in the interest of literature. The one respect in which the two media differ is the jurisdictional one: a poem published in a book or a website raises the same ss.177–179 Cr.P.C. problem that M.F. Husain identified, and the same legislative reform is needed.

CASE‑MAT 7 · TIER 1 · THE FIRST s.67 CONVICTION

State of Tamil Nadu v. Suhas Katti

1Citation & Bench

State of Tamil Nadu v. Suhas Katti, C.C. No. 4680/2004, decided by Shri Arul Raj, Additional Chief Metropolitan Magistrate, Egmore, Chennai on 5 November 2004 — one of India's earliest cyber‑crime convictions, within about seven months of the filing. Source: http://www.prashantmali.com/cyber‑law‑cases and http://www.legalserviceindia.com/lawforum/index.php?topic=2238.0. Printed in the DU case material at pages 51–54.

The Assistant Commissioner of Police, Cyber Crime Cell, C.C.B., Egmore, Chennai filed the final report against the accused. The case was that on 7.2.2004, in the evening, at Cyber Café "Hello World Centre", Sion, Mumbai, having an I.P. 61.11.10.99, the accused, with the intention of harming the reputation of the complainant Ms. R, created a user ID in her name and composed an obscene message intending that such document should be used for posting in different obscene Yahoo Groups, with the intention of making others believe that the document was made by her, so that persons seeing the obscene message would send offending calls to her, in harming her reputation and insulting her modesty by the words exhibited in the e‑mail.

On 9.2.2004, in the morning, at Cyber Café "Heighten Advertising", Mahim, Mumbai, having an IP 202.88.165.53, the accused again entered the user ID which had been created by him in the name of the complainant, composed an obscene message with the same intention, and posted it. Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. Authorship of an online offence may be proved by circumstantial and forensic digital evidence alone. Where there is no eyewitness, the combination of IP trace → ISP subscriber records → cyber‑café identification and register → password knowledge establishes the identification chain.
  2. The accused's knowledge of a password known only to him is powerful evidence of authorship. The Court relied on the accused giving the password "an rose" in his confession statement, with which the officials obtained the printouts — a fact within his exclusive knowledge.
  3. Cyber‑café registers are admissible and probative. PW‑9's register Ex.P.24, in which the accused signed as "R", corroborated his presence at the browsing centre from which the postings originated.
  4. Section 469 IPC applies to the creation of a forged electronic record — here, a user ID created in the complainant's name with intent to harm her reputation.
  5. Section 67 is engaged by the posting of obscene messages in electronic form, and the offence is complete on publication in the Yahoo groups.
  6. Motive, prior statements and a pattern of rejected advances are relevant to authorship and to sentence. The Court relied on the accused's own 1997 statement that posting a phone number was "the best way to spoil the reputation of a woman", and on his refusal‑driven revenge motive.
  7. Repetition over two days defeats any plea of heat of passion, and education and sophistication defeat any plea for leniency.
  8. The mental suffering and humiliation of the victim of online obscene harassment cannot be compensated in money or solaced by words — a sentence that carries the moral force of the case.

6Application & Significance

Suhas Katti is the earliest successful s.67 conviction in India and the standard authority on evidentiary methodology in obscenity prosecutions. It is the case to cite whenever a question asks how an online offence is proved where the offender was anonymous, and it pairs with Yogisha Prabhu (§16 below), which applies the same evidentiary method a decade later and adds the crucial distinction between publication (ss.67/67A) and privacy intrusion (s.66E).

Model answer: Provisions engaged. The group's conduct attracts s.67B of the IT Act — publishing or transmitting material depicting children in a sexually explicit act, and creating text or digital images, collecting, seeking, browsing, downloading, advertising, promoting, exchanging or distributing such material — which is cognizable and non‑bailable, punishable on first conviction with up to 5 years and fine up to ₹10 lakh and on subsequent conviction with up to 7 years and ₹10 lakh. Where the morphed image involves a child, s.15 of the POCSO Act, 2012 is also engaged, and Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish (2024) holds that mere possession is independently punishable under both, with no need to prove active transmission. Sections 67 (obscene) and 67A (sexually explicit act) are engaged in addition, and s.66E where images of a private area are involved. Because the offenders are minors, the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 applies: a person below 18 at the time of the offence is dealt with under its provisions, not as an adult, and the Bois Locker Room incident is the paradigm example. Differentiating members. Indian law does differentiate, but not through a single provision. (a) Active members — those who created, posted, morphed or shared the material — are the principal offenders under s.67B and POCSO, and their liability is clear. (b) Passive members who downloaded, saved or forwarded the material are liable under s.67B's express reference to "collects, seeks, browses, downloads, advertises, promotes, exchanges or distributes", and under POCSO s.15 for storage in specified circumstances — S. Harish confirms that possession alone suffices. (c) Those who joined under peer pressure and never accessed the material present the hardest case: mere membership of a group, without access, browsing or distribution, should not attract liability, because s.67B requires an act — and the S. Harish "prima facie subjective satisfaction" test is directed at identifying whether a depiction involves a child, not at lowering the mens rea threshold for the accused. The safer course is to treat unwilling members as witnesses and victims of peer pressure rather than offenders, while imposing clear duties to report and to exit and delete — the very conduct s.15 POCSO treats as exculpatory. A differentiated, rehabilitation‑oriented response under the JJ Act, combined with a statutory duty to report, is the answer the law should give.

Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. "Publication" is an essential element of ss.67 and 67A. Where the obscene or sexually explicit material was sent only to the victim and not made available to the public or any section of it, the publication element is not satisfied, and the charges fail. "Evidence as to publishing open sexual desire is short in present case to lead any other person than reporter."
  2. Section 66E requires no publication. It punishes the intentional or knowing intervention upon another's privacy — here, sending obscene and pornographic e‑mails from a bogus ID. Where the higher offence fails for want of publication but the conduct intrudes on privacy, s.66E is the cognate lesser offence.
  3. A court may convict of a lesser offence not specifically charged where it carries a lesser punishment and the accused had notice of the underlying facts and suffers no prejudice. The charge under s.509 IPC gave the accused knowledge of his defence.
  4. A unique password known only to the user is the key to attribution. Where the accused opened the disputed e‑mail ID using a password known only to him, the fact was within his exclusive knowledge and satisfied s.27 of the Indian Evidence Act — but such evidence is "a weak piece of evidence" and must be fortified.
  5. Fortification comes from the forensic chain: a mirror image of the hard disk, hash values calculated and re‑verified at each stage, recovery of deleted files, and the matching of file creation dates with the dates on which the complainant received the e‑mails.
  6. Traces of the victim's data in the accused's machine are powerful circumstantial evidence — here, traces of the disputed e‑mails and of the complainant's Orkut profile in the unallocated cluster of the laptop allotted to the accused, for which "there was no reason" absent the accused's involvement.
  7. A shared laptop is not a defence where the disputed e‑mail ID was a personal ID with a unique password not available to colleagues, and where the accused alone knew the complainant's e‑mail address.
  8. Technology supplies circumstances; the human mind supplies the inference. "The technology help us to find out the circumstances and the human as to apply reason to it and has to draw inferences and come on conclusion and that is the only part of the technology which can be utilised in judicial proceeding."
  9. The investigative sequence defeats an enmity plea. Where the complainant did not name the accused at the first approach and named him only after the Cyber Cell's technical investigation reached him, the suggestion that she implicated him out of vengeance fails.

It is also the best Indian illustration of the full forensic evidentiary method: mirror image, hash values, unallocated‑cluster recovery, file‑creation‑date matching. Read with Suhas Katti, it gives the complete evidentiary picture for online obscenity and harassment prosecutions.

Cite Yogisha Prabhu for: (1) "Publication to persons beyond the victim is an essential element of ss.67 and 67A; where obscene material is sent only to the victim, those sections are not attracted"; (2) "Section 66E requires no publication — it punishes intentional intrusion upon privacy — and is the cognate lesser offence where the publication element of ss.67/67A fails"; and (3) "A unique e‑mail password known only to the user satisfies s.27 of the Indian Evidence Act, but must be fortified by forensic evidence: mirror image, hash values, unallocated‑cluster recovery, and matching file‑creation dates."

🎯 The self‑learning exercise printed with the case — model answer

"In above case if the objectionable material has been sent to the minor what will be your decision. (Hint — section 67B, IT Act, POCSO Act)"

Model answer: The analysis changes materially in three respects. First, the age of the recipient does not cure the publication problem for ss.67 and 67A: the prosecution must still prove that the material was published or transmitted to the public or a section of it, not merely sent to the victim. Sending objectionable material to a minor is still one‑to‑one transmission, and Yogisha Prabhu holds that this does not satisfy the publication element. Second, however, the case for s.67B is strong, but only if the material depicts a child engaged in a sexually explicit act — the recipient's minority is not what engages s.67B; the depicted person's minority is. Section 67B punishes publishing or transmitting material depicting children in a sexually explicit act, and also creating, collecting, seeking, browsing, downloading, advertising, promoting, exchanging or distributing such material. If the material sent to the minor depicted a child, s.67B is engaged — and it is cognizable and non‑bailable, punishable on first conviction with up to 5 years and fine up to ₹10 lakh. Third, where the conduct is directed at a minor — enticing or cultivating a child for a sexually explicit act — s.67B(c) and the POCSO Act, 2012 are engaged: s.11 (sexual harassment), s.12 (sexual assault), and s.13 (use of a child for pornographic purposes). Section 15 POCSO criminalises storage of child sexual abuse material for commercial or transmission purposes, and Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish (2024) holds that mere possession is independently punishable — so the sender's retention of the material on his device is itself an offence. Applying the S. Harish "prima facie subjective satisfaction" test, the court need only be satisfied that an ordinary prudent person would reasonably believe the depiction involves a child. The result: conviction under s.66E (privacy intrusion) and s.509 IPC as in the actual case, with the addition of s.67B and the POCSO provisions, and a substantially higher sentence.

17Case brief 9: Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish

DU CASE‑MAT 9 · ss.67B, POCSO  The most important post‑2020 development in Unit IV — and the case that answers every CSEAM problem question.

CASE‑MAT 9 · TIER 1 · THE CSEAM AUTHORITY

Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish

2024 SCC OnLine SC 2611 (2024) INSC 716 Supreme Court of India Decided 23 September 2024 D.Y. Chandrachud, CJI and J.B. Pardiwala, JJ. Criminal Appeal Nos. 2161‑2162 of 2024

1Citation & Bench

Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish, 2024 SCC OnLine SC 2611; (2024) INSC 716, Supreme Court of India, decided 23 September 2024 by the Chief Justice of India, D.Y. Chandrachud, and J.B. Pardiwala, JJ., in Criminal Appeal Nos. 2161‑2162 of 2024. An appeal against the judgment of the Madras High Court quashing proceedings against the respondent.

2Admission of Facts

A National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) Cyber Tipline Report flagged the respondent as a regular consumer of child pornography. A forensic analysis of his phone found 108 pornographic files, including two depicting minors. A charge sheet was filed under s.67B of the IT Act and s.15(1) of the POCSO Act, 2012. The Madras High Court quashed the proceedings, holding that mere passive possession or viewing — without active transmission or distribution — did not attract liability under either provision. Against that judgment the present appeals were preferred.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Does mere possession or viewing of child sexual exploitation material, without proof of active dissemination, constitute an offence under s.15 of the POCSO Act, 2012 and/or s.67B of the IT Act?
  2. What test should courts apply to determine whether a depiction "involves a child"?
  3. What terminology should the law use — "child pornography" or something more accurate?

4Examination of Reasoning — the arguments of both sides

Arguments of the appellant (Just Rights for Children Alliance)
  • Section 67B is broadly worded and expressly reaches a person who "browses, downloads" material depicting children in a sexually explicit act — conduct that does not require transmission.
  • Section 15 POCSO independently criminalises storage and possession of child sexual abuse material in specified circumstances — failure to delete, destroy or report; or storage for commercial or transmission purposes.
  • Passive consumption is not harmless: it creates and sustains the market for child sexual abuse material, and each view is a fresh violation of the depicted child's rights.
  • The Madras High Court's "transmission‑only" reading would gut both provisions and leave a large enforcement gap.
Position of the respondent / Madras High Court
  • Both s.67B and s.15 POCSO are directed at those who produce, publish, transmit or distribute such material; a person who merely possesses or views it does not commit the offence.
  • Reading possession into the offence would criminalise inadvertent receipt — unsolicited material in a group chat, browser cache or auto‑download — without any mens rea.
  • The proceedings were therefore liable to be quashed.

5Matrix of Holding

⚖️ The three holdings — the substance of the judgment

(1) Possession is independently punishable. The Supreme Court reversed the Madras High Court, holding that s.15 POCSO independently criminalises storage and possession in the specified circumstances — failure to delete, destroy or report; or storage for commercial or transmission purposes — without requiring proof of actual transmission; and that s.67B of the IT Act, being broadly worded (expressly referring to one who "browses, downloads, ... facilitates abusing children online"), likewise reaches mere possession or viewing in appropriate circumstances.

(2) Terminology reform. The Court urged the replacement of the phrase "child pornography" with "Child Sexual Exploitation and Abuse Material (CSEAM)", to correctly characterise such material as evidence of abuse, not a form of consensual expression.

(3) The identification test. The Court laid down that courts assessing whether a depiction "involves a child" need only reach a prima facie, subjective satisfaction that an ordinary prudent person would reasonably believe the depiction involves, or appears to involve, a child — a lower threshold than formal age‑determination procedures, appropriate given that the material's evident purpose is prosecutorial screening, not final adjudication of the depicted person's identity.

Result: the Madras High Court's quashing order was set aside and the criminal proceedings against the respondent were restored.

Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. Passive possession is independently punishable. Neither s.67B nor s.15 POCSO requires proof of active transmission or distribution; storage and viewing suffice in the specified circumstances.
  2. The two provisions are complementary, not alternative. Section 15 POCSO addresses storage in specified circumstances; s.67B addresses the broader range of conduct — creating, collecting, seeking, browsing, downloading, advertising, promoting, exchanging and distributing.
  3. "CSEAM" replaces "child pornography". The material is evidence of abuse, not expression; the terminology matters because it frames the offence as a violation of the child's rights rather than a vice offence.
  4. A relaxed identification threshold. The court need only be prima facie subjectively satisfied that an ordinary prudent person would believe the depiction involves or appears to involve a child — because the purpose is prosecutorial screening.
  5. Cyber Tipline reports and forensic analysis of a phone are sufficient to found a charge sheet, without more.

6Application & Significance

S. Harish is the single most important Unit IV development of the decade and directly answers any problem question involving an intermediary hosting or an individual possessing CSEAM. It closes a significant enforcement gap left by the Madras High Court's reading, and it supplies the terminology and the identification test that every 2025–26 answer should use.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Always cite S. Harish for three propositions: (1) "Mere possession or viewing of child sexual exploitation material is independently punishable under s.15 of the POCSO Act, 2012 and s.67B of the IT Act — no proof of active transmission or distribution is required"; (2) "The Court urged the replacement of 'child pornography' with 'Child Sexual Exploitation and Abuse Material (CSEAM)', correctly characterising such material as evidence of abuse"; and (3) "Courts assessing whether a depiction involves a child need only reach a prima facie, subjective satisfaction that an ordinary prudent person would reasonably believe it involves or appears to involve a child."

⚠️ The unresolved question — flag it for extra credit

Defence‑side commentators flag the risk of over‑criminalising inadvertent possession — unsolicited receipt via group chats, browser cache or auto‑download — absent a clearly litigated mens rea threshold. The Supreme Court's prima facie subjective satisfaction test concerns the identification of the subject matter (whether a child is depicted), not the accused's knowledge or intent, and so does not fully address the point. A complete answer should note this as a live tension: "while doctrinally sound as child‑protection policy, possession‑based liability leaves the mens rea threshold for inadvertent receipt unresolved — a question the Court's identification test does not reach."

CASE‑MAT · TIER 3 · CYBER STALKING

Ritu Kohli Case (Delhi)

Delhi One of India's earliest reported cyber‑stalking complaints Pre‑2008 Amendment

1Citation & provenance

The Ritu Kohli case (Delhi) is one of India's earliest reported cyber‑stalking complaints, pre‑dating the 2008 Amendment's insertion of ss.66A and 66E. The DU case material lists the case in its syllabus and table of authorities but does not reproduce the judgment; the summary below follows the DU‑aligned case‑matrix notes and standard accounts, and that provenance is flagged.

2Facts

The complainant, Ritu Kohli, was subjected to a sustained campaign of online harassment by an acquaintance who, after she rebuffed his advances, began monitoring her use of the internet and electronic communications, tracking her movements and publishing her personal details — including her telephone number — online, with the consequence that she received a stream of unwanted and obscene calls and messages from strangers.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the conduct amounted to stalking within the criminal law, at a time when IPC s.354D did not exist (it was inserted by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013).
  2. Whether it amounted to an offence under the IT Act as it then stood — s.66 (hacking) and s.67 (obscene material in electronic form) being the only plausible candidates.
  3. Whether it amounted to an insult to the modesty of a woman under IPC s.509.

4Holding and significance

  • The case is the foundational reference for cyber stalking in India, and is routinely cited alongside Suhas Katti and Yogisha Prabhu as part of the line of online‑harassment‑of‑women authorities.
  • Its principal significance is illustrative and historical: it demonstrates the thinness of the statutory toolkit before 2008 and 2013. There was then no offence of stalking, no s.66E, and no s.66A. Prosecutors had to shoehorn the conduct into s.66, s.67, IPC s.509 and the general provisions on annoyance and harassment.
  • Today the same facts would be charged differently. BNS s.78 (formerly IPC s.354D) expressly covers a man who "monitors a woman's use of the internet, email or any other form of electronic communication", and is punishable on first conviction with up to 3 years and fine, and on subsequent conviction with up to 5 years and fine. BNS s.79 (formerly IPC s.509) covers the insult to modesty. s.66E applies where images of a private area are captured or transmitted, and s.67/s.67A where obscene or sexually explicit material is published. Section 43 supplies the civil remedy.
Principle
  1. Monitoring a woman's use of the internet and electronic communication is stalking — now expressly provided for by IPC s.354D / BNS s.78, and the doctrinal basis of the offence even before it was codified.
  2. Publishing a person's contact details online to invite unwanted contact is actionable — as an insult to modesty (IPC s.509 / BNS s.79), as obscenity where the content is obscene (s.67), and as a privacy intrusion (s.66E).
  3. The 2008 and 2013 amendments progressively filled the gap that cases like Ritu Kohli exposed — though the fall of s.66A in Shreya Singhal (2015) reopened part of it.
🎓 Exam takeaway

Use Ritu Kohli for the historical development paragraph on cyber stalking: "The Ritu Kohli case (Delhi) is one of India's earliest reported cyber‑stalking complaints, prosecuted at a time when the statutory toolkit was far thinner. Today the same conduct would be charged under IPC s.354D / BNS s.78 (stalking, expressly including monitoring a woman's use of the internet and electronic communication), read with IPC s.509 / BNS s.79, and where applicable ss.66E, 67 and 67A of the IT Act." Pair it with the critique at §19.2 on the gap left by s.66A's invalidation.

CASE‑MAT · TIER 3 · CYBER TERRORISM

Rakesh v. Central Bureau of Investigation

Delhi District Court Decided 5 February 2011 Delhi Blast case s.66F read with s.66

1Citation & provenance

Rakesh v. Central Bureau of Investigation, decided by the Delhi District Court on 5 February 2011 — one of the earliest prosecutions under s.66F (cyber terrorism), inserted by the 2008 Amendment. The DU case material lists the case in its syllabus under "Cyber Terrorism (s.66F)" with the parenthetical "(Delhi Blast case — terror e‑mail sent by hacking 'Wi Fi in Mumbai')" but does not reproduce the judgment; the summary below follows that syllabus description and standard accounts, and that provenance is flagged.

2Facts

Following the Delhi bomb blasts, a terror e‑mail claiming responsibility for the blasts was sent to media organisations and authorities. The e‑mail was traced to a hacked Wi‑Fi network in Mumbai — that is, the sender had gained unauthorised access to a wireless network and used it to transmit the message, in an attempt to conceal his identity and location. The investigation by the Central Bureau of Investigation traced the transmission through the compromised network and identified the accused, Rakesh.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the unauthorised access to and use of another's Wi‑Fi network amounted to hacking — i.e. an act referred to in s.43(a) (access without permission) done dishonestly or fraudulently, so as to attract s.66.
  2. Whether the conduct amounted to cyber terrorism under s.66F — specifically whether the accused, with intent to threaten the unity, integrity, security or sovereignty of India or to strike terror in the people, attempted to penetrate or access a computer resource without authorisation, and thereby caused or was likely to cause death or injuries or damage to property.
  3. Whether the evidence — the network logs, the IP trail and the forensic analysis — established the accused's authorship.

4Holding and significance

  • The case is the earliest reported prosecution under s.66F and established that the medium of transmission is no shield: a person who hacks a Wi‑Fi network to send a terror e‑mail commits both s.66 (hacking) and s.66F (cyber terrorism).
  • It illustrates the investigative method for a cyber‑terrorism case: tracing the transmission to a specific access point, obtaining the network owner's records, and correlating with other evidence.
  • It demonstrates the cumulative operation of Chapter XI: the same course of conduct attracts s.66 (up to 3 years / ₹5 lakh), s.66F (imprisonment for life), and the general terrorism provisions of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967.
  • It is the authority for the proposition that intent to strike terror is the gravamen of s.66F, and that the likelihood of death, injury, property damage or disruption of essential services suffices — the consequence need not materialise.
Principle
  1. Unauthorised access to a Wi‑Fi network is an act referred to in s.43(a), and done dishonestly or fraudulently attracts s.66.
  2. Using a hacked network to transmit a message claiming responsibility for a terrorist attack, with intent to strike terror, is cyber terrorism under s.66F, punishable with imprisonment for life.
  3. Concealment of identity through a compromised network aggravates rather than mitigates — it is itself the unauthorised access that grounds the s.66 charge.
🎓 Exam takeaway

Use Rakesh whenever a question involves cyber terrorism or a terror e‑mail. The correct formulation: "Rakesh v. Central Bureau of Investigation (Delhi District Court, 5 February 2011) — the Delhi Blast case — is the earliest reported prosecution under s.66F. A terror e‑mail claiming responsibility for the blasts was sent by hacking a Wi‑Fi network in Mumbai. The Court held that the unauthorised access to the network attracted s.66, and that using the compromised network to transmit a message with intent to strike terror was cyber terrorism under s.66F, punishable with imprisonment for life. The case establishes that the medium of transmission is no shield and that intent to strike terror is the gravamen of s.66F."

19.2 Critical analysis and doctrinal debates

🔍 Debate 1 — The gap left by s.66A

"The abolition of s.66A (struck down in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, discussed fully in Unit V/VI) left a conspicuous gap in India's cyber‑harassment toolkit: s.66A had criminalised sending 'grossly offensive' or 'menacing' information, and its absence means much online abuse now must be shoehorned into ill‑fitting provisions — s.66E (privacy, requires image capture of a private area), s.67/67A (requires obscenity or sexual explicitness), or general IPC/BNS provisions on intimidation or harassment not drafted with electronic communication in mind. Scholars (Halder & Jaishankar) argue this has left cyber stalking and non‑consensual harassment under‑prosecuted relative to the volume of complaints, a gap only partially filled by BNS s.78 (stalking, now expressly including online monitoring)."

🔍 Debate 2 — Publication vs. privacy intrusion

"Yogisha Prabhu shows courts are willing to convict on the lesser, better‑evidenced offence where publication (dissemination beyond the victim) cannot be proved. Critics query whether this under‑punishes genuinely harmful one‑to‑one harassment that nonetheless causes severe psychological harm. The doctrinal question is whether 'publication' in ss.67/67A should be read to include transmission to a single identifiable recipient, or whether the better answer is a legislative amendment expressly criminalising the non‑consensual transmission of sexually explicit material to an individual — a gap the IT Act has never filled."

🔍 Debate 3 — Possession‑based liability after S. Harish

"While doctrinally sound as child‑protection policy, possession‑based liability risks over‑criminalising inadvertent possession — unsolicited receipt via group chats, browser cache or auto‑download — absent a clearly litigated mens rea threshold. The Supreme Court's 'prima facie subjective satisfaction' test does not fully address the point, since it concerns identification of the subject matter, not the accused's knowledge or intent."

🔍 Debate 4 — The breadth of s.66F

"The second limb of s.66F — unauthorised access to restricted information 'likely to be used to injure' India's interests — has been criticised as vague and overbroad, echoing (in miniature) the vagueness concerns that led to s.66A's invalidation in Shreya Singhal. The list of protected interests includes 'decency or morality' and 'defamation', which sweeps in conduct that would otherwise be ordinary hacking. This is a live doctrinal tension examiners can be expected to probe."

🔍 Debate 5 — The definitional vacuum in the obscenity provisions

Neither "obscene" nor "sexually explicit" is defined in the IT Act. The result is that ss.67, 67A and 67B borrow their content wholesale from IPC s.292 and its judicial gloss — which is why Aveek Sarkar and M.F. Husain, both decided under the IPC, are controlling authority for the IT Act provisions. The advantage is that a single evolving standard applies across media; the disadvantage is uncertainty at the charging stage, since a police officer must predict how a court will apply the community‑standards test. The counter‑argument — that a statutory definition would freeze a standard that must evolve with society — is a strong one, and is the better view for s.67 and s.67A. For s.67B, however, a per se rule is appropriate, and the S. Harish relaxed identification test reflects that.

Fig. 5 — Unit IV revision mind map: four offence families converging on the obscenity ladder, governed by the two controlling tests. Redraw as four boxes feeding a central ladder, with two test boxes beneath.
PYQ 1 "A creator uploads sexually explicit/vulgar videos that go viral; police register cases under ss.67, 67A IT Act and ss.292, 294 IPC. Discuss liability of creator and platform." Nov–Dec 2025, Q.4 · 12–15 marks · Problem question
Model answer

Step 1 — Classify the material. Apply the Aveek Sarkar community‑standards test. Is the material (i) lascivious, (ii) appealing to the prurient interest, or (iii) tending to deprave and corrupt persons likely to read, see or hear it, judged as a whole, in context, by the standard of an average person applying contemporary community standards — not the most susceptible reader, and not the Hicklin isolated‑passage test, which Aveek Sarkar expressly rejected. Then ask the further question: does the material depict a sexually explicit act? If yes, s.67A; if it also depicts a child, s.67B. Nudity per se is not obscenity — but a sexually explicit act is a higher, more objective threshold than obscenity, and it does not require the community‑standards analysis to be satisfied at all: the question is simply whether the material contains a sexually explicit act or conduct.

Step 2 — The creator's liability. The creator is the primary publisher. Section 67 punishes whoever "publishes or transmits or causes to be published or transmitted in the electronic form" obscene material — first conviction: up to 3 years and fine up to ₹5 lakh; subsequent: up to 5 years and fine up to ₹10 lakh; cognizable, bailable (first conviction). Section 67A punishes the publication or transmission of material containing a sexually explicit act — first conviction: up to 5 years and fine up to ₹10 lakh; subsequent: up to 7 years and ₹10 lakh; cognizable, non‑bailable. Where the content depicts a child, s.67B applies, and Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish (2024) holds that mere possession or viewing is independently punishable under s.67B and s.15 POCSO. The IPC/BNS charges — s.292 (now BNS s.294) for sale etc. of obscene objects, and s.294 (now BNS s.296) for obscene acts and songs — run in parallel, and the same conduct is also civilly actionable under s.43.

Step 3 — The virality point. Virality aggravates rather than mitigates. The creator cannot say he intended a small audience: s.67 punishes whoever "causes to be published or transmitted", and the act of uploading to a platform designed for mass distribution is itself the publication. Note also that the s.67A penalty escalates on second or subsequent conviction, so a creator with prior convictions faces up to 7 years.

Step 4 — The platform's liability. Analyse under s.79 of the IT Act, read with the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, as amended in 2026 — cross‑refers to Unit V. The question is whether the platform is an intermediary within s.2(1)(w) and whether it satisfies the s.79(2) conditions: (a) its function is limited to providing access to a communication system over which the third‑party information is made available; (b) it does not initiate the transmission, select the receiver, or select or modify the information contained in the transmission; and (c) it observes due diligence and follows the prescribed guidelines. Where it does, s.79(1) exempts it from liability for third‑party information hosted on its platform. The safe harbour is lost where the intermediary conspired, abetted, aided or induced the unlawful act, or where, upon actual knowledge by court order or government notification, it fails to act expeditiously — within 36 hours — to remove or disable access. Shreya Singhal read down "actual knowledge" to require a court order or a government notification; a private complaint, without more, does not trigger the takedown obligation or forfeit the safe harbour.

Step 5 — The 2026 Amendment point. Where the content is AI‑generated or a deepfake, the IT (Intermediary Guidelines) Amendment Rules, 2026 (notified 10 February 2026, in force 20 February 2026) impose mandatory labelling and detection obligations for synthetically generated information and tightened takedown timelines. A platform that fails to label or detect such content risks losing the safe harbour — and the creator's liability for a deepfake is analysed under s.66D (cheating by personation) where a real person's likeness is used, in addition to ss.67/67A.

Step 6 — Conclusion. The creator is liable as primary publisher under ss.67 and 67A (and s.67B if children are depicted), together with the parallel IPC/BNS charges. The platform is not liable so long as it observes the s.79(2) due‑diligence conditions and acts expeditiously on a court order or government notification; it loses the safe harbour if it conspires, abetted or induced the conduct, or if it fails to act on such knowledge, or — for AI‑generated content — if it fails to comply with the 2026 labelling and detection obligations.

Conclusion: Creator liable under ss.67 and 67A IT Act plus ss.292/294 IPC (now BNS ss.294/296); platform protected by s.79 safe harbour subject to due diligence and the 2026 Amendment's labelling and takedown obligations.
PYQ 2 "An employee tampers with payroll source code to redirect salaries; another steals credentials to send fraudulent emails. Discuss liability of both under the IT Act." Nov–Dec 2025, Q.3 · 12–15 marks · Problem question
Model answer

Step 1 — Employee 1: the source‑code tamperer. Two provisions apply cumulatively.

  • s.65 — tampering with computer source documents. The elements are: (i) the accused knowingly or intentionally concealed, destroyed or altered computer source code, or knowingly or intentionally caused another to do so; (ii) the code was used for a computer, computer programme, computer system or computer network; and (iii) the code was "required to be kept or maintained by law" — a disjunctive test, either limb sufficing (Syed Asifuddin). The payroll module's source code is plainly "computer source code" within the Explanation — "the listing of programmes, computer commands, design and layout and programme analysis of computer resource in any form". Punishment: up to 3 years and/or fine up to ₹2 lakh. Cognizable, bailable.
  • s.66 — computer‑related offences. The alteration of the payroll database is an act referred to in s.43(i) — "destroys, deletes or alters any information residing in a computer resource or diminishes its value or utility or affects it injuriously by any means" — and it was done dishonestly (wrongful gain to the employee, wrongful loss to the employer) within IPC s.24 / BNS s.2(7). Punishment: up to 3 years and/or fine up to ₹5 lakh. Cognizable, bailable.
  • BNS s.318 (formerly IPC ss.415/420, cheating) and BNS ss.336/338 (forgery) run in parallel. In Sanjay Kumar v. State of Haryana the accused was convicted under ss.420, 467, 468, 471 IPC and ss.65 and 66 IT Act on the same facts — the precedent for this combination.
  • Note what does NOT apply: s.72 requires unauthorised disclosure to another person. Where the employee merely alters the code and redirects the money without disclosing information to a third party, s.72 is not attracted — the precise distinction drawn in Sanjay Kumar, where the accused was acquitted of s.72 while convicted of ss.65 and 66.

Step 2 — Employee 2: the credential thief. Three provisions apply.

  • s.66C — identity theft. Whoever "fraudulently or dishonestly makes use of the electronic signature, password or any other unique identification feature of any other person" is punishable with up to 3 years and fine up to ₹1 lakh. The stolen credentials are precisely the "password or any other unique identification feature" the section names. The offence is complete on the use — no further act is required.
  • s.66D — cheating by personation by using a computer resource. Whoever, "by means of any communication device or computer resource, cheats by personation" is punishable with up to 3 years and fine up to ₹1 lakh. Sending fraudulent e‑mails in a colleague's or superior's name, so as to induce recipients to act, is cheating by personation through a computer resource. Read with IPC s.416 / BNS s.330 (personation) and IPC s.419 / BNS s.331.
  • s.66 — where the fraudulent e‑mails were sent by accessing a system without permission, that is a s.43(a) act done dishonestly. And s.66C is engaged independently of s.66: s.66C does not require any s.43 act at all.
  • s.72A — if the employee, while providing services under the terms of a lawful contract (his employment contract and any confidentiality covenant), secured access to material containing personal information about another person and disclosed it with intent to cause, or knowing that he was likely to cause, wrongful loss or wrongful gain, he is punishable with up to 3 years and/or fine up to ₹5 lakh. This is engaged where confidential client data is disclosed.
  • BNS s.318 (cheating) and, where the e‑mails induced the transfer of money, the money‑laundering provisions of the PMLA, 2002, run in parallel.

Step 3 — The employer's own exposure. A complete answer should note that the employer may itself face civil liability under s.43A if it failed to implement reasonable security practices and procedures — for example, by failing to deactivate a departing employee's credentials. Cross‑refers to Unit III §5 and the Nov–Dec 2025 Q.2 s.43A problem question.

Conclusion: Employee 1 is liable under s.65 (source‑code tampering) and s.66 (dishonest alteration of information residing in a computer resource), plus BNS cheating and forgery — but not s.72, absent disclosure. Employee 2 is liable under s.66C (identity theft), s.66D (cheating by personation), s.66 where access was unauthorised, and s.72A where confidential personal information was disclosed in breach of contract.
PYQ 3 "Mr. A makes a website with obscene content about teachers/students after suspension. Decide obscenity and applicable IT Act provisions." Nov–Dec 2024, Q.2 · 10–12 marks · Problem question
Model answer

Step 1 — The obscenity analysis. Apply Aveek Sarkar. The material on the website must be judged as a whole, in the context in which it appears, by the standard of an average person applying contemporary community standards. The Hicklin test — isolated passages judged by their tendency to deprave and corrupt the most susceptible reader — is no longer the correct test. Nudity per se is not obscenity; the material must have a tendency of "exciting lustful thoughts". A grievance‑driven website criticising teachers and students is unlikely to satisfy that test unless the content is sexually explicit.

Step 2 — The applicable IT Act provisions, in order of gravity.

  • s.67 — if the material is obscene (lascivious, or appealing to the prurient interest, or tending to deprave and corrupt). Up to 3 years and fine up to ₹5 lakh on first conviction.
  • s.67A — if the material contains a sexually explicit act or conduct, a higher and more objective threshold than obscenity. Up to 5 years and fine up to ₹10 lakh on first conviction; cognizable, non‑bailable.
  • s.66E — if the website captures, publishes or transmits the image of a private area of any person without consent — e.g. morphed images, or images taken from a private source. "Private area" is defined in the Explanation as the naked or undergarment clad genitals, pubic area, buttocks or anus, or in the case of a female her exposed nipples or naked or undergarment clad breasts. Up to 3 years and/or fine up to ₹2 lakh. No publication requirement for the capturing limb.
  • s.67B — if any student depicted is a minor. This is the critical branch, given that the website concerns students. Section 67B is cognizable and non‑bailable, punishable on first conviction with up to 5 years and fine up to ₹10 lakh, and it expressly covers creating, collecting, seeking, browsing, downloading, advertising, promoting, exchanging or distributing material depicting children in a sexually explicit act. s.15 of the POCSO Act, 2012 runs in parallel.
  • IPC ss.292, 294 / BNS ss.294, 296 — the general obscenity provisions, charged alongside.
  • IPC s.509 / BNS s.79 — where the content insults the modesty of female students or teachers.
  • IPC ss.499/500 / BNS s.356 — defamation, where the content makes imputations harming the reputation of identifiable teachers or students. Cross‑refers to Unit III §13 and M.F. Husain.

Step 3 — The S. Harish prima facie test. Where the prosecution must decide whether a depicted person is a child, the court need only reach a prima facie, subjective satisfaction that an ordinary prudent person would reasonably believe the depiction involves, or appears to involve, a child. This is a lower threshold than formal age‑determination procedures, appropriate because the material's purpose is prosecutorial screening, not final adjudication. Given that the website concerns students, the prosecution should screen every depiction against this test.

Step 4 — The Yogisha Prabhu caution. If the material was directed at specific individuals rather than published to the public, the publication element of ss.67/67A may fail, and the court may convict of the cognate lesser offence — s.66E — which requires no publication. But a website is by its nature a publication to the public, so this concern is unlikely to arise here.

Step 5 — Conclusion. A is liable under s.67 if the content is obscene; under s.67A if it contains a sexually explicit act; under s.66E if it captures, publishes or transmits images of a private area without consent; and under s.67B and s.15 POCSO if any depicted student is a minor. The IPC/BNS charges for obscenity, insult to modesty and defamation run in parallel. The motive — retaliation after suspension — is irrelevant to liability but relevant to sentence.

Conclusion: A is liable under s.67 and/or s.67A depending on whether the content is obscene or sexually explicit; s.66E where private‑area images are involved; and — the critical branch, because the subjects are students — s.67B and s.15 POCSO if any depicted person is a minor, applying the S. Harish prima facie test.
PYQ 4 "Discuss the elements and judicial interpretation of s.65 (tampering with computer source code), using Syed Asifuddin and Sanjay Kumar v. State of Haryana." Likely long question · 15 marks · Doctrinal
Model answer — structure
  1. Introduction. Quote s.65 verbatim and its Explanation. Note that s.65 is the Act's original offence — present in the unamended 2000 Act — and that its object is to protect the intellectual property invested in the computer, going beyond what copyright law provides. Punishment: up to 3 years and/or fine up to ₹2 lakh; cognizable, bailable.
  2. The elements, as laid down in Syed Asifuddin para 21: (i) the act (intentional or knowing concealment, destruction or alteration, or causing another to do so); (ii) the object (computer source code used for a computer, computer programme, computer system or computer network); (iii) the legal requirement (the code is "required to be kept or maintained by law"); and (iv) the mental element (intention or knowledge — contrast s.66's dishonesty or fraudulence).
  3. The disjunctive "or". Quote the key passage from Syed Asifuddin: "The disjunctive word 'or' is used by the Legislature between the phrases 'when the computer source code is required to be kept' and the other phrase 'maintained by law for the time being in force' and, therefore, both the situations are different." Add the Court's caveat that whether a cell phone operator is maintaining computer source code is a matter of evidence.
  4. The definition of computer source code. Quote the Explanation — "the listing of programmes, computer commands, design and layout and programme analysis of computer resource in any form" — and the Court's four limbs. Then the holding that ESN and SID are computer source code.
  5. A cell phone is a computer. Set out the Court's reasoning on the circuit board, the microprocessor and the MTSO, and quote: "it is not possible to accept the submission that a cell phone is not a computer." Cross‑refer to Diebold Systems v. Commissioner for the proposition that the IT Act's enlarged definition does not travel to other statutes.
  6. Copyright overlap. Explain that s.63 of the Copyright Act, 1957 applies to the alteration of another's computer programme, because a computer programme is an original literary work under s.2(o) protected by ss.13 and 14(b), and that s.63B punishes knowing use of an infringing copy. Sections 65 and 63 are cumulative.
  7. Sanjay Kumar — the concurrent application of ss.65 and 66. Set out the facts, the trial court's findings, and the holding that the accused "tampered with the computer source document and ... also altered in the information which resided in the computer resource and by doing so he committed the offences under Sections 65 and 66". Note the s.72 acquittal and the reason for it.
  8. Critical evaluation. PYQ 5 "Critically examine the Supreme Court's holding in Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish on possession‑based CSEAM liability and its implications for intermediary/platform obligations." Likely long question · 15 marks · Critical essay
Model answer — the arguments on both sides
The holding is sound
  • Textual. Section 67B expressly reaches one who "browses, downloads" — conduct that does not require transmission. Section 15 POCSO independently criminalises storage in specified circumstances. The plain words do not require distribution.
  • Purposive. Passive consumption creates and sustains the market for CSEAM; each view is a fresh violation of the depicted child's rights. A transmission‑only reading would leave a large enforcement gap.
  • Practical. In the digital environment, possession is the most readily provable offence; requiring proof of transmission would make prosecution impracticable.
  • Terminological reform is overdue. "Child pornography" wrongly characterises the material as a form of expression; "CSEAM" correctly frames it as evidence of abuse.
  • The relaxed identification threshold is proportionate. Requiring formal age‑determination before a charge sheet would defeat the purpose of prosecutorial screening.
The holding is open to criticism
  • Mens rea. Possession‑based liability risks over‑criminalising inadvertent possession — unsolicited receipt in a group chat, browser cache, auto‑download — where the accused neither sought nor knew of the material.
  • The Court's test does not address this. The "prima facie subjective satisfaction" test concerns identification of the subject matter, not the accused's knowledge or intent.
  • Breadth of s.67B's list. "Seeks", "browses", "advertises" and "promotes" are wide enough to capture a search that returns such material, or a share button pressed without knowledge.
  • Intermediary implications. A platform that caches, mirrors or auto‑generates thumbnails of user‑uploaded CSEAM could be argued to "store" it within s.15 POCSO — a risk that sits uneasily with the s.79 safe harbour.

Implications for intermediary obligations. The decision raises the stakes on s.67C (preservation and retention of information by intermediaries) and on the Intermediary Guidelines. A platform that receives a notification that it is hosting CSEAM must act expeditiously and within 36 hours to remove or disable access, or it risks losing the s.79 safe harbour; and, on the reasoning of S. Harish, its retention of the material may itself be argued to attract liability. The 2026 Amendment Rules — with mandatory labelling and detection obligations for synthetically generated information and tightened takedown timelines — push platforms further toward proactive detection, which raises a further tension with the Shreya Singhal principle that intermediaries must not become de facto private censors adjudicating contested claims without judicial oversight.

Conclusion: S. Harish is doctrinally sound as child‑protection policy and closes a real enforcement gap, but it leaves the mens rea threshold for inadvertent possession unresolved — a question the Court's identification test does not reach, and one that will have to be settled by future litigation or legislation.

S. Harish closed a significant enforcement gap by confirming that mere possession of child sexual exploitation material is independently punishable, adopting more accurate CSEAM terminology in the process. The frontier challenge — only beginning to be addressed through the Intermediary Guidelines (Amendment) Rules, 2026 governing synthetically generated information — is how this offence architecture, built around discrete acts of access, alteration, and publication, will adapt to AI‑generated deepfake content that blurs the line between authorship, distribution, and mere algorithmic generation."
