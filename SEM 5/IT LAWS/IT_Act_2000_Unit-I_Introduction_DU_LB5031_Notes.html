★ The single most important idea in Unit I

The IT Act, 2000 is not a standalone code. It is an adaptation layer grafted onto pre‑digital commercial, evidentiary and criminal law, enabling those regimes to work in the electronic medium through the principle of functional equivalence — electronic records, signatures and communications perform the same legal functions as their paper counterparts. Every long answer you write in Units II–IX should open with this sentence.

1 Information Technology: Meaning, Definition & Scope

“Information Technology (use of computers to store, retrieve, transmit and manipulate data)…” — DU syllabus, Part A, Unit I. Those four verbs are the entire subject‑matter of the IT Act: every section of the Act regulates one or more of them.

1.1 What is Information Technology?

Information Technology (IT) is the use of computers, digital networks and software to store, retrieve, process, transmit and manipulate data — i.e., reducing information into electronic form so that it can be stored, retrieved and distributed rapidly, without the geographic and time constraints of paper‑based systems.

📌 Why this matters for the exam

“IT” is not defined in the IT Act — the Act defines the objects (computer, computer resource, information, electronic record) and the conduct (access, tampering, transmission). So an answer on “meaning of IT” must always (a) give the functional definition, and (b) immediately anchor it to the four verbs and the s.2(1) definitions.

2 Cyberspace: The Notional Environment

“Cyberspace is a notional environment in which communication over computer network occurs; [a] borderless environment” — DU syllabus, Part A, Unit I.

Cyberspace is the conceptual space in which information is stored, processed and transmitted through computer networks. It is notional (a space of data and interaction rather than of geography), borderless (it does not correspond to any territory), and intermediated (it exists only because computers and networks exist). The classic academic statement, recommended in the DU reading list, is Lawrence Lessig, Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace (1999; Code version 2.0, 2006) and S.K. Verma & Raman Mittal (eds.), Legal Dimensions of Cyberspace (2004): in cyberspace, architecture (“code”) itself regulates, alongside law, norms, markets and physical constraints.

⚠ Examiner trap

Do not answer “cyberspace cannot be regulated because it is borderless.” The Delhi High Court in My Space Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. (FAO(OS) 540/2011, DB, 23.12.2016 — in the DU case material) expressly rejected the argument that the intermediary industry is “inherently incapable of being regulated” or that it is “global/borderless” — courts do regulate, while calibrating relief to the practical realities of the medium.

🎓 Short‑note answer in 6 lines

Internet = the physical/digital infrastructure (a network of networks using TCP/IP). Web = one application layer on that infrastructure (HTTP/HTML, browsers, URLs). E‑mail = another application layer (SMTP/IMAP/POP3) for asynchronous message and file exchange. The Web gave us websites, domain names and clickable e‑commerce; e‑mail gave us the first legally significant electronic contracts and remains the most litigated form of electronic evidence. All three generate “information” and “electronic records” which the IT Act, 2000 legally recognises.

4 Uses of IT: Academics, E‑Commerce & Social Networking

The syllabus requires you to know the use of IT — academics, e‑commerce (B2B, B2C, C2C) and social networking by individuals — because each use creates its own legal problem and its own statutory response.

4.1 Academics

  • What: online learning (SWAYAM/NPTEL/MOOCs), digital libraries, e‑research databases, online admission and examination systems, plagiarism detection.
  • Legal issues: copyright in digital courseware and journals (ss.14, 51, 63 Copyright Act, 1957); data protection of student records (s.43A, DPDP Act, 2023); validity of online examination and degree records (ss.4–5 IT Act, and the presumptions in s.85B Evidence Act — corresponding to ss.62–63 BSA); intermediary liability of learning platforms (s.79).
  • Example: a university issues a marksheet as an electronically signed PDF — its legal recognition flows from ss.4–5 IT Act and the presumptions in favour of electronic records under s.85B of the Evidence Act, 1872 (corresponding to ss.62–63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023).

4.3 Social networking by individuals

  • What: user‑generated content platforms — messaging (WhatsApp), micro‑blogging (X), photo/video (Instagram, YouTube), professional networks (LinkedIn), communities (Reddit, Facebook Groups).

5.3 The five challenges in detail

(a) Mobiles

The mobile phone is now the primary computer for most Indians. Two consequences follow. First, the mobile is a “computer” within s.2(1)(i) — held in Syed Asifuddin — and since 2008 also a “communication device” under s.2(1)(ha), which extends offences such as s.66D (cheating by personation using a computer resource or communication device) and mirrors s.354D IPC/s.78 BNS (stalking). Second, mobile‑number control is the new authentication factor: SIM‑swap/duplicate‑SIM frauds generate OTPs for banking frauds — the fact pattern of Sanjay Dhande v. ICICI Bank & Vodafone and State Bank of India v. Chander Kalani, both in the DU case material, where liability was apportioned between the bank and the telecom operator under s.43A.

(b) Cyber security

The attack surface expands as critical infrastructure, financial systems and personal data move online. Threats range from individual account compromise (phishing, credential theft) to nation‑state attacks on power grids and telecommunications: data breaches, ransomware, DDoS, supply‑chain attacks, and AI/ML‑based threats (deepfakes, autonomous malware). India’s response: ss.69 (interception/monitoring/decryption), 69A (blocking), 70 (protected systems), 70A (review committee), 70B (CERT‑In — the national nodal agency), the Information Technology (Interception, Monitoring and Decryption) Rules and the IT (Procedure and Safeguards for Blocking) Rules, 2009, and the CERT‑In Directions, 2022 (reporting of incidents within six hours of knowledge; 180‑day log retention; KYC obligations on VPN/cloud providers). s.66F punishes cyber terrorism with imprisonment up to life.

(c) Cloud computing and data privacy

Organisations store and process data on remote servers (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud), often outside India. Two questions arise: is personal data adequately protected if it transits foreign servers, and can Indian authorities access data stored abroad? India’s specific issue is data localisation versus free flow: the DPDP Act, 2023 adopts a “trusted countries” whitelist rather than hard localisation, while the RBI (payment data) and IRDAI (insurance data) impose sectoral localisation — a fragmented approach. The s.43A liability of body corporates for negligent handling of sensitive personal data survives for pre‑DPDP conduct, and the Puttaswamy three‑fold test (legality, legitimate aim, proportionality — least restrictive means) governs any state intrusion on privacy.

(d) Misuse of social media

Social media enables viral dissemination of misinformation, hate speech, cyberbullying, non‑consensual intimate imagery, doxxing and coordinated harassment. Documented Indian examples: communal violence triggered by rumours on WhatsApp/Facebook; election‑time disinformation; COVID‑19 medical misinformation; and the harassment of women. Response: s.69A blocking, ss.79–81 intermediary liability with the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 (as amended 2022, 2023, 2025 and 2026) — grievance officer, nodal officer, chief compliance officer, 36‑hour takedown, traceability of the first originator, and statutory labelling of synthetically generated information (deepfakes) from 2026.

(e) Cyber crimes

“Cyber crime” is not defined in the IT Act, the IT Amendment Act, 2008, or any other Indian statute — as the DU case material notes, “any offence or crime in which a computer is used is a cyber crime”. The computer may be the target (hacking, virus, DDoS, source‑code tampering), the tool (phishing, identity theft, cheating by personation, cyber stalking, defamation, obscenity, terrorism), or the repository of evidence. Financial frauds (money laundering, credit‑card frauds), social crimes (stalking, pornography, identity theft), IPR‑related crimes, cyber terrorism and defamation are all prescribed for study, mapped to ss.43, 65, 66, 66C, 66D, 66E, 66F, 67, 67A, 67B, 72, 72A and to parallel IPC/BNS provisions.

  • Mobiles: “The handset is both a computer (s.2(1)(i)) and a communication device (s.2(1)(ha)) — so every fraud routed through a phone attracts both IT Act and BNS liability.”
  • Cyber security: “Prevention is institutional (CERT‑In, s.70B), investigation is procedural (s.69, 2009 Rules), and punishment is criminal (ss.65–67B, s.66F).”
  • Cloud & privacy: “s.43A made the body corporate liable for negligence; the DPDP Act, 2023 replaced the penalty‑based model with a rights‑based one, subject to the Puttaswamy proportionality test.”

6 Purpose and Object of the Information Technology Act, 2000

“…to facilitate e‑commerce to remove major hurdles of writing and signature requirement for legal recognition, providing regulatory regime to supervise certifying authorities and digital signature certificates, to create civil and criminal liabilities for contravention of provisions, and consequential amendments in other Acts.”

6.1 The genesis — why India needed the Act

  • Mid‑1990s: globalisation and computerisation accelerated; e‑commerce grew enormously. Until then, most international trade ran on documents transmitted by post and telex; evidence and records were paper‑based.
  • 1996: UNCITRAL adopted the Model Law on Electronic Commerce; in January 1997 the UN General Assembly recommended that all States give it favourable consideration — providing legal recognition to electronic records on the same footing as paper.
  • India’s response: Parliament passed the IT Act, 2000 as Act No. 21 of 2000 — Presidential assent on 9 June 2000, brought into force on 17 October 2000 (GSR 788(E)). It was one of the earliest comprehensive e‑commerce statutes in the world, deliberately enacted to position India as a stable, law‑friendly jurisdiction for digital commerce and IT services.
  • 2008–09: the Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008 (Act 10 of 2009) received assent on 5 February 2009 and came into force on 27 October 2009, because the 2000 Act was criticised as technology‑specific (it hard‑wired the asymmetric cryptosystem/PKI model into s.3), and simultaneously as too draconian and too diluted.
⚖ The preamble (the “why” of the Act)

“An Act to provide legal recognition for transactions carried out by means of electronic data interchange and other means of electronic communication, commonly referred to as ‘electronic commerce’, which involve the use of alternatives to paper‑based methods of communication and storage of information, to facilitate electronic filing of documents with the Government agencies and further to amend the Indian Penal Code, the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, the Bankers’ Books Evidence Act, 1891 and the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto.”

🔎 The academic critique (useful for “critically examine”)

Hemali Shah & Aashish Srivastava, Signature Provisions in the Amended Indian IT Act 2000: Legislative Chaos, 43 Common Law World Review 208 (2014) — a DU‑listed reading — argue that despite the 2008 amendment the Act remains a weak, complex and ambiguous legislation that fails to address many aspects of electronic‑signature usage, particularly when compared with the UNCITRAL Model Laws, the EU Electronic Signatures Directive and other jurisdictions’ statutes. The 2000 Act’s PKI‑exclusive design was technologically premature and had to be supplemented by the technology‑neutral s.3A, producing a dual‑track regime whose CA‑licensing infrastructure (ss.17–42) has not kept pace with Aadhaar e‑Sign and OTP authentication.

Also useful: the original 2000 Act was criticised as draconian by one arm of industry and too diluted/lenient by another, and there were conspicuous omissions — which is why investigators continued to rely on the Indian Penal Code even in technology‑based cases.

⚠ Examiner trap

Never say “the IT Act replaced the IPC/Evidence Act.” The Act amended them (ss.91–94) and runs in parallel with them. Also, never cite s.66A as a live offence — it was struck down in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015).

7.2 Extraterritorial jurisdiction — s.75

⚖ Section 75 — Act to apply to offence or contravention committed outside India

75(1) Subject to the provisions of sub‑section (2), the provisions of this Act shall apply also to any offence or contravention committed outside India by any person irrespective of his nationality.
75(2) For the purposes of sub‑section (1), this Act shall apply to an offence or contravention committed outside India by any person if the act or conduct constituting the offence or contravention involves a computer, computer system or computer network located in India.

Practical significance: this is the statutory basis for prosecuting a hacker sitting abroad who attacks an Indian server. Its limit is enforcement, not jurisdiction — actual cross‑border investigation depends on Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (MLATs), which are slow and often refused; India is not a party to the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime, 2001. Related private‑law jurisdictional rules (Unit IX): s.20(c) CPC (cause of action), ss.177–179, 186, 188, 189 CrPC (now BNSS), s.3 IPC, and the “purposeful availment” test of Banyan Tree Holding v. A. Murali Krishna Reddy (Del HC, DB, 23.11.2009).

7.3 Section 81 — overriding effect and its proviso

⚖ Section 81 — Act to have overriding effect

“The provisions of this Act shall have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith contained in any other law for the time being in force.”

Proviso: “Provided that nothing contained in this Act shall restrict any person from exercising any right conferred under the Copyright Act, 1957 or the Patents Act, 1970.”

  • Main text — the enabling limb: the IT Act does not make unlawful what was previously lawful; it adapts existing law to electronic form. Where the IT Act speaks, it prevails over inconsistent provisions of other laws.
  • Proviso — the IPR carve‑out: the safe harbour in s.79 cannot be used to defeat copyright or patent rights. This is the textual battleground in My Space Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. (Del HC, DB, 23.12.2016), where the Division Bench held that ss.79 and 81 of the IT Act and s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act must be read harmoniously — the proviso to s.81 does not preclude the affirmative defence of safe harbour for an intermediary in copyright actions, but the intermediary must act on specific notice within 36 hours.
  • Exam use: cite s.81 whenever a question asks whether the IT Act “replaces” other law, and always mention the proviso when the question involves copyright, patents or intermediary liability.
🎓 Model short‑note answer — “Overriding effect of the IT Act (s.81)”

s.81(1) gives the IT Act overriding effect: its provisions operate notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other law for the time being in force. This is a consistency provision, not a repeal provision — the Act supplements and adapts pre‑existing commercial, evidentiary and criminal law rather than displacing it, and it does not criminalise what was previously lawful. The proviso, inserted by the 2008 amendment, protects rights under the Copyright Act, 1957 and the Patents Act, 1970, so that the s.79 intermediary safe harbour cannot be read as licensing infringement. In My Space v. Super Cassettes the Delhi High Court harmonised s.79, s.81 and s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act, holding that copyright infringement against an intermediary requires actual knowledge (specific notice with the work and its URL) and that interim relief must be specific — removal within 36 hours of such notice.

8 Key Definitions — Section 2(1) IT Act, 2000

The syllabus prescribes six definitions for Unit I: information 2(1)(v), computer s.2(1)(i), computer network s.2(1)(j), computer resource 2(1)(k), computer system 2(1)(l), communication device 2(1)(ha). Everything else in the Act hangs off them — which is why both prescribed cases are, at bottom, cases about the meaning of “computer”.

Fig. 8 — How the four “computer” definitions interlock. Note that a modern smartphone sits in all four boxes at once.
s.2(1)(v)

“Information”

“information” includes data, message, text, images, sound, voice, codes, computer programmes, software and databases or micro film or computer generated micro fiche.
Note: “data, message, text” were inserted by the IT Amendment Act, 2008. The word “includes” makes the definition inclusive and expansive, not exhaustive.

Significance: the broadest term in the Act. An SMS, a WhatsApp text, a voice note, a photograph, a video file, a source‑code file, a database dump — all are “information”. It is the content that the Act protects (e.g. s.66E, ss.67–67B, s.72A).

Contrast: “data” (s.2(1)(o)) is a representation of information/knowledge/facts/concepts/instructions prepared for processing; “electronic record” (s.2(1)(t)) is data, record, image or sound stored/received/sent in electronic form — the thing the Act legally recognises.
s.2(1)(i)

“Computer”

“computer” means any electronic, magnetic, optical or other high‑speed data processing device or system which performs logical, arithmetic and memory functions by manipulations of electronic, magnetic or optical impulses, and includes all input, output, processing, storage, computer software or communication facilities which are connected or related to the computer in a computer system or computer network.
Three cumulative elements: (1) an electronic/magnetic/optical/other high‑speed data‑processing device or system; (2) performing logical, arithmetic and memory functions; (3) by manipulation of electronic, magnetic or optical impulses.

Inclusive tail: peripherals and connected communication facilities are swept in — so a keyboard, monitor, server or a network it belongs to are part of the “computer”.

Functional, not brand‑based: the definition does not ask “is this a PC?” It asks “what does the device do?” That is why the Andhra Pradesh High Court in Syed Asifuddin held a cell phone is a computer, and why a washing machine, router or switch can, on their functions, be brought within it.

Limit: Diebold Systems — the enlarged definition cannot be transplanted into a fiscal statute; an ATM is not a “computer” for a sales‑tax entry.
s.2(1)(j)

“Computer network”

“computer network” means the inter‑connection of one or more computers or computer systems or communication devices through — (i) the use of satellite, microwave, terrestrial line, wire, wireless or other communication media; and (ii) terminals or a complex consisting of two or more inter‑connected computers or communication devices whether or not the inter‑connection is continuously maintained.
Two limbs: (i) the media of interconnection (satellite, microwave, terrestrial line, wire, wireless); (ii) the configuration (terminals or a complex of two or more interconnected devices).

Key points: (a) “communication device” and “wire, wireless” were inserted in 2008 — the definition is now technology‑neutral; (b) continuity is not required — a temporary or intermittent connection still creates a network; (c) this is the core enabling technology of cyberspace.

Examples: the internet; a bank’s ATM network; a mobile telecom network; a corporate LAN/WAN; a WhatsApp server‑client link.

Exam use: cite for jurisdiction (s.75(2) — “a computer, computer system or computer network located in India”) and for s.65 (source code “used for a computer, computer programme, computer system or computer network”).
s.2(1)(k)

“Computer resource”

“computer resource” means computer, computer system, computer network, data, computer database or software.
The umbrella term. It covers hardware (computer, computer system, computer network) and the intangible (data, computer database, software).

Why it matters: the central civil offence (s.43), the central criminal offence (s.66), the receiving offence (s.66B), the confiscation power (s.76) and the data‑protection provision (s.43A) are all framed around “computer resource”. If a device or dataset falls inside this definition, the entire liability architecture can attach to it.

Example: A cloud account containing a customer database = computer system + computer database + data + software → a “computer resource”.
s.2(1)(l)

“Computer system”

“computer system” means a device or collection of devices, including input and output support devices and excluding calculators which are not programmable and capable of being used in conjunction with external files which contain computer programmes, electronic instructions, input data and output data that performs logic, arithmetic, data storage and retrieval, communication control and other functions.
Two points examiners look for:
1. It is broader than “computer” — it can be a single device or a collection of devices working together (e.g. a server + terminals + printers).
2. It contains an express exclusion — non‑programmable calculators. Everything else with input/output support and programmable capability is in.

In Syed Asifuddin: the Court read ss.2(1)(i), (j) and (l) together and concluded that “any electronic, magnetic or optical device used for storage of information received through satellite, microwave or other communication media and the devices which are programmable and capable of retrieving any information by manipulations of electronic, magnetic or optical impulses is a computer which can be used as [a] computer system in a computer network.”
s.2(1)(ha)

“Communication device”

“communication device” means cell phones, personal digital assistance or combination of both or any other device used to communicate, send or transmit any text, video, audio or image.
Inserted by the IT Amendment Act, 2008. It takes in cell phones, PDAs and, in practice, smartphones, tablets and Wi‑Fi/cellular devices of the iPad kind.

Consequence: offences drafted around “computer resource or communication device” reach the phone directly — e.g. s.66D (cheating by personation using a computer resource or communication device) and the cyber‑stalking provisions (s.354D IPC / s.78 BNS).

Historical note: before 2008, Syed Asifuddin had to characterise the handset as a computer under s.2(1)(i) to bring it within s.65. Post‑2008, the handset is independently a communication device — the legislature caught up with the court.

★ How to answer any “meaning of computer” question

Quote s.2(1)(i) verbatim → break it into its three functional elements → explain the inclusive tail → then say “therefore the definition is functional and expansive” → apply it with Syed Asifuddin (cell phone = computer) → limit it with Diebold (definition is context‑bound; cannot be imported into a fiscal statute) → close with the 2008 addition of s.2(1)(ha). That structure alone secures full marks.

⚖ A note on how to use the two cases together

Syed Asifuddin is the expansive authority: a purposive, IT‑Act‑specific reading of “computer” that brings a cell phone within s.2(1)(i). Prescribed Case 1 · Part A, Unit I

Syed Asifuddin v. The State of Andhra Pradesh

2006 (1) ALD (Cri) 96 2005 CriLJ 4314 High Court of Andhra Pradesh Decided 29 July 2005 s.482 CrPC petition

1 Procedural posture

Two criminal petitions filed by different persons under s.482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, heard together and disposed of by a common order, seeking the quashing of F.I.R. No. 20 of 2003 registered by the Criminal Investigation Department (C.I.D.) Police, Hyderabad. The F.I.R. was registered under ss.409, 420 and 120B IPC, s.65 of the Information Technology Act, 2000 and s.63 of the Copyright Act, 1957. While admitting the petitions, the Court had stayed investigation; the matters were finally heard on the vacation of that stay.

2 Facts (as recorded by the Court)

  • The scheme: Reliance Infocomm Ltd., Hyderabad launched “Reliance India Mobile” (RIM) under the Dhirubhai Ambani Pioneer Offer, later modified into the “POBF” scheme. A subscriber received a third‑generation digital handset worth ₹10,500 on payment of only ₹3,350, plus a three‑year service bundle at about ₹600 per month, with one year’s warranty and three years’ insurance — on condition of remaining on the Reliance network for a lock‑in period of three years.
  • The market response and the rivalry: The offer produced a phenomenal response in the twin cities. Competing CDMA service providers — principally Tata Indicom (Tata Tele Services Ltd., TTSL, providing basic and Wireless in Local Loop services) — allegedly began “weaning away” RIM subscribers.
  • The modus operandi (from the complaint): Callers in Abids, Begumpet, Koti, Himayatnagar and Malakpet would contact RIM subscribers, claim better tariffs, and invite them to keep a “world class” Reliance‑branded handset while switching. The customer would be asked to wait for an hour while an usher carried the handset to an undisclosed location in Secunderabad for a 45‑minute to 1½‑hour “conversion process”. During that time, the ESN (Electronic Serial Number) of the Reliance instrument was hacked by reprogramming, and the handset was returned with instructions to switch it off and on later in the day and start enjoying the new services.
  • Investigation and raid: On the written complaint of the Head of the Sales and Marketing Wing of Reliance, the senior executive officer of the C.I.D., on the instructions of the Additional Director General of Police, registered Crime No. 20 of 2003 on 31‑5‑2003. Investigation revealed that Reliance handsets were being migrated to the Tata Indicom network. Raids were conducted at the Tata Indicom head office at Khan Lathif Khan Estate, Hyderabad, and at a centre opposite Harihara Kala Bhavan, Secunderabad.
  • Arrests and seizures: One Raj Naren, an officer of Tata Indicom, disclosed that the General Manager (Marketing), Madhavan, and Manager (Marketing), Anil Ambati, were accepting Reliance handsets and re‑programming them. Raj Naren and Shaik Mustaffa were arrested, and two handsets were seized. At the head office, Syed Asifuddin, Patlay Navin Kumar and Khaja/Gareed Nawaj (petitioners in Crl.P. No. 2601 of 2003) and Manoj (petitioner No. 2 in Crl.P. No. 2602 of 2003) were arrested. Two re‑programmed Samsung N191 handsets co‑branded with Reliance (with distinct ESN and serial numbers) were seized along with 63 application forms of persons who had migrated from Reliance to Tata Indicom, with affidavits.
  • Seizure detail: The handset recovered from Raj Naren was a Samsung N191 co‑branded with Reliance bearing ESN No. 3F7AB 832; it had been migrated to Tata Indicom (No. 56376361 allotted by Tata Indicom); its original RIM number was 31086523.
  • Remand report (3‑6‑2003): Reliance offered the handset under the Pioneer Scheme subject to a three‑year network lock‑in, with exit only by surrendering the handset or paying its cost; there was an agreement between Samsung and LG manufacturers and Reliance that models Samsung N191 and LG‑2030 were to be used exclusively by Reliance India Mobile. Tata Indicom staff were tampering with the pre‑programmed CDMA handsets “with all dubious means”, which was “an offence under Section 65, I.T. Act”; further, till the lock‑in was over the handset was joint property of the company, so dealing with it was “a clear case of Breach of Trust”; and a conspiracy had been hatched to hijack Reliance customers, 63 of whom had so far migrated.

3 Issues before the Court

  1. Is a telephone handset a “computer”, “computer system” or part of a “computer network” within ss.2(1)(i), (l) and (j) of the IT Act?
  2. Does re‑programming the ESN amount to altering “computer source code” within the Explanation to s.65?
  3. Is the statutory condition — that the source code be “required to be kept or maintained by law for the time being in force” — satisfied on these allegations?
  4. Does altering the embedded computer programme amount to infringement of copyright under s.63 of the Copyright Act, 1957?
  5. Are the IPC offences under ss.409 (criminal breach of trust), 420 (cheating) and 120B (conspiracy) made out at all?
  6. Can the F.I.R. be quashed under s.482 CrPC while investigation is pending?

4 Arguments of the parties

For the petitioners — Sri C. Padmanabha Reddy, Senior Counsel
  • It is always open to a subscriber to change service providers; a subscriber who leaves TTSL takes his handset to BSNL or Reliance to get service connected. CDMA handsets brought to TTSL by subscribers of other providers are capable of accommodating two separate lines and can be activated on principal assignment mobile (NAM 1 or NAM 2). Mere activation of NAM 1 or NAM 2 by TTSL for a handset brought by a subscriber of another provider does not amount to any crime.
  • s.409 IPC is not made out even on the F.I.R. and remand report — there is no entrustment of property to TTSL or its employees, and no dishonest appropriation or conversion to their own use, so criminal breach of trust does not arise.
  • s.420 IPC is not made out — there is no allegation that the petitioners deceived Reliance fraudulently or dishonestly to deliver or retain property; the subscriber was never induced to deliver property.
  • s.120B IPC is misconceived because it is relatable only to the alleged offences under ss.409 and 420.
  • s.65 IT Act: a telephone handset is not a computer nor a computer system containing a computer programme.
  • Alternatively, in the absence of any law in force requiring the maintenance of “computer source code”, the allegation that the petitioners concealed, destroyed or altered computer source code is devoid of substance, and therefore the offence of hacking is absent.
  • Copyright: there is no allegation that Reliance has copyright in the source code of the computer programme in the handsets it supplied, so infringement does not arise.
  • TTSL already had a subscriber base of one lakh customers in Andhra Pradesh, so there was no necessity to woo the respondent’s customers.
For the prosecution / respondent — Sri H. Prahlad Reddy, Addl. Public Prosecutor & Sri D. Seshadri Naidu, counsel for the respondent
  • Where a cognizable offence under various provisions of different statutes has been registered and investigation is pending, the High Court cannot quash the F.I.R. at the stage of investigation.
  • After conducting appropriate preliminary investigation and examining witnesses, the police concluded that the petitioners committed offences involving highly technical aspects; unless and until proper evidence is let in before the criminal court, a crime cannot be quashed on the mere assertions of the accused.
  • The cell phone handsets with CDMA technology supplied by Reliance to its subscribers are dedicated to Reliance; by interfering with the computer programme and converting the handsets to be responsive to the technology adopted by TTSL, the petitioners committed what is itself an offence.

5 Reasoning of the Court

(a) On the IPC charges — accepted the petitioners’ submission

The Court held that even if the allegations in the F.I.R. were taken as true, no offence under ss.409, 420 or 120B IPC was made out. Under s.405 IPC, criminal breach of trust requires (i) entrustment with property and (ii) dishonest use or disposal of that property by the person to whom it is entrusted — and both were absent: there was no allegation that property in respect of which Reliance had rights was entrusted to TTSL or its employees. The subscriber was given the instrument with an exclusive right to use it; if the accused induced a subscriber to opt for TTSL’s services, that was not criminal breach of trust. Likewise, cheating under s.415 IPC was not made out because the subscriber was never induced to deliver property to TTSL, nor was there dishonest or fraudulent inducement — “indeed the delivery of the property as such is not present in the case.” s.120B failed with the substantive offences. A trial on these charges would result in a miscarriage of justice; relying on State of West Bengal v. Swapan Kumar, State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal, 1992 Cri LJ 527 (SC), R.P. Kapoor v. State of Punjab and State of Tamil Nadu v. Thirukkural Permal, the Court held that where the F.I.R. does not disclose the commission of a cognizable offence, the police have no power to investigate it.

(b) On “computer” — the technological excursion

The Court quoted the definitions in ss.2(1)(i) (computer), (l) (computer system) and (j) (computer network) and held that “any electronic, magnetic or optical device used for storage of information received through satellite, microwave or other communication media and the devices which are programmable and capable of retrieving any information by manipulations of electronic, magnetic or optical impulses is a computer which can be used as [a] computer system in a computer network.”

It then explained that a computer must be instructed through a series of 0s and 1s in different permutations and combinations — machine language, expressed in a computer language; the instructions, known in computer parlance as “source code”, are not seen by users: “A source code is thus a programme as written by the programmer. Every computer functions as a separate programme and thus a separate source code.” Citing Diane Rowland and Elizabeth MacDonald, Information Technology Law, Dundish Publishing Ltd. (1997), p. 17, the Court noted that source code is a series of statements written in a human‑readable programming language, is closely guarded by companies, and that the law is developing towards recognising copyright in source code — “If source code is copied, it would certainly violate copyright of developer.”

The Court then traced the technology: Alexander Graham Bell’s telephone (1876); radio communication invented by Nikolai Tesla (1880) and presented by Guglielmo Marconi (1894); the radio telephone; the division of a city into cells, each user receiving frequencies, enabling crores of simultaneous communications — hence “cell mobile phone”. It explained the MTSO (Mobile Telephone Switching Office), which automatically transfers signals from tower to tower as the user moves between cells, and the three identifiers:

  • SID (System Identification Code) — a unique 5‑digit number assigned to each carrier by the licensor;
  • ESN (Electronic Serial Number) — a unique 32‑bit number programmed into the phone when it is manufactured;
  • MIN (Mobile Identification Number) — a 10‑digit number derived from the cell phone number given to a subscriber.

On switching on, the phone listens for a SID on the control channel and compares it with the SID programmed into the phone; if they match, the phone knows it is communicating with its home system. The Court held that every cell phone contains a circuit board — the brain of the phone — a combination of several computer chips programmed to convert analog to digital and digital to analog, a microprocessor similar to that used in a desktop computer’s compact disk. “Without the circuit board, cell phone instrument cannot function. Therefore, it is not possible to accept the submission that a cell phone is not a computer.” Even by the very definitions of computer and computer network in the IT Act, “a cell phone is a computer which is programmed to do among others the function of receiving digital audio signals, convert it into analog audio signal and also send analog audio signals in a digital form externally by wireless technology.”

(c) On s.65 — tampering with computer source documents

The Court set out the ingredients of the offence under s.65 as: (i) intentional concealment, destruction or alteration of computer source code used for a computer, computer programme, computer system or computer network; (ii) intentional or knowing causation of another to conceal, destroy or alter such source code; and (iii) the source code being required to be kept or maintained by law for the time being in force. Punishment: imprisonment up to three years or fine up to ₹2,00,000 or both. It quoted the Explanation to s.65: “‘computer source code’ means the listing of programmes, computer commands, design and layout and programme analysis of computer resource in any form.”

Applying it: by the very definition of computer source code, (a) a list of programmes, (b) computer commands, (c) design and layout, and (d) programme analysis of computer resource in any form is “computer source code”. “Going by the definition, ESN of Samsung N191 model cell phone handset or ESN of LG‑2030 model cell phone handset exclusively used by the second respondent as well as SID of second respondent come within the definition of computer source code.” Every cell phone operator must obtain its SID from the licensor (the Government of India); ESN is a permanent part of the phone, whereas MIN and SID are programmed into it when a service plan is purchased and the phone activated. If someone manipulates and alters the ESN, the exclusive Samsung/LG handsets become usable by other providers. “Therefore, prima facie, when the ESN is altered, the offence under Section 65 of I.T. Act is attracted.”

On the “required to be kept or maintained by law” objection, the Court rejected it as devoid of merit: the legislature used the disjunctive word “or” between “when the computer source code is required to be kept” and “maintained by law for the time being in force”, so both situations are different and either suffices. Whether a cell phone operator is in fact maintaining computer source code is a matter of evidence; going by the allegations in the complaint, the second respondent was in fact maintaining the computer source code. If there is an allegation against any person, an offence under s.65 is made out, and the crime cannot be quashed on that count.

(d) On s.63 of the Copyright Act

Parliament amended the Copyright Act in 1995 to bring computer programmes within its fold as literary works. The Court quoted s.2(ffb) (“computer” includes any electronic or similar device having information processing capabilities), s.2(ffc) (“computer programme” means a set of instructions expressed in words, codes, schemes or in any other form, including a machine readable medium, capable of causing a computer to perform a particular task or achieve a particular result) and s.2(o) (“literary work” includes computer programmes, tables and compilations including computer databases), together with ss.13, 14 and 14(b). Reading them together, “a computer programme is by very definition original literary work and, therefore, the law protects such copyright”, and under s.63 any infringement of copyright in a computer programme or source code is punishable. “Therefore, prima facie, if a person alters computer programme of another person or another computer company, the same would be infringement of the copyright” — subject to the evidence led at trial, which the trial court must deal with.

Decision (operative part)
  1. Crime No. 20 of 2003 is QUASHED insofar as it relates to ss.409, 420 and 120B of the Indian Penal Code.
  2. The criminal petitions are DISMISSED insofar as the crimes under s.65 of the IT Act, 2000 and s.63 of the Copyright Act, 1957 are concerned.
  3. The C.I.D. Police is directed to complete the investigation and file a final report before the Metropolitan Magistrate competent to take cognizance, within three months from receipt of the order.

6 Principles evolved (the ratio)

Ratio decidendi
  1. A cell phone is a “computer” within s.2(1)(i) of the IT Act. The definition is functional: any electronic, magnetic or optical device that stores information received through satellite, microwave or other communication media, is programmable, and retrieves information by manipulation of electronic, magnetic or optical impulses, is a computer usable as a computer system in a computer network. Brand, form factor and ordinary‑language labels are irrelevant.
  2. The ESN (and SID) programmed into a handset fall within “computer source code” as defined in the Explanation to s.65 — the listing of programmes, computer commands, design and layout, and programme analysis of computer resource in any form.
  3. The “required to be kept or maintained by law” condition is disjunctive. Because the legislature used “or” between “required to be kept” and “maintained by law for the time being in force”, either situation satisfies the condition; and whether the operator in fact maintains the source code is a question of evidence, not of pleading.
  4. Criminal breach of trust requires entrustment plus dishonest misappropriation (s.405 IPC); where the subscriber, not the accused, holds the handset and was never induced to deliver property, ss.409 and 420 fail — and s.120B falls with them.
  5. Quashing under s.482 CrPC is justified where the F.I.R. does not disclose a cognizable offence and a trial would be a miscarriage of justice; but quashing is not justified where the allegations, taken at face value, disclose a technical offence requiring evidence to be led.
  6. Parallel liability: the same conduct can simultaneously attract the IT Act (civil and criminal), the Copyright Act and the IPC — the statutes are cumulative, not alternative.

7 Significance and exam use

  • Foundational authority for an expansive, purposive reading of “computer” — cited in virtually every subsequent Indian decision on the scope of computer‑related offences, and the reason that tampering with a handset’s programming is prosecutable at all.
  • It is the principal authority for s.65 (tampering with computer source documents) in the DU syllabus, and the immediate precursor of s.2(1)(ha) (“communication device”), inserted in 2008.
  • It demonstrates the s.43 → s.66 bridge: the Court noted that if alteration of the ESN amounts to hacking with a computer system, it is always open to the police to alter the F.I.R. or to the criminal court to frame a charge specifically under s.66.
  • Companion case: Sanjay Kumar v. State of Haryana (P&H HC, 10.01.2013) applies the same principle to a bank’s software and upholds conviction under ss.65 and 66 — see §9.3 below.
  • Contrast: Diebold Systems — see §9.2.
⚠ Common mistake in answers

Do not write that the Court held the petitioners guilty. It held only that a prima facie case under s.65 and s.63 Copyright Act was made out, so the F.I.R. could not be quashed on those counts; the IPC counts were quashed and the investigation was directed to be completed. Precision on the relief granted is what separates a first‑class answer.

Prescribed Case 2 · Part A, Unit I

Diebold Systems Pvt. Ltd. v. The Commissioner

ILR 2005 KAR 2210 2006 (144) STC 59 (Kar) High Court of Karnataka Decided 31 January 2005 H.L. Dattu & H.N. Nagamohan Das, JJ.

1 Nature of the proceeding

An appeal before the High Court of Karnataka against the order of the Commissioner of Commercial Taxes, who had, in suo motu revisional proceedings under s.22‑A(1) of the Karnataka Sales Tax Act, 1957 (KST Act), revised the classification of Automated Teller Machines (ATMs) and subjected their sale to a higher rate of tax. The appeal turned entirely on a question of statutory classification: is an ATM a “computer” / “computer terminal”, or an “electronic good”?

2 Facts

  • The appellant, Diebold Systems Pvt. Ltd., is a public limited company engaged in the manufacture and supply of ATMs.
  • To ascertain the applicable rate of tax, the company sought a clarification from the Authority for Advance Rulings under s.4 of the KST Act.
  • The Advance Ruling Authority, by majority, held that ATMs are “computer terminals” falling under Entry 20(ii)(b) of Part ‘C’ of the Second Schedule, attracting a basic tax of 4%. The Chairman dissented, opining that ATMs fit the description of electronic goods, parts and accessories thereof under Entry 4 of Part ‘E’, attracting 12%.
  • The Commissioner of Commercial Taxes took the view that the Advance Ruling Authority had erroneously classified ATMs as computer terminals at 4% instead of electronic goods at 12%, thereby causing prejudice to the revenue, and initiated suo motu revisional proceedings under s.22‑A(1) of the KST Act, issuing a show‑cause notice dated 2.9.2003 proposing the higher classification.
  • The revisional authority’s findings were: (i) ATMs are not “computers of all kinds” because they are not mentioned in any of the sub‑entries of Entry 20 of Part ‘C’; and (ii) entries in a taxing statute must be construed not in their scientific or technical sense but as understood in common parlance or popular sense. It also observed that, as stated by the dealer himself, an ATM consists of a computer (motherboard with processor), computer peripherals such as RAM, drives, keyboard, monitor, mouse, and also software — but that “in common parlance or popular sense, ATM is a Teller Machine … which is automated with the aid of computer, computer peripherals, software and other devices.”
  • The company filed a detailed reply defending the advance ruling, but nowhere conceded that an ATM works on the principles of electronics and is commonly understood to be electronic goods.

3 Issues

  1. Is an ATM a “computer” (so as to fall within Entry 20(i) of Part ‘C’ of the Second Schedule to the KST Act), or a “computer terminal” (Entry 20(ii)(b)), or an “electronic good” (Entry 4 of Part ‘E’)?
  2. Can the enlarged definition of “computer” in s.2(1)(i) of the IT Act, 2000 be imported to interpret an Entry in a fiscal statute such as the KST Act?
  3. Was the Commissioner justified in exercising revisional powers under s.22‑A of the KST Act?

4 Arguments of the parties

For the appellant — Sri K.P. Kumar, learned Senior Counsel
  • Relying on the definition of “computer” in the IT Act, 2000, an ATM is nothing but a computer: it is a combination of a computer, it runs on a processor, and the purpose for which it is put to use is to dispense cash.
  • It therefore fits the description of “computers of all kinds” in Entry 20(i) of Part ‘C’ of the Second Schedule — particularly given the words “namely” and “and the like” — attracting 4% tax; alternatively it is a “computer terminal” under Entry 20(ii)(b).
  • Relying on Computers, Concepts and Applications for Users by Robert C. Nikenson: an ATM cannot function without a computer; the computer is a multiple‑user computer because different people use it through many ATMs at one time; when you use an ATM, you are using the computer. Hence the “soul” of an ATM is the computer.
  • If it is not a computer, it must at least be a computer terminal, since the Advance Ruling Authority so classified it by majority.
  • The revisional authority should not interfere with an advance ruling unless it is both erroneous and prejudicial to the revenue, particularly where two views are possible — and the passing observations in the revisional order cannot be said to show total non‑application of mind.
For the respondent — Sri Anand, learned Government Advocate
  • ATMs are electronic goods; although operated with the assistance of computer technology, in common parlance they cannot be construed as computers or computer terminals.
  • Entry 20(i) of Part ‘C’ uses the word “namely”, which imports an exhaustive list — “computers of all kinds namely, main frame, mini, personal, micro computers and the like and their parts” — and ATMs are not of that kind. Similarly, the peripherals in Entry 20(ii)(b) are defined with the words “that is to say”, again exhaustive, and ATMs are not included.
  • The Government Advocate produced voluminous literature on computers to demonstrate that ATMs by no stretch of imagination could be construed as computers or computer terminals, and that the Advance Ruling Authority was not justified in classifying them as such.
  • Entry 4 of Part ‘E’ covers “electronic goods, parts and accessories thereof other than those falling under any other Entry”, so ATMs are liable to tax at 12%.
  • The revisional authority was justified in concluding that ATMs are electronic goods, and the order dated 1.10.2002 (and the notice dated 6/2002‑03 of the same date) should not be set aside.

5 Reasoning of the Court

(a) The IT Act definition is wide — but only for the IT Act. The Court began by acknowledging that “to suit the purpose and object of the IT Act, Parliament has defined the expression ‘computer’ by giving it a very wide meaning”; and that by using the word “means” immediately after “computer”, the legislature made the definition exhaustive, so that no other meaning can be assigned to the expression than what is included in the definition. But the Court refused to carry that enlarged, IT‑Act‑specific meaning into a taxing statute: the wide definition of “computer” in the IT Act, 2000 is inapplicable while interpreting an Entry under fiscal legislation such as the Karnataka Sales Tax Act, 1957.

(b) Fiscal entries are read in common parlance. “The entries in a Taxing Statute require to be construed not in their scientific or technical sense, but as understood in common parlance or popular sense.” The Court relied on standard reference works — including the Columbia Encyclopedia (6th edn.) on computer terminals and Computers, Concepts and Applications for Users — for the proposition that an ATM is not a computer by itself: it is connected to a computer that performs the tasks requested by the person using the ATM, and that computer is connected electronically to many ATMs which may be located at some distance from it.

(c) The Entry structure. Entry 20(i) of Part ‘C’ enumerates “computers of all kinds namely, main frame, mini, personal, micro computers and the like and their parts” — the word “namely” imports an exhaustive list, limiting the entry to computers akin to those specified. Entry 20(ii)(b) defines peripherals with the words “that is to say”, again exhaustive, and ATMs are not specifically included. ATMs therefore fall outside Part ‘C’ altogether and within Entry 4 of Part ‘E’ — “electronic goods, parts and accessories thereof other than those falling under any other Entry” — at 12%.

(d) Characterisation of the ATM. “An Automatic Teller Machine, in our view, is an electronic device, which allows a bank’s customer to make cash withdrawals, and check their account balances at any time without the need of human teller, probably that most widely used means of ‘electronic funds transfer’… In common parlance, it is understood as electronic device and therefore, the revisional authority is justified in holding that ATM’s are electronic goods and the levy of tax on the sale of ATM’s requires to be made under Entry 4 of Part ‘E’ of Second Schedule to the Act.”

Decision
  1. The appeal is dismissed.
  2. An ATM is not a “computer” by itself — it is connected to a computer which performs the tasks requested by the user — and it is not a “computer terminal” within Entry 20(ii)(b) of Part ‘C’ of the Second Schedule to the KST Act, 1957.
  3. ATMs are “electronic goods” falling under Entry 4 of Part ‘E’ of the Second Schedule, liable to a basic tax of 12%; the Commissioner’s revisional order under s.22‑A is upheld.

6 Principles evolved (the ratio)

Ratio decidendi
  1. Statutory definitions are purposive and context‑bound. A definition enacted “to suit the purpose and object” of one statute — here, the IT Act’s very wide meaning of “computer” — cannot be transplanted to interpret an Entry under a different, unrelated statute, particularly fiscal legislation.
  2. “Means” makes a definition exhaustive within that statute, but its exhaustive character does not travel across statutes. The very width that makes s.2(1)(i) effective for cyber offences is why it cannot be used to re‑classify goods for tax.
  3. Entries in taxing statutes are construed in their common‑parlance or popular sense, not in a scientific or technical sense, and words such as “namely” and “that is to say” introduce exhaustive lists.
  4. A device connected to a computer is not itself necessarily a “computer.” The ATM is an electronic device whose intelligence resides in a remote computer that serves many terminals.
  5. Revisional powers of the Commissioner under s.22‑A may be exercised where the subordinate authority’s classification is erroneous and prejudicial to the revenue.

7 Significance and exam use

  • It is the limiting principle on functional equivalence: IT Act definitions are not automatically read into unrelated legislation.
  • It is the standard contrast case to Syed Asifuddin — the examiner’s favourite device. Asifuddin reads the definition expansively for the purpose of the IT Act; Diebold refuses to read it expansively for the purpose of a tax entry. Both are correct, because both apply the same rule: definitions serve the purpose of the statute in which they appear.
  • Critical view (worth a sentence for “critically examine”): commentators have argued that the reasoning is not flawless — an ATM contains a motherboard with a processor, RAM, drives, keyboard, monitor, mouse and software, and cannot function without a connected computer, so it could equally have been treated as a computer or at least a computer terminal. The decision nonetheless stands for the primacy of common parlance in fiscal entries.
🎓 Model short‑note answer — “Is an ATM a computer?”

In Diebold Systems Pvt. Ltd. v. The Commissioner, ILR 2005 KAR 2210, the Karnataka High Court held that an ATM is not a computer by itself and not a “computer terminal” for the purposes of the Karnataka Sales Tax Act, 1957. Although the IT Act, 2000 defines “computer” very widely — and exhaustively, through the word “means” — that enlarged definition was framed to suit the purpose and object of the IT Act and cannot be used to interpret an Entry under fiscal legislation. Entries in a taxing statute are construed in their common‑parlance sense, not in a technical sense; and since an ATM is merely an electronic device connected to a remote computer that actually performs the requested tasks, it falls within “electronic goods” (Entry 4 of Part ‘E’) at 12% rather than “computers/computer terminals” (Entry 20 of Part ‘C’) at 4%. The principle is that statutory definitions are context‑bound and purposive — the very rule that, applied in Syed Asifuddin, produced the opposite result for the cell phone under the IT Act itself.

Companion case (next prescribed authority for s.65 in the DU case material)

Sanjay Kumar v. State of Haryana

Punjab & Haryana High Court Decided 10 January 2013 CRM No. 1353 of 2013; CRR No. 66 of 2013 (O&M) Paramjeet Singh, J.

1 Facts

The petitioner was deputed by M/s Virmati Software and Telecommunication Ltd. to maintain the software system supplied to Vijay Bank, NIT, Faridabad, and also looked after the software systems of certain other banks. In that capacity he had access to the banks’ computerised accounting systems and could enter into ledgers and other accounts. While reconciling accounts, discrepancies were discovered: the petitioner, who held SB Account No. 21499 in his own name in the bank, had manipulated entries by forging and fabricating certain entries from one account to another in the computer system by handling the software, and withdrew amounts on various dates by issuing cheques in his own favour and through cash counter and transfer/clearing transactions. Enquiry revealed that he had wrongfully withdrawn ₹17,67,409/‑, causing wrongful loss to the bank and wrongful gain to himself. Confronted, he gave evasive replies and admitted embezzling only ₹17 lakhs. F.I.R. No. 165 dated 11.02.2003 was registered under ss.406, 420, 467, 468, 469, 471 IPC and ss.65, 66, 72 of the IT Act. After trial, the Judicial Magistrate First Class, Faridabad convicted and sentenced him on 01.09.2011 (2 years each under ss.420, 468, 471 IPC, 3 years under s.467 IPC, and 2 years each under ss.65 and 66 IT Act, with fines); the Sessions Judge, Faridabad dismissed his appeal on 21.08.2012.

2 Issue and argument

In revision, the petitioner contended that he had been falsely implicated — the complainant and eyewitness were inimical, having had a 15–16 year old dispute — and that there was no direct evidence connecting him to the offence, so no prima facie case was made out.

3 Decision and principle

The High Court dismissed the revision and upheld the conviction. From the judgments of both courts below it transpired that the petitioner had manipulated the computerised bank account, i.e., the interest entries, and thereby cheated the bank by forging an electronic record, causing wrongful loss to the bank and wrongful gain to himself of ₹17,67,409/‑; and the documentary evidence on record established the forgery and the withdrawals. The revision was heard on the merits and rejected; delay in filing was condoned and the connected criminal miscellaneous application allowed.

Why this case matters for Unit I / s.65
  1. It applies the Syed Asifuddin principle to a banking software system — the “computer” there is the bank’s server and workstations, and the “source code” the accounting software — showing how far the functional definition reaches.
  2. It demonstrates the cumulative operation of the IT Act and the IPC: the same conduct attracted ss.420, 467, 468, 471 IPC and ss.65, 66 IT Act, with sentences to run concurrently.
  3. It confirms that forging an electronic record by manipulating a computer system is both an IT Act offence and the offence of forgery under the IPC (now ss.336/338 BNS).

11.2 Model answers — Unit I short notes (the most likely form in which Unit I is examined)

SHORT NOTE5–7 marks · 100–150 wordsMeaning and scope of Information Technology

Information Technology means the use of computers to store, retrieve, transmit and manipulate data — reducing information into electronic form so that it can be processed and distributed without the geographic and time constraints of paper. The IT Act, 2000 does not define “IT”; instead it defines its objects — “information” (s.2(1)(v): data, message, text, images, sound, voice, codes, computer programmes, software, databases, micro film, computer generated micro fiche), “computer” (s.2(1)(i)), “computer system” (s.2(1)(l)), “computer network” (s.2(1)(j)), “computer resource” (s.2(1)(k)) and “communication device” (s.2(1)(ha)) — and its conduct: “access” (s.2(1)(a)), transmission, and alteration (ss.43, 65, 66). Its principal uses are academics, e‑commerce (B2B, B2C, C2C), social networking, governance, finance, healthcare and critical infrastructure — each of which the Act regulates through functional equivalence.

SHORT NOTE5–7 marksCyberspace — meaning and characteristics

Cyberspace is a notional environment in which communication over computer networks occurs — a borderless, conceptual space in which information is stored, processed and transmitted, unconstrained by geography or jurisdiction. Its characteristics are: borderless (information crosses state boundaries instantaneously), decentralised (no central authority controls the whole network), asynchronous (communication need not be simultaneous), pseudonymous (attribution is difficult) and persistent (published data is extraordinarily difficult to truly delete). The legal difficulty is that traditional law assumes territorial jurisdiction, synchronous dispute resolution and clear attribution — all of which cyberspace subverts. India’s answer lies in s.75 (extraterritorial reach where a computer or network is located in India), ss.11–13 (attribution, acknowledgment, despatch and receipt) and, in private law, the purposeful‑availment test of Banyan Tree Holding v. A. Murali Krishna Reddy. Regulation is also architectural: per Lawrence Lessig, “code” itself regulates cyberspace alongside law, norms and markets.

SHORT NOTE5–7 marksDistinguish the Internet, the World Wide Web and E‑mail

The Internet is the global infrastructure — an interconnected network of computer networks using TCP/IP, descended from ARPANET. The World Wide Web is one application layer running on that infrastructure, using HTTP/HTTPS and HTML, invented by Tim Berners‑Lee at CERN in 1989; it created the domain‑name system and made e‑commerce visually possible. E‑mail is another application layer (SMTP to send; IMAP/POP3 to retrieve) providing asynchronous message and file exchange. The distinction matters legally: the Internet is the “computer network” of s.2(1)(j) and the anchor of s.75 jurisdiction; the Web generated domain names, website jurisdiction and platform liability; e‑mail generated the first legally significant electronic contracts and remains the most litigated form of electronic evidence under s.63 BSA. All three generate “information” and “electronic records” which ss.4–5 IT Act legally recognise.

SHORT NOTE5–7 marksPurpose and object of the Information Technology Act, 2000

Enacted as Act 21 of 2000 (assent 9 June 2000; in force 17 October 2000), the Act was passed to provide legal recognition for transactions carried out by means of electronic data interchange and other means of electronic communication, commonly referred to as “electronic commerce”, involving alternatives to paper‑based methods of communication and storage, to facilitate electronic filing of documents with Government agencies, and to amend the IPC, the Indian Evidence Act 1872, the Bankers’ Books Evidence Act 1891 and the RBI Act 1934. Its five objects are: (1) remove the legal disability of electronic form — ss.4, 5, 10A; (2) facilitate e‑commerce by eliminating the writing‑and‑signature hurdle — ss.6–10A, 11–13; (3) create a regulatory regime for digital signatures and certifying authorities supervised by the Controller — ss.3, 3A, 17–42, 73–74; (4) create civil and criminal liabilities — ss.43, 43A, 46, 65–75; and (5) make consequential amendments to other statutes — ss.91–94. It was substantially amended by the IT (Amendment) Act, 2008 (in force 27 October 2009) to achieve technology neutrality and to add privacy, cyber security and cyber‑crime provisions.

SHORT NOTE5–7 marksApplicability of the IT Act and the First Schedule exclusions (s.1(4))

By s.1(2) the Act extends to the whole of India and, save as otherwise provided, applies also to any offence or contravention committed outside India by any person; s.75 gives it extraterritorial reach where the computer, computer system or computer network involved is located in India. But s.1(4) provides that nothing in the Act applies to documents or transactions specified in the First Schedule: (a) a negotiable instrument other than a cheque (NI Act, s.13); (b) a power of attorney (Powers‑of‑Attorney Act, s.1A); (c) a trust (Indian Trusts Act, s.3); (d) a will or any other testamentary disposition (Succession Act, s.2(h)); (e) any contract for the sale or conveyance of immovable property or any interest therein; and (f) any other class notified by the Central Government. These exclusions are the single most frequently tested scope limitation in the Act and must be stated in every answer on the scope of electronic recognition.

SHORT NOTE5–7 marksOverriding effect of the IT Act (s.81)

s.81(1) provides that the provisions of the Act shall have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith contained in any other law for the time being in force. This is a consistency, not a repeal, provision: the Act adapts pre‑existing law to electronic form and does not make unlawful what was previously lawful. The proviso — inserted by the 2008 amendment — protects any right conferred under the Copyright Act, 1957 or the Patents Act, 1970, so the s.79 intermediary safe harbour cannot be used to license infringement. In My Space Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. (Del HC, DB, 2016) the Court read ss.79 and 81 harmoniously with s.51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act, holding that copyright infringement against an intermediary requires actual knowledge — specific notice identifying the work and its URL — with removal within 36 hours.

SHORT NOTE5–7 marksInterface of information technology and law: current challenges

IT law is a multi‑disciplinary interface spanning contract, tort, IP, criminal, constitutional and administrative law, resting on Art. 19(1)(a) & (g), Art. 21 (privacy — Puttaswamy), Art. 14, and Entries 31 and 97 of List I. The five challenges prescribed are: (1) mobiles — the handset is both a computer (s.2(1)(i)) and a communication device (s.2(1)(ha)), and SIM‑swap frauds generate s.43A apportionment (Sanjay Dhande; Chander Kalani); (2) cyber security — phishing, ransomware, DDoS, supply‑chain and AI threats, answered by ss.69, 69A, 70, 70B and CERT‑In Directions 2022; (3) cloud computing and data privacy — data residency versus free flow, s.43A/SPDI Rules 2011 superseded by the DPDP Act 2023 and Rules 2025; (4) misuse of social media — misinformation, hate speech, NCII and doxxing, answered by ss.69A, 79–81 and the Intermediary Guidelines 2021 (amended 2026 for deepfakes); and (5) cyber crimes — financial frauds, stalking, pornography, identity theft, IPR crimes, cyber terrorism and defamation, under ss.43, 65–67B, 72, 72A read with the BNS 2023.

SHORT NOTE5–7 marksIs an ATM a computer? (Diebold Systems)

In Diebold Systems Pvt. Ltd. v. The Commissioner, ILR 2005 KAR 2210, the Karnataka High Court held that an ATM is not a computer by itself and not a “computer terminal” for the purposes of the Karnataka Sales Tax Act, 1957. Parliament had defined “computer” very widely — and exhaustively, through the word “means” — to suit the purpose and object of the IT Act; but that enlarged definition cannot be used to interpret an Entry under fiscal legislation. Entries in taxing statutes are construed in their common‑parlance sense, not technically; and since an ATM is merely an electronic device connected to a remote computer that performs the requested tasks, it is an “electronic good” under Entry 4 of Part ‘E’ taxable at 12%, not a “computer/computer terminal” under Entry 20 of Part ‘C’ at 4%. Principle: statutory definitions are purposive and context‑bound — the very rule that, applied in Syed Asifuddin, made a cell phone a “computer” under the IT Act.

11.3 Model answers — long questions on Unit I

Q. “Discuss the purpose and object of the Information Technology Act, 2000. Has the Act achieved its objects?” 12–15 marks
Model answer — structure

I. Introduction. Open with the multi‑layered‑framework sentence and the preamble: the IT Act, 2000 (Act 21 of 2000; assent 9 June 2000; in force 17 October 2000) was enacted to provide legal recognition for transactions carried out by electronic data interchange and other electronic communication — “electronic commerce” — involving alternatives to paper‑based methods of communication and storage, to facilitate electronic filing with Government agencies, and to amend the IPC, the Evidence Act 1872, the Bankers’ Books Evidence Act 1891 and the RBI Act 1934. Note the mischief: pre‑2000 commercial, evidentiary and criminal law assumed paper documents and handwritten signatures.

II. The five objects, with the implementing sections (set out in §6.2 above): removal of the legal disability of electronic form (ss.4, 5, 10A); facilitation of e‑commerce (ss.6–10A, 11–13); a regulatory regime for digital signatures and certifying authorities under the Controller of Certifying Authorities (ss.3, 3A, 17–42, 73–74); creation of civil and criminal liabilities (ss.43, 43A, 46, 65–75); and consequential amendments (ss.91–94).

III. The limits deliberately built in. s.1(4) and the First Schedule exclusions; s.81 and its IPR proviso; s.1(2)/s.75 extraterritorial reach and its MLAT‑dependent enforcement.

IV. Has it achieved its objects? Largely yes, with caveats. Successes: (a) e‑commerce and e‑governance are legally routine — ss.4–5 and 10A removed the writing‑signature hurdle, as Trimex International v. Vedanta Aluminium (SC, 2010) confirms for contracts formed by e‑mail exchange; (b) a certification infrastructure exists (ss.17–42), and the 2008 amendment achieved technology neutrality through s.3A and the Second Schedule, accommodating Aadhaar e‑Sign, OTP and biometrics; (c) s.43 created India’s first civil remedy for data theft and ss.65–67B a workable criminal code; (d) courts have applied the definitions purposively — Syed Asifuddin. Shortcomings: the CA hierarchy has not kept pace with practical authentication; the Act remains technology‑heavy and ambiguous in parts (Shah & Srivastava, “Legislative Chaos”, 43 CLWR 208 (2014)); s.66A had to be struck down (Shreya Singhal); data protection had to wait for the DPDP Act, 2023; and cyber‑crime investigation still leans heavily on the IPC/BNS. Conclude: the Act achieved its enabling object and remains the spine of Indian cyber law, but its regulatory object required the 2008 amendment and its privacy object required the DPDP Act 2023.

Q. “Explain the meaning of ‘computer’ under s.2(1)(i) of the IT Act, 2000. Discuss the judicial interpretation with reference to Syed Asifuddin and Diebold Systems.” 12–15 marks
Model answer — structure

I. The text. Quote s.2(1)(i) verbatim and break it into three cumulative elements (electronic/magnetic/optical/other high‑speed data‑processing device or system; performing logical, arithmetic and memory functions; by manipulations of electronic, magnetic or optical impulses), plus the inclusive tail covering connected input, output, processing, storage, software and communication facilities.

II. The character of the definition. Functional, not brand‑ or form‑based; read with s.2(1)(l) (computer system — device or collection of devices, excluding non‑programmable calculators) and s.2(1)(j) (computer network). Therefore a programmable washing machine, a router, a switch, a server, a smart TV or a mobile handset can all be “computers” if they perform the enumerated functions.

III. Syed Asifuddin — the expansive reading. Facts in brief (ESN reprogramming of Reliance‑locked CDMA handsets by Tata Indicom employees; F.I.R. under ss.409, 420, 120B IPC, s.65 IT Act, s.63 Copyright Act). The Court examined the technology — the circuit board, the microprocessor converting analog↔digital signals, the SID/ESN/MIN identifiers, the MTSO — and held that “it is not possible to accept the submission that a cell phone is not a computer.” It further held that ESN and SID fall within “computer source code” under the Explanation to s.65, and that the “required to be kept or maintained by law” condition is disjunctive. The IPC counts were quashed; the s.65 and Copyright counts survived.

IV. Diebold — the limiting principle. Facts (ATM classification for sales tax in Karnataka), the advance ruling, the Chairman’s dissent, the Commissioner’s revision under s.22‑A. Held: the IT Act definition is wide and exhaustive for the IT Act, but cannot be transplanted into an Entry of a fiscal statute; taxing entries are read in common parlance; “namely” and “that is to say” are exhaustive; an ATM is an electronic device connected to a remote computer, so it is an “electronic good” at 12%.

V. Synthesis. The two decisions are consistent because both apply the same rule: a definition serves the purpose of the statute in which it appears. Within the IT Act, “computer” is read expansively and functionally to make the Act’s regulatory objects workable; outside it, the definition is not borrowed. Note the legislative sequel: s.2(1)(ha) (communication device), inserted in 2008, now independently brings cell phones and PDAs within offences such as s.66D — the legislature catching up with the Court.

Q. “Discuss the applicability of the IT Act, 2000, including the First Schedule exclusions, extraterritorial jurisdiction and overriding effect.” 12–15 marks

Follow the decision‑tree structure in Fig. 7: (1) s.1(1)–(2) — name and territorial extent, plus application to offences and contraventions committed outside India by any person; (2) s.1(3) — commencement (17 October 2000); (3) s.1(4) and the First Schedule — enumerate all six exclusions with the source statute for each and explain the practical effect (electronic wills remain fraught; land sale still requires a registered paper memorandum; bills of exchange and promissory notes remain outside, while cheques were brought in by the 2008 amendment); (4) s.75 — extraterritorial application irrespective of nationality, provided the act involves a computer, computer system or computer network located in India, and the enforcement reality of MLATs and India’s non‑accession to the Budapest Convention; (5) s.81 — overriding effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other law, and the proviso preserving Copyright Act 1957 and Patents Act 1970 rights, with My Space v. Super Cassettes on harmonising ss.79, 81 and s.51(a)(ii); (6) conclude with the private‑law jurisdictional overlay — s.20(c) CPC, ss.177–179, 186–189 CrPC/BNSS, and Banyan Tree’s purposeful‑availment test.

Q. “Discuss the elements of s.65 IT Act (tampering with computer source documents) with the help of Syed Asifuddin and Sanjay Kumar.” 12–15 marks

Set out the three elements from Fig. 10: (i) knowing or intentional concealment, destruction or alteration of computer source code used for a computer, computer programme, computer system or computer network (or knowingly causing another to do so); (ii) the statutory condition that the code be required to be kept or maintained by law for the time being in force — disjunctive, so either suffices, and whether the operator in fact maintains the code is a question of evidence; and (iii) the meaning of “computer source code” in the Explanation — the listing of programmes, computer commands, design and layout and programme analysis of computer resource in any form. Punishment: up to three years, or fine up to ₹2,00,000, or both. Then apply Syed Asifuddin (ESN/SID of a CDMA handset = source code; reprogramming attracts s.65 prima facie; F.I.R. not quashable) and Sanjay Kumar v. State of Haryana (banking software; forged electronic records; conviction under ss.65 and 66 IT Act together with ss.420, 467, 468, 471 IPC, sentences concurrent). Close with the s.43 → s.66 bridge (Fig. 11): the same act is civilly actionable and, if done dishonestly or fraudulently, criminal.

12 Exam Strategy, High‑Value Points & Common Mistakes

12.1 High‑value points — do not miss these

  • The IT Act is an adaptation layer, not a standalone code — say this in the first line of every essay.
  • The First Schedule exclusions (s.1(4)): negotiable instruments other than cheques, powers of attorney, trusts, wills/testamentary dispositions, immovable‑property contracts, and notified classes.
  • “Computer” is defined functionally, not by brand or form factor — Syed Asifuddin (cell phone) proves it; Diebold limits it to the IT Act’s own context.
  • “Communication device” (s.2(1)(ha)) was inserted only in 2008 — before that, the handset had to be characterised as a computer.
  • The disjunctive “or” in s.65 — “required to be kept or maintained by law” — either limb suffices.
  • s.81 proviso (Copyright Act 1957 / Patents Act 1970) is the textual hinge for intermediary‑liability questions — cite it whenever s.79 is in issue.
  • s.75 extraterritorial reach requires a computer/network located in India; enforcement still depends on MLATs.
  • Distinguish digital signature (s.3, PKI‑specific) from electronic signature (s.3A, technology‑neutral, Second Schedule) — this distinction alone carries marks in nearly every paper.
  • India is not a signatory to the Budapest Convention — always mention it as a lacuna in cross‑border enforcement.
  • s.66A is dead law after Shreya Singhal (2015) — never cite it as an available charge.
  • Post‑2023 answers must cross‑refer the BSA 2023 (ss.62, 63 for electronic evidence), the BNS 2023 (renumbered criminal provisions), the DPDP Act 2023 + Rules 2025, and the Intermediary Guidelines (2026 amendment) on synthetically generated information/deepfakes.
  • Cite the statutory definition letters accurately — e.g., private key s.2(1)(zc) and public key s.2(1)(zd) as the statute now reads (the syllabus prints them the other way round).

12.2 Common exam mistakes

  • Treating the IT Act as a standalone code rather than an adaptation layer over the Contract Act, Evidence Act/BSA, IPC/BNS and IP statutes.
  • Forgetting the First Schedule exclusions even in a Unit I‑specific question.
  • Conflating the Internet, the Web and e‑mail.
  • Saying the petitioners in Syed Asifuddin were held guilty — the Court held only a prima facie case under s.65 and s.63 Copyright Act, and quashed the IPC counts.
  • Using Diebold to argue that an ATM is a computer — it held the opposite, for a fiscal Entry.
  • Answering entirely from the old Evidence Act without noting the BSA 2023 renumbering.
  • Citing Navjot Sandhu as good law without noting it was overruled in Anvar P.V. and reaffirmed against in Arjun Panditrao.
  • Failing to cite the purpose/object language when asked why the IT Act was enacted.
  • Missing the DPDP Act 2023 as the post‑IT‑Act privacy regime.

12.3 The model Unit I opening paragraph (drop‑in for any essay)

“The Information Technology Act, 2000, enacted to facilitate electronic commerce and remove the legal disability attached to electronic form, operates not as a standalone code but as an adaptation layer integrating with (and amending) the Indian Contract Act, 1872, the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (now the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023), the Indian Penal Code (now the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023), and intellectual‑property statutes. The Act’s core principle of functional equivalence — recognising electronic records, signatures and communications as performing the same legal functions as their paper counterparts — is the thread binding the substantive framework of authentication, civil liability, criminal liability, intermediary regulation, cyber security, e‑contract formation, domain‑name protection and cyberspace jurisdiction.” — Model Unit I answer opener (DU LB‑5031)

12.4 The model Unit I concluding paragraph

“The IT Act’s authentication regime reflects a deliberate legislative choice — functional equivalence over formal identity — but its implementation has been uneven: the 2000 Act’s PKI‑exclusive design was technologically premature and had to be supplemented by the technology‑neutral s.3A in 2008, producing a dual‑track regime whose CA‑licensing infrastructure has not kept pace with Aadhaar‑based and biometric authentication. Its definitions have been read expansively and purposively (Syed Asifuddin) but held context‑bound (Diebold Systems); its evidentiary counterpart has been judicially stabilised through Anvar P.V. and Arjun Panditrao; and its privacy objective has been carried forward by the DPDP Act, 2023. The doctrinal architecture is sound; what remains is institutional modernisation and legislative clarification.” — Model Unit I conclusion (DU LB‑5031)

13 Revision Summary & Mind Map

Fig. 12 — Unit I mind map: the whole of Part A, Unit I on one page
★ 60‑second revision script

IT = computers storing, retrieving, transmitting, manipulating data. Cyberspace = notional, borderless, decentralised, asynchronous, pseudonymous, persistent. Internet = pipes (TCP/IP); Web = one app layer (HTTP/HTML, 1989 Berners‑Lee); e‑mail = another (SMTP/IMAP/POP3). Uses: academics, e‑commerce (B2B/B2C/C2C), social networking. The IT Act 2000 (Act 21 of 2000; 17.10.2000; amended 2008, in force 27.10.2009) exists to remove the writing‑signature hurdle, facilitate e‑commerce, regulate certifying authorities, create civil/criminal liability, and make consequential amendments (ss.91–94). It applies throughout India and extraterritorially (s.1(2), s.75) but NOT to First Schedule documents (s.1(4)). It overrides inconsistent law (s.81) subject to the Copyright/Patents proviso. Definitions: information 2(1)(v), computer 2(1)(i), computer network 2(1)(j), computer resource 2(1)(k), computer system 2(1)(l), communication device 2(1)(ha). Asifuddin: cell phone is a computer; ESN reprogramming = s.65. Diebold: ATM is not a computer for a tax entry — definitions are context‑bound.
