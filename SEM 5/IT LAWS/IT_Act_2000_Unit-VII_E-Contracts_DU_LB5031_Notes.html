This is the first Unit of Part B — Applicability of Other Laws on E-Commerce, and it is the Unit in which the whole paper changes gear. Parts A asked what the IT Act, 2000 itself does. Part B asks how the ordinary, pre-internet law of India — the law of contract, evidence, taxation, crime and jurisdiction — maps onto electronic commerce. Unit VII handles the largest of those mappings: contract. Everything you learned in Units I–VI is used here as a set of building blocks — s.4 (electronic form = writing), s.5 (electronic signature), s.3A (the four-fold reliability test), s.7 (retention), s.11 (attribution), s.12 (acknowledgment) and s.13 (time and place of despatch and receipt). This Unit supplies the question they answer: when does an exchange of electronic messages become a legally binding contract, and where?

🎯 The one idea of this Unit — and the answer to almost every question

A contract is not unenforceable merely because it was formed electronically. Section 10A of the IT Act, 2000 says exactly this: where the proposal, the acceptance, and the revocation are "expressed in electronic form or by means of an electronic record", such a contract "shall not be deemed to be unenforceable solely on the ground that such electronic form or means was used for that purpose." That single sentence is the bridge. But note carefully what it does not do: it does not create a new, self-contained law of e-contracts. The substantive law of contract — offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, free consent, legality of object — remains the Indian Contract Act, 1872. The IT Act changes only the medium rules: ss.11–13 supply attribution, acknowledgment and the time/place of despatch and receipt, and ss.3A and 5 supply authentication. Everything in this Unit flows from holding those two ideas apart — the ICA governs whether there is a contract; the IT Act governs whether the electronic medium and its authentication are legally sufficient.

1The statutory framework — s.10A, ss.4–5 and ss.11–13

Syllabus item 1  This section establishes the architecture. Get this right and the rest of the Unit is detail; get it wrong and every answer will read as though the IT Act replaced the Contract Act.

1.1 Section 10A — the operative provision (learn it verbatim)

s.10A IT ACT 2000 · VALIDITY OF CONTRACTS FORMED THROUGH ELECTRONIC MEANS · VERBATIM

Validity of contracts formed through electronic means

Where in a contract formation, the communication of proposals, the acceptance of proposals, the revocation of proposals and acceptances, as the case may be, are expressed in electronic form or by means of an electronic record, such contract shall not be deemed to be unenforceable solely on the ground that such electronic form or means was used for that purpose.
Three drafting features to notice, because examiners test them. (i) It covers four communications, not two — the proposal, the acceptance, the revocation of the proposal and the revocation of the acceptance. (ii) The disqualifying ground is limited by the word "solely": the section removes only the electronic medium as a ground of invalidity. A contract formed electronically can still fail for want of consideration, for incapacity, for a void object, for fraud, or for failing the s.3A reliability test for an electronic signature. (iii) It is not a rule of formation — it is a rule against a specific invalidity. It creates no contract; it merely forbids one mode of destroying one. Section 10A is the Indian equivalent of Article 11 of the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce 1996, and it traces back to the recommendation of the Twelfth Report of the Law Commission of India (April 2006) on "Need for Amendment of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891 and the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934", which is the reason ss.5 and 6 were inserted into the Act by the Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008.
⚠️ The most common error in answers on this Unit

Students write "the IT Act governs e-contracts". It does not. The Indian Contract Act, 1872 governs e-contracts. The IT Act supplies the medium rules — what counts as "writing", what counts as a "signature", who is treated as having sent a record, and when and where it is treated as sent and received. Write this sentence in your answer: "The IT Act, 2000 does not create a separate law of e-contracts; it removes the medium as a ground of invalidity and supplies rules of attribution and of time and place, leaving the substantive law of contract to the Indian Contract Act, 1872." That sentence, on its own, is worth several marks in any long answer.

1.3 The bridge diagram

✅ Real example that runs through the whole diagram

You book a flight on an airline's website. ICA: the fare-and-schedule page is an invitation to offer (s.2(a)); your "Book now" click is the proposal; the airline's confirmation e-mail with the PNR is the acceptance (s.2(b)) and, if absolute and unqualified, forms an agreement (s.2(e)) supported by consideration (s.2(d)) — your fare for the carriage. IT Act: s.4 makes the confirmation a document; s.5/3A are satisfied by the login authentication or OTP-based e-signature; s.7 requires the airline to retain it; s.11(c) attributes the automatically generated confirmation to the airline because it came from a system programmed on its behalf; and s.13(3) deems the confirmation received at your place of business — which is why a suit for breach can be filed where you are.

🧩 What s.10A does not do — the four gaps you must name in an answer
  1. It does not make a contract out of a non-contract. An e-mail saying "we may supply 5,000 tonnes if the market suits us" is not a proposal under s.2(a) of the ICA; s.10A does not convert it into one.
  2. It does not validate an electronic signature that fails s.3A. The First Schedule to the IT Act (read with the proviso to s.1(4)(a)) excludes wills, any trust, powers of attorney, negotiable instruments other than a cheque, and conveyances of immovable property. A contract of sale of land by e-mail is still unenforceable on that ground — a ground independent of the medium used.
  3. It does not supply capacity, consent or a lawful object. A minor's online purchase remains void under s.11 of the ICA (see §3.4); a contract obtained by an online fraud remains voidable under ss.14–19.
  4. It does not tell you where the contract was made. That is s.13 — and it is the reason P.R. Transport Agency and WWE v. Reshma Collections exist as separate cases (§4).

2Kinds of e-contracts — email, web contracts, standard form

Syllabus item 2  The syllabus names three families: contracts by e-mail, web contracts and standard form contracts. "Web contracts" in turn divide into the familiar three — click-wrap, shrink-wrap and browse-wrap — plus the newer "scroll-wrap" and "sign-in-wrap". Learn the taxonomy as a tree, because a question asking you to "explain the kinds of e-contracts" is a question asking you to reproduce the tree with the enforceability ranking.

2.1 The taxonomy

2.2 Contract by e-mail — the simplest and, in India, the most-litigated

An e-mail contract is an ordinary ICA contract in which the proposal, acceptance and (if any) revocation are "expressed in electronic form" within s.10A. Three practical points deserve attention in an answer:

  • Whether a signature is needed. The ICA does not require a contract of this kind to be in writing at all, so no signature is needed. Where a statute does require writing — an arbitration agreement under s.7(4) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 is the classic example, and the pre-2016 position was unsettled — s.4 of the IT Act makes the e-mail a document, and ss.5/3A can supply the signature. This is precisely the analysis that carried the day in Trimex (§6), where the arbitration clause lived in an e-mailed commercial offer.
  • Which e-mail is the contract. In a chain of e-mails the contract is the last communication in which the parties are ad idem on all essential terms. Where later e-mails add new terms, they are counter-offers (ICA s.7), not acceptances. Where later e-mails merely clarify, the original contract stands. Trimex is the leading illustration: the Court refused to hold that a later, unsigned draft contract displaced a contract already concluded by e-mail.
  • Proof. Under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (in force from 1 July 2024, replacing the Indian Evidence Act, 1872), electronic records are documents (s.2(1)(d) read with s.2(1)(e)) and are admissible, subject to the certification requirement formerly in s.65B of the 1872 Act and now in s.63 of the BSA. Keep the mail server logs; an un-cached e-mail is not proof.

🧩 The one sentence to use when a question asks you to distinguish them

Click-wrap = the offeree sees the terms and affirmatively clicks assent (strongest); shrink-wrap = the offeree learns the terms on or after opening the product and assent is inferred from use or non-return (contested); browse-wrap = the offeree is deemed to agree merely by browsing, with no manifestation of assent at all (weakest, and not yet adjudicated in India). Standard form contracts are the drafting technique that all three employ — one party sets the terms, the other adheres — and they are tested not for assent but for unconscionability and unequal bargaining power under ss.16, 23 and (in the consumer context) s.2(46) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019.

2.4 Standard form contracts — the Indian law that polices them

A standard form contract is one in which one party (the "drafter") fixes the terms in advance and the other can only accept or reject the whole. This is the normal form of e-commerce: airline tickets, insurance, software licences, OTT subscriptions, telecom plans, app-store agreements. 3.3 The three "new" questions that only e-contracts raise

🧩 Question 1 — Is the web page an offer or an invitation to offer?

This is the question WWE v. Reshma Collections answered, and it is the single most examinable point in this Unit. The ordinary rule is that a display of goods with a price is an invitation to offer: Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v. Boots Cash Chemists (Southern) Ltd., [1953] 1 QB 401 (goods on a self-service shelf; the contract is made at the till); Fisher v. Bell, [1961] 1 QB 394 (a flick-knife displayed in a shop window was not an "offer for sale" for criminal law purposes); Harvey v. Facey, [1893] AC 552 (a statement of the lowest price is not an offer). The electronic application is that the web page is the shop window. The customer's order is the offer, and the seller's confirmation is the acceptance. WWE applied exactly this rule, and it is the reason the seller — not the customer — is the offeree.

But note the counter-argument, which a good answer should flag: where a website states a definite price, a definite quantity ("in stock: 1") and an unambiguous "Buy now", the page may itself be a unilateral offer, accepted by the customer's click. If so, the contract is made at the seller's server, not at the customer's location — which reverses the jurisdictional conclusion. Indian law has not resolved this; the answer must acknowledge the tension.

🧩 Question 2 — Can a machine accept?

Yes. s.11(c) of the IT Act attributes an electronic record to the originator if it was sent by an "information system programmed by or on behalf of the originator to operate automatically". An automated order confirmation, an auto-generated "we accept your bid" e-mail, or an algorithmic trading message is therefore the principal's own act. This is the statutory answer to the common-law anxiety that a machine cannot have contractual intention; combined with s.2(e) of the ICA (every promise and every set of promises forming the consideration for each other is an agreement), the automated exchange of promise and acceptance is a valid agreement. This is the doctrinal foundation for smart contracts (see §9.3).

🧩 Question 3 — Whose terms prevail?

In a "battle of the forms", the last shot ordinarily wins, but the Indian rule is stricter than the English: under s.7 of the ICA an acceptance must be absolute and unqualified, so any variance is a counter-offer and there is no contract until the last variation is accepted. This was the heart of Vedanta's defence in Trimex: that the terms were still being negotiated. The Supreme Court rejected it because the essential terms were already settled — the later exchanges merely recorded or clarified, they did not vary.

  • Flipkart order: product page (invitation) → "Buy now" (offer) → "Order confirmed, arriving Tuesday" (acceptance, auto-generated, s.11(c)) → payment captured (consideration) → contract complete on receipt of the confirmation at the buyer's place (s.13(3)).
  • IRCTC ticket: the availability page is an invitation; the booking request is the offer; the PRS "booking successful" e-mail/SMS is the acceptance; the fare is the consideration.

4Time and place of formation — s.13, Kedia and the internet

Syllabus item 4  Two of the three DU cases live here. This is the section that converts a doctrinal puzzle — when and where is an e-contract made? — into a decision tree you can apply in an exam.

4.1 The puzzle stated

Under the postal rule of the common law, an acceptance is complete on posting, so the contract is made where the letter is posted. India has never fully adopted the postal rule: s.4 of the ICA says the communication of an acceptance is complete "as against the proposer, when it is put in a course of transmission to him, so as to be out of the power of the acceptor; as against the acceptor, when it comes to the knowledge of the proposer". The first limb is the postal rule; the second is the receipt rule. The Supreme Court's answer to which limb applies came in Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas, AIR 1966 SC 543, where the Court held that the postal exception applies only to non-instantaneous communications (the post) and not to instantaneous ones (the telephone). A contract made by telephone is made where the acceptance is received and heard.

s.13 IT ACT 2000 · TIME AND PLACE OF DESPATCH AND RECEIPT · VERBATIM (FIVE SUB-SECTIONS)

Time and place of despatch and receipt of electronic record

(1) Save as otherwise agreed between the originator and the addressee, the despatch of an electronic record occurs when it enters a computer resource outside the control of the originator.
(2) Save as otherwise agreed between the originator and the addressee, the receipt of an electronic record occurs when—
  (a) if the addressee has designated a computer resource for the purpose of receiving such electronic record, receipt occurs—
    (i) when the electronic record enters the designated computer resource; or
    (ii) if the electronic record is sent to a computer resource of the addressee that is not the designated computer resource, when the electronic record is retrieved by the addressee; or
  (b) if the addressee has not designated a computer resource for the purpose of receiving such electronic record, receipt occurs when the electronic record enters any computer resource of the addressee.
(3) Save as otherwise agreed between the originator and the addressee, an electronic record is deemed to be despatched at the place where the originator has his place of business, and is deemed to be received at the place where the addressee has his place of business.
(4) The provisions of sub‑section (2) shall apply notwithstanding the fact that the place where the computer resource is located may be different from the place where the electronic record is deemed to have been received under sub‑section (3).
(5) For the purposes of this section—
  (a) if the originator or the addressee has more than one place of business, the place of business is that which has the closest relationship to the underlying transaction or, where there is no underlying transaction, the principal place of business;
  (b) if the originator or the addressee does not have a place of business, reference is to be made to the usual place of residence; and
  (c) if the originator or the addressee is a body corporate, its usual place of residence is the place of registration.
Why s.13(4) matters and is always missed: the server may be in Singapore, the cloud region in Mumbai, the mailbox in Virginia — it makes no difference. Receipt is deemed to occur at the addressee's place of business. Sub-section (4) exists precisely to stop the argument "the data was stored abroad, so the contract was made abroad". Sub-section (5)(c) is the corporate-residence tie-breaker, and sub-section (5)(a) resolves the multi-branch problem by reference to the closest relationship to the underlying transaction.

5Authentication, signature and the UN Convention 2005

Syllabus item 5  Two matters are grouped here because they are both about making the electronic record trustworthy: the IT Act's authentication apparatus (which answers the Nov–Dec 2025 question), and the United Nations Convention on the Use of Electronic Communications in International Contracts, 2005, which is the syllabus's only international instrument and the international counterpart of s.10A.

5.1 Electronic signature and authentication — the machinery that makes an e-contract provable

s.5 IT ACT · LEGAL RECOGNITION OF ELECTRONIC SIGNATURES · VERBATIM

Legal recognition of electronic signatures

Where any law provides that information or any other matter shall be authenticated by affixing the signature or any document shall be signed or bear the signature of any person, then, notwithstanding anything contained in such law, such requirement shall be deemed to have been satisfied, if such information or matter is authenticated by means of an electronic signature affixed in such manner as may be prescribed by the Central Government.
Explanation. For the purposes of this section, "signed", with its grammatical variations and cognate expressions, shall, with reference to a person, mean affixing of his hand written signature or any mark on any document, and the expression "signature" shall be construed accordingly. Section 5 is the counterpart of Article 7 of the UNCITRAL MLEC 1996 and Article 7 of the 2005 Convention.
s.3A IT ACT + SECOND SCHEDULE · THE FOUR-FOLD RELIABILITY TEST · VERBATIM

Electronic authentication technique — reliability

An electronic authentication technique shall be considered reliable only if—
  (a) the signature creation data or the authentication data are, within the context in which they are used, linked to the signatory or the authenticator and to no other person;
  (b) such signature creation data or authentication data were, at the time of signing, under the control of the signatory or the authenticator and of no other person;
  (c) any alteration to the electronic signature made after affixing such signature is detectable;
  (d) where the purpose of the legal requirement for a signature is to provide assurance as to the integrity of the information to which it relates, any alteration made to the information after the time it was signed is detectable.
Read the four limbs as four separate evidentiary promises: (1) identity — the key belongs to one person only; (2) control — that person, and nobody else, held it at the moment of signing; (3) signature integrity — tampering with the signature itself is visible; (4) record integrity — tampering with the signed document is visible. A party alleging that an e-signature is not reliable must attack one of these four limbs; that is the structure of the answer to the Nov–Dec 2025 question.
⚠️ The First Schedule — five documents that CANNOT be electronically signed

The proviso to s.1(4)(a) and the First Schedule exclude: (i) any trust; (ii) any will (and any other testamentary disposition); (iii) any power of attorney as defined in the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (s.1A); (iv) any negotiable instrument as defined in the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, other than a cheque; and (v) any contract for the sale or conveyance of immovable property or any interest in such property. These remain governed by the paper requirements of the Registration Act, 1908 and the Transfer of Property Act, 1882. This is a complete answer to the "denial of validity" limb of any e-signature question: an e-signature fails not because of the medium but because of the document. A contract for the sale of land cannot be concluded by e-mail, whatever the platform; everything else can.

✅ Real example — an e-signature dispute, worked through the four limbs

A borrower says the ₹10 lakh loan agreement uploaded on the lender's portal was never signed by her. The lender produces the e-Sign audit trail: the Aadhaar-linked OTP was delivered to the mobile number registered with the lender, the signature creation data were bound to her Aadhaar number alone (limb (a)), she alone controlled the mobile and the OTP at the moment of signing (limb (b)), the signed PDF's SHA-256 hash matches the hash recorded at signing (limbs (c) and (d)). The only escape for the borrower is to prove the OTP was intercepted or that the mobile was not in her control — i.e. to attack limb (b). If she cannot, the signature stands, and under s.11 the record is attributed to her.

5.2 The United Nations Convention on the Use of Electronic Communications in International Contracts, 2005

This is the syllabus's only international instrument, and it deserves a standalone answer. It is the first treaty (as opposed to a model law) to deal with electronic contracting, and it was designed to retrofit existing international trade treaties — the CISG, the New York Convention 1958, the Limitation Convention, the Hamburg Rules and others — for the electronic age, by removing the obstacles that paper-based requirements in those treaties create.

FeatureDetail

Article 3 — interpretationRegard is to be had to its international character, to the need to promote uniformity and to the observance of good faith in international trade.
Article 4 — non-discriminationA communication or contract shall not be denied legal effect, validity or enforceability solely on the ground that it is in electronic form. This is the textual parent of s.10A of the IT Act.
Article 5 — legal recognition of electronic signaturesSignature requirements are met by electronic signature where the method used is (a) as reliable as appropriate for the purpose, and (b) the signatory has indicated an intention to sign. Mirrors s.3A's reliability approach.
Article 6 — writingA legal requirement that information be in writing is met by electronic information if it is accessible so as to be usable for subsequent reference — the same test as s.4(a) of the IT Act.
Article 7 — signatureParallel to Art. 5 and to ss.5 and 3A of the IT Act.
Article 8 — originalAn electronic record meets a requirement for an "original" if its integrity is assured and it is accessible — the analogue of s.9 of the IT Act (retention/attributability) rather than of s.8, which India did not enact.
Article 9 — admissibility and evidential weightElectronic communications shall not be denied admissibility or evidential weight solely on the ground that they are electronic. Deemed despatch at the originator's place of business and deemed receipt at the addressee's place of business; multiple places of business resolved by the closest relationship to the underlying transaction. This is a near-exact parallel of s.13 of the IT Act — and is therefore the international anchor for both P.R. Transport Agency and WWE.
Article 11 — invitations to make offersA proposal to conclude a contract made through an interactive application (i.e. a website) is an invitation to make offers, unless the proposal clearly indicates an intention to be bound. This is the international rule that WWE v. Reshma Collections applied — it is the single most useful cross-reference in this Unit, because it shows the Indian court was not inventing a rule but following an internationally accepted one.
Article 12 — use of automated message systemsA contract formed through the interaction of an automated message system and a natural person is not invalid or unenforceable solely because no natural person reviewed each action. This is the international parent of s.11(c) of the IT Act — the provision that lets a machine accept.
Article 13 — availability of contract termsWhere information is to be made available to a party, it is not denied legal effect solely because it is not in the form in which it was originally provided, so long as its integrity and accessibility are assured. The basis for unilateral variation clauses in e-commerce terms of service.
Article 14 — error in electronic communicationsGives a person who made an input error in an interactive communication with an automated system the right to withdraw the erroneous portion, provided the system did not allow correction and the person notifies the other party without undue delay. The analogue of s.14 of the ICA (mistake) for the digital context — and a genuinely useful provision for mis-clicked orders.
Article 17 — place of businessLocation of equipment and technology supporting an information system does not by itself constitute a place of business; the closest-relationship rule mirrors s.13(5)(a) and reinforces s.13(4) (server location is irrelevant).
Articles 18–21Declarations (a State may exclude certain matters), final clauses, and the right to exclude the Convention's application to specified instruments. Note that the Convention permits States to make reservations — a point worth one sentence in an answer.

India's s.10A is the weakest of these instruments: it says only that electronic form is not by itself a ground of unenforceability. 6.2 Issues

⚖️ The issues framed
  1. Whether the exchange of e-mails between the parties concluded a binding contract for the supply of bauxite for five shipments.
  2. Whether the parties were ad idem on all the essential terms, or whether material terms (specifications, price inclusions, delivery point, insurance, commencement and conclusion dates, transfer of title, quality check, demurrage) remained open for negotiation.
  3. Whether the fact that a formal contract document was never prepared or executed prevented the contract from coming into existence.
  4. Whether the arbitration clause (clause 6) contained in the e-mailed commercial offer constituted an arbitration agreement enforceable under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996.
  5. Whether Dresser Rand S.A. v. Sanghvi Refracs Ltd., (2009) 4 SCC 341 — the authority Vedanta relied on — governed the facts.

6.3 Arguments of the parties

Vedanta Aluminium Ltd. (respondent) — the case AGAINST a concluded contract

  • No concluded contract. The e-mails were part of an ongoing negotiation; the parties never reached the stage of a final, executed agreement. A draft contract was in fact prepared but was never confirmed or signed.
  • The parties were not ad idem on essential features. The offer had contained two shipment options — 2 or 5 — and only the number was agreed. Everything else was still open: product specifications, price, what was included in the price, the point of delivery, insurance, the commencement and conclusion dates, transfer of title, quality check and demurrage.
  • The ambiguities were expressly pointed out in the e-mails themselves — so both sides knew the terms were unsettled.
  • Clause 6 was vague and ambiguous and could not found an arbitration agreement.
  • There was therefore no arbitration agreement at all, and the s.11(6) petition was misconceived.
  • Dresser Rand S.A. was directly applicable: there the Supreme Court held that the modification of general conditions did not conclude the contract, and that neither the Letter of Intent nor the General Conditions contained an arbitration agreement. On that footing Vedanta argued that an exchange of e-mails and a draft could not supply an arbitration clause.

Trimex International FZE Ltd. (petitioner — argued by Mr. K.K. Venugopal, Senior Advocate) — the case FOR a concluded contract

  • The contract was concluded by the acceptance of the five-shipment offer. Vedanta's e-mail of 16.10.2007 confirming "the deal for 5 shipments" was an acceptance absolute and unconditional within s.7 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872.
  • All essential terms were agreed: price, quantity, specifications, point of delivery, payment terms, discharge port, shipment lots, demurrage, the quality benchmark, and arbitration.
  • The intention to be bound was manifested by conduct. Trimex concluded its upstream deal with the Australian supplier on the very same day, and entered a binding Charter Party Agreement in Oslo on 17.10.2007, and later a formal Bauxite Sales Agreement with Rio Tinto for 225,000 tonnes. A party does not commit itself to third parties unless it believes itself bound.
  • Vedanta's own conduct confirmed the contract: it convened a meeting at Lanjigarh on 26.10.2007 at which signed minutes acknowledged the acceptance, and on 08.11.2007 Vedanta itself sent a formal contract containing a detailed arbitration clause (clause 29), which Trimex accepted with minor changes the same evening. A party cannot formalise a contract it says never existed.
  • The absence of a formal, executed document is irrelevant. Once a contract is concluded orally or in writing, the mere fact that a formal contract has to be prepared and initialled does not affect the acceptance or the implementation of the contract already concluded.
  • Dresser Rand S.A. was distinguishable — there, unlike here, no arbitration clause existed in any of the operative documents and the essential terms were left genuinely open.

6.4 Decision and reasoning

Held — petition allowed. The Supreme Court allowed the Arbitration Petition, appointed Justice B.N. Srikrishna as the sole arbitrator, fixed the place of arbitration at Mumbai, and directed the arbitrator to enter upon the reference within four weeks. The Court expressly stated that it expressed no opinion on the merits of the claims — that was for the arbitrator.
The reasoning, in the order the Court gave it. (1) Once a contract is concluded orally or in writing, the mere fact that a formal contract has not been prepared and initialled does not affect either the acceptance or the implementation of the contract already concluded. (2) On the facts, the essential terms were settled and the parties were ad idem: the quantity (five shipments), the price, the specifications, the point of delivery, the payment terms, the discharge port, the shipment lots, demurrage, the quality benchmark and arbitration. (3) The subsequent e-mails did not introduce new or unsettled terms; they recorded and clarified a bargain already struck. (4) The communication of acceptance was complete as against Vedanta when it was put in a course of transmission so as to be out of the acceptor's power, and as against Trimex when it came to Trimex's knowledge — the two limbs of s.4 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (Trimex opened the e-mail at 3.06 p.m., one minute after Vedanta sent it). (5) The arbitration clause in the e-mailed commercial offer (clause 6) was a valid and enforceable arbitration agreement; a separately signed document was not required. (6) The Court relied on English Court of Appeal and Privy Council authority that Indian law is no different on the point that the parties' intention to execute a formal agreement later does not defeat a contract already complete in substance.
✅ The principle evolved — write this, verbatim, in any answer on Trimex

An exchange of e-mails, containing an offer with all its essential terms and an unconditional acceptance of those terms, concludes a binding contract; the absence of a separately prepared and executed formal contract does not affect the acceptance or implementation of the contract already concluded; and an arbitration clause contained in the e-mailed offer is itself a valid arbitration agreement enforceable under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. Section 10A of the IT Act, 2000 removes the electronic medium as a ground of invalidity; the substantive question — whether there was offer, acceptance and ad idem — is answered entirely by the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

🧩 Why Trimex is the most useful of the three DU cases

Because it is the only Indian Supreme Court authority that (i) applies s.10A in substance, (ii) applies s.4 of the ICA to an e-mail acceptance, and (iii) holds that an arbitration agreement can be formed by e-mail — which, read with the 2015/2016 amendment inserting s.7(4)(b) into the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 ("including communication through electronic means"), closes the loop. Trimex is also the authority you cite when a party argues "we never signed anything": the answer is that the ICA never required a signature for this kind of contract, and s.10A confirms that the medium is irrelevant.

🧮 Model exam paragraph on Trimex (about 150 words)

"In Trimex International FZE Ltd. v. Vedanta Aluminium Ltd., (2010) 3 SCC 1, Trimex, a Dubai-based mineral trader, e-mailed a commercial offer on 15 October 2007 to Vedanta, an Indian aluminium producer, for the supply of bauxite; the offer contained an arbitration clause at clause 6 and was valid till 1200 hours IST on 16 October 2007. Vedanta confirmed 'the deal for 5 shipments' by e-mail at 3.05 p.m. on 16 October 2007, which Trimex opened at 3.06 p.m. Relying on it, Trimex concluded its deal with an Australian supplier the same day and entered a binding Charter Party Agreement in Oslo on 17 October 2007; signed minutes of a meeting at Lanjigarh on 26 October 2007 acknowledged the acceptance, and on 8 November 2007 Vedanta itself sent a formal contract containing an arbitration clause. When Vedanta later refused to perform and denied that any contract existed, the Supreme Court held that the exchange of e-mails had concluded a binding contract — the essential terms were settled and the parties were ad idem, the acceptance was complete under s.4 of the Contract Act, and the arbitration clause in the e-mailed offer was enforceable. The petition was allowed and Mr Justice B.N. Srikrishna was appointed arbitrator, with Mumbai as the place of arbitration."

7Case brief 2: World Wrestling Entertainment v. Reshma Collections (Del HC DB, 15.10.2014)

DU case 2FAO (OS) 506/2013 in CM Nos. 17627/2013, 18606/2013Del (DB)15.10.2014Badar Durrez Ahmed J.

Citation and status — and why this case is the centrepiece of the Unit

World Wrestling Entertainment, Inc. v. M/s Reshma Collection, FAO (OS) 506/2013 in CM Nos. 17627/2013 and 18606/2013, Delhi High Court (Division Bench), decided on 15 October 2014 by a Division Bench of Badar Durrez Ahmed and Vibhu Bakhru JJ. (the judgment was delivered by Ahmed J.). Reported in the trade-marks reports as 2014 (60) PTC 452 (Del) (DB). It is printed in full in the DU case material at pp. 181–194 — the only one of the three Unit VII cases that is.

The decision is formally about territorial jurisdiction (Unit IX), but it reaches that result through the law of contract formation — which is why the DU syllabus places it in Unit VII. It is the leading Indian authority on when and where a contract on a website is concluded, and it contains the only judicial statement in India that a website listing is an invitation to offer. Note the title: the DU syllabus writes "Reshma Collections"; the printed report writes "M/s Reshma Collection".

7.1 Facts

ElementThe facts as pleaded and as recorded by the Division Bench
PlaintiffWorld Wrestling Entertainment, Inc., a company incorporated under the laws of the State of Delaware, USA. It develops, produces and markets television programming, pay-per-view programming and live events, and licenses and sells branded consumer products bearing the well-known WWE brand. The WWE scratch logo and "World Wrestling Entertainment" are registered trademarks worldwide, including in India, and it owns copyright in the pictorial representations of WWE and its wrestlers. It holds registrations for talent names and slogans such as JOHN CENA, BATISTA, UNDERTAKER, TRIPLE H and RANDY ORTON. It offers apparel, posters, calendars, fact books, sunglasses, water bottles, key-rings and DVDs, and has more than 200 licensees in 86 countries, including India.
DefendantsM/s Reshma Collection and others, all residing and stationed in Mumbai, engaged in the manufacture and sale of garments and apparel — T-shirts, wrist bands, caps and other counterfeit goods — bearing reproductions of WWE's talents and prominent representations of WWE characters.
The suitCS (OS) 1801/2013 in the Delhi High Court, seeking permanent injunction for infringement of copyright, infringement of trademarks, passing off, dilution, rendition of accounts, damages and delivery up.
The impugned orderBy order dated 04.10.2013 the learned Single Judge, without issuing summons, directed the return of the plaint to the plaintiff under Order VII Rule 10 CPC for presentation before a court of competent jurisdiction, holding that the Delhi High Court had no territorial jurisdiction.
The jurisdictional pleaMade in paragraph 32 of the plaint (quoted verbatim below), under s.134(2) of the Trademarks Act, 1999 and s.62(2) of the Copyright Act, 1957 — the provisions that allow a plaintiff to sue where it "carries on business", notwithstanding the CPC.
The admitted positionThe plaintiff is a US company; all the defendants reside in Mumbai and do not carry on business within the jurisdiction of the Delhi High Court; the plaintiff does not reside or personally work for gain in Delhi; and the plaintiff has no agent, manager, servant or office in Delhi — not even a branch office.
The appealFAO (OS) 506/2013 — a Letters Patent Appeal against the return of the plaint.
📜 Verbatim extract — paragraph 32 of the plaint (DU case material, p. 181)

"32. It is submitted that this Hon'ble Court has territorial jurisdiction to entertain and try the present suit under Section 134 (2) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 and Section 62 (2) of the Copyright Act, 1957 on account of the fact the Plaintiff carries on business within the territorial limits of this Hon'ble court as briefly summarized below:
  i. The Plaintiff's programmes, consisting of its various characters including John Cena, Undertaker, Triple H, Randy Orton and Batista are broadcast at Delhi, within the territorial limits of this Hon'ble Court;
  ii. The Plaintiff's products, such as its merchandising goods and books, are available within the territorial limits of this Hon'ble Court;
  iii. The Plaintiff's goods and services are sold to consumers in Delhi through its websites which can be accessed and operated from all over the country, including from Delhi."

📜 Verbatim extract — s.134(2) Trademarks Act, 1999 and s.62(2) Copyright Act, 1957 (as set out by the Division Bench, DU case material, pp. 184–185)

Section 134(2) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999: "For the purpose of clauses (a) and (b) of sub-section (1), a 'District Court having jurisdiction' shall, notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (5 of 1908) or any other law for the time being in force, include a District Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction, at the time of the institution of the suit or other proceeding, the person instituting the suit or proceeding, or where there are more than one such person any of them, actually and voluntarily resides or carries on business or personally works for gain." Explanation: "For the purpose of sub-section (2), 'person' includes the registered proprietor and the registered user."

Section 62(2) of the Copyright Act, 1957: "For the purpose of sub-section (1), a 'district court having jurisdiction' shall, notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (5 of 1908), or any other law for the time being in force include a district court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction, at the time of the institution of the suit or other proceedings, the person instituting the suit or other proceeding or, where there are more than one such persons, any of them actually and voluntarily resides or carries on business or personally works for gain."

7.2 Issues

⚖️ The issues framed by the Division Bench
  1. Whether the Delhi High Court had territorial jurisdiction to entertain the suit, given that the plaintiff was a US company and the defendants were all in Mumbai.
  2. Whether, on the averments in the plaint alone (a demurrer), the plaintiff "carries on business" in Delhi within the meaning of s.134(2) of the Trademarks Act, 1999 and s.62(2) of the Copyright Act, 1957.
  3. Whether the three conditions of Dhodha House v. S.K. Maingi, 2006 (9) SCC 41 were satisfied — in particular the third condition, that "the essential part of the business must take place" at the place where jurisdiction is claimed.
  4. The e-contract question at the heart of the case: where is a contract concluded when the transaction takes place over the internet? — and, therefore, at which place does the essential part of the business transaction take place?
  5. Whether the Single Judge was right to hold, on the authority of Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas & Co., AIR 1966 SC 543, that internet transactions are no different from transactions over the telephone or fax, so that the tests for "when and where the contract is made" remain unchanged.
  6. Whether a plaint should be returned under Order VII Rule 10 CPC at the threshold, on a demurrer, where a case for territorial jurisdiction is made out on the plaint's averments.

7.3 Arguments of the parties

WWE (appellant / plaintiff) — argued by Mr. Praveen Anand

  • The "carries on business" provisions are wider than the CPC. The word "include" in ss.134(2) and 62(2) makes the provisions broader than s.20 of the CPC — reliance on Exphar SA v. Eupharma Laboratories Ltd., AIR 2004 SC 1682.
  • "Carries on business" is not confined to the head office; it can include a branch office — Wipro Ltd. v. Oushadha Chandrika Ayurvedic India (P) Ltd., 2008 (37) PTC 269 (Mad) (DB), where it was observed that the plain meaning of the expression "only conveys that wherever there is a business activity — be it the principal place or branch or branches — the party concerned is said to carry on business at such places".
  • No office is needed at all. Dhodha House itself says that the presence of the person at a place is not necessary and that business may be carried on through an agent, manager or servant. So the requirement of an office cannot be fatal.
  • The "new media" cases were not considered below. Dhodha House and Archie Comic Publications v. Purple Creations Pvt. Ltd., 172 DLT 234 (DB) did not deal with web-based business models — reliance on Yahoo! Inc. v. Akash Arora, 1999 (19) PTC 201; Banyan Tree Holding (P) Ltd. v. A. Murali Krishna Reddy, 2010 (42) PTC 361 (Del); and Wipro (Mad) (DB).
  • Virtual presence. Goods and services are sold to consumers in Delhi through the WWE website, which is accessible and operable from Delhi; payment is made from Delhi; goods are delivered to Delhi. Therefore the essential part of the business, insofar as Delhi customers are concerned, takes place in Delhi.
  • Procedural point. Territorial jurisdiction is a mixed question of law and fact; at the stage of a return of plaint under Order VII Rule 10, only the averments in the plaint ought to be seen, and if on a demurrer a case for jurisdiction is made out, the plaint ought not to be returned.

Reshma Collection (respondents / defendants)

  • The defendants are all in Mumbai and carry on no business in Delhi. The plaintiff is a US company. Neither resides nor works for gain in Delhi.
  • Dhodha House governs. Under the three conditions laid down there, the plaintiff cannot be said to carry on business in Delhi: (i) there is no special agent who attends exclusively to the plaintiff's business and carries it on in the plaintiff's name; (ii) there is no agent in the strict sense — indeed there is no agent at all; and (iii) the essential part of the business does not take place in Delhi — the plaintiff has no office, let alone a branch office, there.
  • Archie Comic (a Division Bench of the same Court) applied Dhodha House to a similar web-based merchandising dispute and held that the plaintiff did not carry on business in Delhi.
  • Mere availability of goods in Delhi is not enough. Dhodha House itself says (para 51) that "only because, its goods are being sold at a place would thus evidently not mean that it carries a business at that place", and that the plaintiff's goods being available in the Delhi market would not by itself mean the plaintiff carried on business in Delhi.
  • Parliament did not amend the law for the new media. While enacting the Information Technology Act, 2000, Parliament consciously did not amend the Trademarks Act, 1999, the Copyright Act, 1957 or the CPC to introduce concepts relating to new media. The basic concepts and legal principles therefore remain the same as those applicable to the "old media" of telephone and fax.

7.4 Decision

Held — appeal allowed; the impugned order is set aside; the suit is restored to its original number and placed before the Roster Bench for further steps on 28.10.2014. The Delhi High Court would, on a demurrer, have jurisdiction to entertain the suit, inasmuch as the appellant/plaintiff would be regarded as carrying on business in Delhi within the meaning of s.134(2) of the Trademarks Act, 1999 and s.62(2) of the Copyright Act, 1957. The learned Single Judge ought not to have returned the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 CPC. However, this would not preclude the defendants from raising the plea of jurisdiction on facts — which, if raised, could be considered by the court on the evidence and upon the law explained in the judgment. The Court expressly left that open.

7.5 Reasoning — the chain of reasoning in the Division Bench's own words

🧩 Step 1 — the case turns entirely on the meaning of "carries on business"

"The provisions of Section 134 (2) of the Trademarks Act, 1999 and of Section 62(2) of the Copyright Act, 1957 are material. Both the provisions are virtually identical. They are both non-obstante provisions ... Furthermore, the word 'include' is used in both the provisions so as to include a District Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction ... actually and voluntarily resides or 'carries on business' or personally works for gain. ... Therefore, the entire case turns on the meaning to be given to the expression 'carries on business'." (para 12). Since the plaintiff neither resides nor personally works for gain in Delhi, everything depends on "carries on business".

🧩 Step 2 — the Dhodha House three-condition test

The Division Bench extracted the three conditions from Dhodha House v. S.K. Maingi, 2006 (9) SCC 41, at para 46 of that decision: "The expression 'carries on business' and the expression 'personally works for gain' connote two different meanings. For the purpose of carrying on business only presence of a man at a place is not necessary. Such business may be carried at a place through an agent or a manager or through a servant. The owner may not even visit that place. The phrase 'carries on business' at a certain place would, therefore, mean having an interest in a business at that place, a voice in what is done, a share in the gain or loss and some control thereover." The three conditions are:

  1. The agent must be a "special agent" who attends exclusively to the business of the principal and carries it on in the name of the principal, and not a general agent who does business for anyone that pays him.
  2. The person acting as agent must be an agent in the strict sense of the term — the manager of a joint Hindu family is not an "agent" within this condition.
  3. "To constitute 'carrying on business' at a certain place, the essential part of the business must take place in that place." The illustration the Court gave: "a Bombay firm that has a branch office at Amritsar, where orders are received subject to confirmation by the head office at Bombay, and where money is paid and disbursed, is carrying on business at Amritsar and is liable to be sued at Amritsar." By contrast: "Only because, its goods are being sold at a place would thus evidently not mean that it carries a business at that place."
🧩 Step 3 — the first two conditions fail; the case stands or falls on the third

"Insofar as the first two conditions are concerned, they relate to agents. Inasmuch as it is an admitted position that the appellant/plaintiff has no agent in Delhi, those two conditions are clearly not fulfilled." (para 18). And: "The question then arises as to whether the third condition specified in Dhodha House is satisfied or not. ... the Supreme Court had observed that the expression 'carries on business' ... is much wider than what the expression in normal parlance connotes ... The Supreme Court, however, cautioned that although the expression has a very wide meaning, it is necessary that the three conditions specified in the said decision should be satisfied." (para 17). So the whole case reduces to one question: where does the essential part of an internet transaction take place?

🧩 Step 4 — the e-contract holding (paras 19, 22 and 23 — quote these verbatim)

Para 19 — the question posed: "Considering the fact that in Dhodha House, the third condition stipulated that to constitute 'carrying on business' at a certain place, the essential part of the business must take place there, it would be necessary to examine this aspect in the backdrop of business over the internet. When a transaction takes place over the internet, where is the contract concluded? This is the question which needs to be answered. In other words, at which place does the essential part of the business transaction take place?" The Court then described the transaction: "if a customer in Delhi wishes to purchase an article, which is available on the website of the appellant/plaintiff, the purchaser in Delhi accesses the said website on his or her computer in Delhi. The said purchaser places the order for the said article from his computer in Delhi. The payment is made either through a credit/debit card or through a cash card from Delhi and ultimately the goods are delivered to the customer in Delhi." And the Court noted that the server is in all likelihood in the USA and has not been pleaded to be in Delhi.

Para 22 — the extension of Kedia to the internet: "It is interesting to note that the Supreme Court had observed that the draftsman of the Indian Contract Act could not have envisaged use of the telephone as a means of personal conversation between parties separated in space and could not have intended to make any rule in that behalf. This applies with greater vigour insofar as commerce and business over the internet is concerned. Just as in the case of telephone conversation, there is instantaneous communication, so, too, in the case of business over the internet. Therefore, the rule that applies to contracts concluded over the telephone would apply with equal vigour to contracts concluded over the internet. In other words, contracts would be completed at the place where the acceptance is communicated."

Para 23 — the offer/acceptance analysis (the single most quotable passage in the Unit): "Let us now apply these principles to the type of transaction over the internet, which we have explained above. The website of the appellant/plaintiff refers to various goods and services. It is not an offer but an invitation to an offer, just as a menu in a restaurant. The invitation, if accepted by a customer in Delhi, becomes an offer made by the customer in Delhi for purchasing the goods 'advertised' on the website of the appellant/plaintiff. When, through the mode of the software and the browser, the transaction is confirmed and payment is made to the appellant/plaintiff through its website, the appellant/plaintiff accepts the offer of the customer at Delhi. Since the transaction between the two takes place instantaneously, the acceptance by the appellant/plaintiff is instantaneously communicated to its customer through the internet at Delhi. Therefore, in such a case, part of the cause of action would arise in Delhi."

🧩 Step 5 — the "virtual presence" / "virtual shop" reasoning

"...if the contracts and/or transactions entered into between the appellant/plaintiff on the one hand and its customers are being concluded in Delhi, can it not be said that the essential part of the business of the appellant/plaintiff, insofar as its transactions with customers in Delhi are concerned, takes place in Delhi? The offers are made by customers at Delhi. The offers are subject to confirmation/acceptance of the appellant/plaintiff through its website. The money would emanate or be paid from Delhi. Can it not then be considered that the appellant/plaintiff is, to a certain extent, carrying on business at Delhi? In our view, it would be so. Because of the advancements in technology and the rapid growth of new models of conducting business over the internet, it is possible for an entity to have a virtual presence in a place which is located at a distance from the place where it has a physical presence. The availability of transactions through the website at a particular place is virtually the same thing as a seller having shops in that place in the physical world. ... When the shop in the 'physical sense' is replaced by the 'virtual' shop because of the advancement of technology, in our view, it cannot be said that the appellant/plaintiff would not carry on business in Delhi." And, importantly, the Court found the Single Judge's reliance on Kedia correct but incomplete: "although the learned Single Judge had made a correct reference to the decision of the Supreme Court in the case of Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia, the full ramifications of that decision were not perceived by him."

✅ The principle evolved — write this, verbatim

(1) A website that displays goods and services is not an offer but an invitation to offer — "just as a menu in a restaurant". (2) The customer's order, placed from her computer and paid for from her location, is the offer. (3) The seller's confirmation of the transaction through the software and browser is the acceptance. (4) Because the internet transaction is instantaneous — like the telephone in Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia — the acceptance is communicated instantaneously to the customer at the customer's location, so the contract is completed there. (5) Consequently, part of the cause of action arises at the customer's place, and the seller has a "virtual presence" there: "the availability of transactions through the website at a particular place is virtually the same thing as a seller having shops in that place in the physical world". (6) The postal-rule exception is confined to non-instantaneous communications (post and telegram) and does not apply to the telephone or to the internet.

These are jurisdictional (Unit IX) questions, but the first is the natural critical follow-up to the WWE reasoning: WWE decided the case on a demurrer and expressly left the defendants free to prove on facts that no genuine transaction ever took place in Delhi. A pure "display" website, with no completed transaction in the forum, is therefore not squarely covered by WWE — the minimum contacts test and the Big Tree Entertainment line (on the effect of an exclusive cause-of-action clause) remain the answer.

The impugned order was set aside, the plaint restored, and the defendants were left free to raise the plea of jurisdiction on facts."

8Case brief 3: P.R. Transport Agency v. Union of India, AIR 2006 All 23

DU case 3AIR 2006 All 232004 (55) ALR 807Allahabad HC (DB)S. Harkauli and U. Pandey JJ.

Citation and status

P.R. Transport Agency v. Union of India & Ors., AIR 2006 Allahabad 23; 2004 (55) ALR 807. Allahabad High Court, Division Bench of S. Harkauli and U. Pandey JJ., decided on 24 September 2005. It was a writ petition (under Article 226 of the Constitution) challenging the cancellation of an e-auction bid — not a civil suit. Source note: this case is listed in the DU syllabus and TOC but is not printed in the DU case material; the brief below is drawn from the reported decision. Its significance for Unit VII is that it is the first Indian decision to apply s.13(3) of the IT Act to an e-mailed acceptance, and to hold that an e-mail is received where the addressee's place of business is.

8.1 Facts

ElementThe facts
The partiesPetitioner: P.R. Transport Agency (PRTA), a firm based at Chandauli and Varanasi, both in Uttar Pradesh. Respondents: the Union of India and Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. (BCC), a public sector undertaking held by the Court to fall within Article 12 of the Constitution (a "State").
The transactionBCC held an e-auction for the allocation of coal. PRTA submitted a bid. BCC accepted PRTA's bid for 4,000 metric tonnes at ₹1,625/- per metric tonne, from the Dobari Colliery.
Communication of acceptanceBCC communicated the acceptance by e-mail on 19 July. The e-mail was addressed to PRTA at its place of business — Chandauli and Varanasi, both within Uttar Pradesh.
Performance by PRTAPRTA, in accordance with the Terms of Allocation, deposited ₹81.12 lakhs by cheque drawn in favour of BCC.
The cancellationBCC thereafter e-mailed a cancellation of the allocation, citing "technical issues and unavoidable reasons". The real reason, as emerged, was that a higher bid had been missed because of a software flaw in the e-auction system.
The challengePRTA filed a writ petition in the Allahabad High Court — the bench at Allahabad, within whose territorial jurisdiction the cause of action was said to arise because the e-mail had been received at Chandauli and Varanasi.

8.2 Issues

⚖️ The issues framed by the Division Bench
  1. Whether the Allahabad High Court had territorial jurisdiction to entertain the writ petition, in the absence of any cause of action arising within Uttar Pradesh other than the receipt of the e-mail there.
  2. Whether the ouster clause (clause 10.5) of the tender agreement — which confined disputes to a court/forum elsewhere — excluded the writ jurisdiction of the High Court.
  3. On the merits: whether the cancellation of the accepted bid at a belated stage, without notice or hearing, violated natural justice.

8.3 Arguments of the parties

Union of India / BCC (respondents)

  • No cause of action in Uttar Pradesh. The e-auction was conducted, the bid accepted and the cancellation communicated from BCC's own systems; the petitioner's deposit was made by cheque; the coal was to be lifted at a colliery outside Uttar Pradesh. Nothing connected the dispute with the Allahabad High Court's territorial jurisdiction.
  • Clause 10.5 of the tender agreement is an ouster clause and the parties had agreed to it; the petitioner was bound by it and could not invoke the High Court's writ jurisdiction in a different forum.
  • The cancellation was justified on "technical issues and unavoidable reasons" — a matter within BCC's commercial and administrative discretion, not amenable to judicial review in the manner sought.

P.R. Transport Agency (petitioner)

  • A concluded contract existed. The bid was the offer; BCC's e-mail of 19 July accepting it was the acceptance; the contract was therefore complete, and the subsequent deposit of ₹81.12 lakhs was performance.
  • The acceptance was received in Uttar Pradesh. Under s.13(3) of the Information Technology Act, 2000, an electronic record is "deemed to be received at the place where the addressee has his place of business". PRTA's place of business was Chandauli and Varanasi, both in Uttar Pradesh. A part of the cause of action therefore arose within the Court's territorial jurisdiction.
  • Section 13 applies unless the parties have agreed otherwise ("save as otherwise agreed between the originator and the addressee"), and no such contrary agreement existed as to the place of receipt of the acceptance e-mail.
  • Clause 10.5 cannot oust the writ jurisdiction of the High Court under Article 226. A statute creates the jurisdiction of civil courts; that jurisdiction cannot be conferred on a court lacking territorial or pecuniary jurisdiction by consent. Under s.28 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 parties cannot bar the statutory jurisdiction of civil courts, though where a statute confers territorial jurisdiction on several civil courts they may agree to limit themselves to one or more. Ouster clauses can oust the territorial jurisdiction of civil courts only, not of the High Court under Article 226, where part of the cause of action arose within its territory.
  • Natural justice. BCC cancelled the accepted bid at a belated stage without any notice or hearing, and that too because of its own software failure. That was arbitrary and unfair, and violated the principles of natural justice.

8.4 Decision and reasoning

Held — the writ petition was allowed. The Allahabad High Court held that it had territorial jurisdiction, that the ouster clause did not exclude its writ jurisdiction, that BCC fell within Article 12 of the Constitution, and that the cancellation of the bid at a belated stage without hearing violated natural justice. The Court directed BCC to hand over the coal to P.R. Transport Agency in accordance with its bid.
The reasoning, in four steps. (1) Territorial jurisdiction. The e-mail communicating the acceptance was intended for the address at which the company was working — Chandauli and Varanasi, both within Uttar Pradesh. Applying s.13(3) of the IT Act, the record is deemed to be received at the addressee's place of business. The place of contract is determinative of territorial jurisdiction in a breach-of-contract action; and a part of the cause of action arose within the Court's jurisdiction. (2) The ouster clause. Jurisdiction is subject to the agreement of the parties and, absent agreement, to s.13 of the IT Act. But the jurisdiction of civil courts is created by statute and cannot be conferred on a court lacking territorial or pecuniary jurisdiction by consent. Under s.28 of the ICA, parties cannot bar the statutory jurisdiction of civil courts; but where a statute confers territorial jurisdiction on several civil courts, they may agree to limit themselves to one or more without violating s.28. Courts having jurisdiction under s.20(c) of the CPC are not ousted. (3) Article 226. Ouster clauses can oust the territorial jurisdiction of civil courts only, and not of the High Court under Article 226, where part of the cause of action arose within its territory. Article 226 and s.20 CPC are similar in permitting jurisdiction where the cause of action arises in full or in part; they differ in that the High Court can act suo motu. (4) Natural justice. BCC was a "State" within Article 12; the cancellation, at a belated stage, without hearing, was arbitrary.
✅ The principle evolved — write this, verbatim

An e-mail communicating an acceptance is received where the addressee's place of business is; s.13(3) of the Information Technology Act, 2000 therefore fixes the place of receipt, and the place of contract determines territorial jurisdiction in an action for breach of contract, so a part of the cause of action arises within the territorial jurisdiction of the court at that place. An ouster clause in the tender agreement cannot exclude the writ jurisdiction of the High Court under Article 226 of the Constitution, although it may oust the territorial jurisdiction of an ordinary civil court. Where a public undertaking within Article 12 cancels an accepted bid at a belated stage without notice or hearing, that cancellation violates natural justice and will be set aside.

🧩 Why P.R. Transport Agency matters for Unit VII, and its limits
  • It is the Indian application of s.13(3) — the only decision of its kind. Where WWE uses the Kedia instantaneous-communication route, P.R. Transport Agency uses the statutory receipt rule. Both end at the addressee's place.
  • It is an Article 226 writ petition, not a civil suit. So its ratio on jurisdiction is about the High Court's writ jurisdiction, not about a district court's civil jurisdiction. Say so in an answer — examiners reward the distinction.
  • It confirms the "save as otherwise agreed" proviso. Had the tender agreement contained a clause agreeing that the acceptance would be deemed received at BCC's place of business, s.13(3) would have been displaced and the outcome on jurisdiction would have been different. This is the practical drafting lesson of the case: an e-contract should specify the place of formation.
  • It is not an IT Act case at first instance — the Court used the IT Act as a jurisdictional tool. But the tool is s.13, which is precisely the section the syllabus pairs with s.10A.
🧮 Model exam paragraph on P.R. Transport Agency (about 130 words)

  • ss.15–20, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 — place of suing; s.20(c) permits suit where the cause of action wholly or in part arises.
  • Article 226, Constitution of India — the High Court's power, in terrorem, to issue writs "throughout the territorial limits in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction".

9.2 Critical analysis — the four criticisms that earn marks

⚠️ Criticism 1 — the "instantaneous" assumption in WWE

WWE extends Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia (telephone = instantaneous communication) to internet transactions on the footing that "just as in the case of telephone conversation, there is instantaneous communication, so, too, in the case of business over the internet." But in practice most website confirmations are not instantaneous in the contractual sense: they are (i) delayed — an order may be acknowledged instantly but confirmed after a manual review; (ii) batch-processed overnight; (iii) conditional — "your order is subject to availability"; or (iv) human-approved for high-value transactions. In each case the acceptance is not "instantaneously communicated" and the Kedia analogy breaks down; the correct analysis would then be s.13(2) (receipt on entry into the designated resource) and, if the parties have agreed a place, the agreement. The answer to a critical question is: WWE is right for genuinely interactive transactions but is over-inclusive for delayed or conditional confirmations.

⚠️ Criticism 2 — two textually consistent but distinct routes

P.R. Transport Agency applies s.13(3) to an e-mail and fixes the place at the addressee's place of business. WWE applies the Kedia instantaneous rule to a website and fixes the place where the acceptance is received. Usually the two converge. But they can diverge: where an e-mail acceptance is sent to a mailbox in a place other than the addressee's place of business — a Gmail account hosted abroad, or a shared corporate mailbox — s.13(3) still points to the place of business, whereas a purely instantaneous analysis might point elsewhere. And where the website's confirmation is not instantaneous, the s.13 route and the Kedia route give different answers. A first-class answer names both routes and says which applies and why.

⚠️ Criticism 3 — no Indian authority on browse-wrap, and none on the unilateral-offer reading

India has no decision on browse-wrap, shrink-wrap or scroll-wrap terms; the enforceability ranking is drawn entirely from US authority (ProCD, Step-Saver, Specht, Meyer v. Uber), which is persuasive only. Worse, WWE's "invitation to offer" reading is not the only possible one: where a website states a definite price, a definite available quantity and an unambiguous "Buy now", the page may be a unilateral offer accepted by the click — in which case the contract is made at the seller's server and the jurisdictional conclusion is reversed. Article 11 of the 2005 Convention leaves this open by its final words ("unless the proposal clearly indicates an intention to be bound"). Say this, and you show you have read the case critically rather than merely reproduced it.

⚠️ Criticism 4 — the First Schedule exclusions and the DPDP consent layer

Two structural gaps in Indian e-contract law deserve mention. (a) The First Schedule excludes wills, trusts, powers of attorney, non-cheque negotiable instruments and conveyances of immovable property from e-signature — so the "everything is enforceable electronically" narrative is wrong for a significant class of transactions. (b) The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025 now layer a separate consent on top of the contractual one: consent under s.6 of the DPDP Act must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous with a clear affirmative action, and must be limited to the specified purpose, with a notice itemising the data and the purpose. A single bundled "I Agree to the Terms of Service and Privacy Policy" checkbox may satisfy the ICA but not the DPDP Act — a genuinely modern point that very few answers will contain.

DU Previous Year Question · Nov–Dec 2025 · Question 1 · Unit VII (E-Contracts) · the only real DU PYQ for this Unit
Q.1Mr. Arjun enters an online agreement via email with a US company. Both parties digitally sign via an online platform. The company later denies the validity of the electronic signature. Examine the validity of this e-contract.

Step 1 — Identify the three legal questions the problem actually raises. (i) Is an agreement formed by e-mail a valid contract? (ii) Is an electronic signature legally valid? (iii) What must the company prove to defeat it? Answer all three; a candidate who answers only (ii) loses half the marks.

Step 2 — The medium is not a ground of invalidity (s.10A IT Act, 2000). Section 10A provides that "where in a contract formation, the communication of proposals, the acceptance of proposals, the revocation of proposals and acceptances, as the case may be, are expressed in electronic form or by means of an electronic record, such contract shall not be deemed to be unenforceable solely on the ground that such electronic form or means was used for that purpose." Mr. Arjun's e-mailed agreement with a US company is therefore not unenforceable merely because it was formed by e-mail. Note the word "solely": the section removes only the electronic medium as a ground of invalidity, and does not cure defects of offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, consent or object, which remain governed by the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

Step 3 — The substantive law of contract is the ICA. The e-mails must still contain a proposal (s.2(a)) and an acceptance that is absolute and unqualified (ss.2(b), 7), supported by consideration (s.2(d)), by parties competent to contract (s.11), with free consent (ss.13–22) and a lawful object (s.23). Where the website listing is the invitation to offer and the customer's order is the offer (as held in World Wrestling Entertainment, Inc. v. M/s Reshma Collection, FAO (OS) 506/2013, Del HC DB, 15.10.2014), the seller's confirmation is the acceptance. The leading authority that an exchange of e-mails alone concludes a binding contract is Trimex International FZE Ltd. v. Vedanta Aluminium Ltd., (2010) 3 SCC 1, where the Supreme Court held that the e-mailed commercial offer and its acceptance concluded a contract, that the absence of a separately prepared and executed formal document did not affect the acceptance or implementation of the contract already concluded, and that the arbitration clause contained in the e-mailed offer was itself enforceable.

Step 4 — The electronic signature is legally recognised (s.5 and s.3A IT Act). Section 5 provides that where any law requires information or a document to be authenticated by affixing a signature, that requirement is deemed satisfied if it is authenticated by an electronic signature affixed in the prescribed manner. The prescribed manner is supplied by s.3A read with the Second Schedule: an electronic authentication technique is reliable only if (a) the signature creation data or authentication data are, within the context in which they are used, linked to the signatory and to no other person; (b) those data were, at the time of signing, under the control of the signatory and of no other person; (c) any alteration to the electronic signature made after affixing it is detectable; and (d) any alteration to the information after it was signed is detectable. If both Mr. Arjun and the US company signed through an online platform that satisfies these four conditions — for example a class-3 Digital Signature Certificate, an Aadhaar-based e-Sign, or a platform that hashes the signed record — the signatures are valid and the company's denial is legally untenable.

Step 5 — Check the First Schedule exclusions. The proviso to s.1(4)(a) and the First Schedule exclude from electronic signature: wills and other testamentary dispositions; trusts; powers of attorney under s.1A of the Indian Contract Act, 1872; negotiable instruments other than a cheque; and any contract for the sale or conveyance of immovable property or any interest in it. A commercial agreement of the kind described — an ordinary online agreement by e-mail — is not within any of these exclusions. So the First Schedule does not assist the company.

Step 6 — Proof and attribution. The signed record is attributed to the signatory under s.11 of the IT Act (sent by him, or by a person with authority to act on his behalf, or by an information system programmed on his behalf to operate automatically). Its admissibility depends on the s.63 certificate under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (formerly s.65B of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872). If the agreement contains an arbitration clause, it is valid in writing by virtue of s.7(4)(b) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 ("including communication through electronic means"), and after N.N. Global Mercantile (P) Ltd. v. Indo Unique Flame Ltd., (2023) 8 SCC 175, even an unstamped such agreement is not void but merely inadmissible until stamp duty is paid.

Step 7 — What the company must prove to succeed, and the conclusion. The company's only route is to attack one of the four limbs of s.3A — typically limb (b), by proving that the signature creation data were not under Mr. Arjun's exclusive control at the time of signing (for example that the platform's private key was compromised, or that a shared credential was used), or limb (a), by showing that the signature was not uniquely linked to him. The burden of proving that rests on the company as the party disputing the record. Failing that proof, the contract is valid and binding: the e-contract stands, and the denial of the electronic signature's validity is legally untenable.

Model conclusion: The e-contract between Mr. Arjun and the US company is valid and enforceable. Section 10A of the IT Act, 2000 prevents the electronic medium from being a ground of invalidity; the substantive validity is tested under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, and Trimex International FZE v. Vedanta Aluminium confirms that an exchange of e-mails alone concludes a binding contract. The electronic signatures are valid under s.5 read with s.3A of the IT Act because all four reliability conditions are satisfied, and the transaction is not within the First Schedule exclusions. The company's denial can succeed only if it proves that one of the four s.3A conditions was not met — most commonly that the signatory did not have exclusive control of the authentication data at the time of signing.

11.2 Common exam mistakes — avoid every one of these

⚠️ The ten mistakes that lose the most marks in this Unit
  1. Writing "the IT Act governs e-contracts". It does not. The ICA governs; the IT Act supplies medium rules. Say this in the first two lines of any long answer.
  2. Treating s.10A as a rule of formation. It is a rule against a specific invalidity — note the word "solely".
  3. Forgetting the word "solely" and therefore forgetting that capacity, consent, consideration and object still matter.
  4. Confusing the two routes to "where": Kedia/instantaneous for websites (WWE) and s.13(3)/receipt for e-mail (P.R. Transport Agency). Name both; say which applies.
  5. Saying WWE decided the contract was invalid, or that WWE was about counterfeit goods only. It decided jurisdiction, on a demurrer, and left the defendants free to contest it on facts.
  6. Calling P.R. Transport Agency a civil suit. It was an Article 226 writ petition.
  7. Omitting the First Schedule exclusions. A question on electronic signature validity is not complete without them.
  8. Not naming the four limbs of s.3A. A question that says "examine the validity of the electronic signature" is asking you to work through (a)–(d).
  9. Stating the ranking without the standard-form overlay. The ranking is click-wrap > shrink-wrap > browse-wrap, but every web contract is also a standard form contract and attracts ICA ss.16/23 and the consumer-protection overlay.
  10. Writing nothing current. Mention the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (s.63), the DPDP Act, 2023 and DPDP Rules, 2025, the E-Commerce Rules, 2020, and s.7(4)(b) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. That single paragraph distinguishes a good answer from a first-class one.

Reshma Collections establishes where a website contract is concluded and that the seller thereby has a virtual presence at the customer's place; and P.R. Transport Agency establishes where an e-mailed acceptance takes effect for the purposes of jurisdiction. The law is, however, not complete: there is no Indian authority on browse-wrap terms, the assumption that website confirmations are instantaneous does not always hold, and the DPDP Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025 have now added a statutory consent layer that the Indian Contract Act alone does not supply. The direction of travel — in India and under the UN Convention of 2005 — is towards functional equivalence: the electronic record is to be treated as the legal equivalent of the paper record, and its form is to be no ground for denying its effect."
