Why “pleadings” when the State is the prosecutor?

KAMKUS answers it in the opening of its Criminal unit: “In offences State becomes the party and the accused has to put up his defence. … it is the responsibility of the State to launch prosecution” 📗 — so no private pleading is needed to start a police case. Private drafting enters the criminal process at exactly five doors: (i) a private complaint (KAMKUS (I); item 29 is its statutory species); (ii) a criminal miscellaneous petition (KAMKUS (II); s. 482 CrPC/528 BNSS quashing etc.); (iii) a bail application (KAMKUS (III); items 27–28); (iv) statutory private applications — maintenance (item 30), compensation, DV (d3); and (v) appeals/revision (KAMKUS (IV)). Every DU model in this unit lives in doors (i), (iii) and (iv).

Section 2 · Item 27 — pp. 76–77

Application for Grant of Regular Bail Bail while in custody — CJM (District), Delhi — s. 480 BNSS (= s. 437 CrPC)

📘 DU Item 27 — Application for Grant of Bail 📗 KAMKUS “(iii) Bail Application” pp. 73–81 BNSS ss. 478–484, 187(3) ⟲ s. 436 / 437 / 439 CrPC PYQ: DU 2017 Q1(b) (Form 27)

2.1 — The theory every bail answer must carry (KAMKUS, prescribed book) 📗

(a) History & meaning — the “why” behind bail

KAMKUS opens the bail application with the institutional memory of the remedy: “The concept of bail has a long history and deep roots in English and American law. In medieval England, the custom grew out of the need to free untried prisoners from disease-ridden jails while they were waiting for the delayed trials conducted by traveling justices; prisoners were bailed, or delivered, to reputable third parties … who accepted responsibility for assuring their appearance at trial.”

“Bail, in English common law, is the freeing or setting at liberty of one arrested or imprisoned upon any action, either civil or criminal, on surety taken for his appearance on a certain day and a place named.” KAMKUS, p. 73 (uploaded book) — DU-adjacent prescribed reading for this unit.

And the operative Indian-law effect, in KAMKUS’s words: “The effect of granting bail is … not to set the prisoner free from jail or custody, but to release him from the custody of law and to entrust him to the custody of his sureties who are bound to produce him to appear at his trial at a specified time and place.”

Old law vs new law — a definition now exists!

KAMKUS states that under the CrPC “the word ‘bail’ has not been defined” — bailable/non-bailable offences were defined (its cite: s. 4(1)(b) & s. 2(a) CrPC). Under BNSS 2023 that has changed: “bail” and “bail bond” are defined in s. 2(1)(b) and s. 2(1)(d) 🌐, and the whole bail chapter runs as Chapter XXXV, ss. 478–496. Perfect exam line: “KAMKUS’s ‘undefined term’ observation no longer holds after 1-7-2024.”

(b) The three heads of bail (KAMKUS framework — memorise this triad)

Authorities that grant bail (KAMKUS’s list): (a) police officers — under ss. 41, 42, 43, 151 (arrest without warrant) and for warrant arrests, may release on bail or on a bond without sureties; (b) magistrates & courts — the whole Chapter XXIII machinery (old ss. 436–439); (c) Government — powers of suspension/remission of sentence (old s. 432). Practice rule KAMKUS insists on: a person desiring bail should ordinarily first approach the lower court — not inflexible, because s. 439 (now s. 483 BNSS) confers special powers on the High Court and Court of Session.

“It would be advantageous if notice is given to the public prosecutor … Since bail in bailable cases is a matter of right … there is no scope for giving notice to the Public Prosecutor.” KAMKUS, p. 74 — notice-to-PP discipline for discretionary bail (no statutory compulsion; court may direct notice).

(c) The three risks the prosecution will argue (KAMKUS’s checklist — learn as M-A-T)

M — absconding / flight risk on release A — tampering with prosecution witnesses by influence T — tendency to repeat / continue the offence

DU item 27’s paragraphs 8–9 answer M and A, paragraph 11 answers the “history” limb of T — this is how you map theory paragraphs to drafting paragraphs.

Real-practice nugget KAMKUS drops inside the form chapter

Before the charge-sheet is filed, the case is only a CR Number (Crime Register number) of the police station; “as soon as the charge-sheet is filed in the court, the case is numbered as Court-case on its file.” That is why DU’s bail caption asks for FIR no. + police station (crime-register identity) while the application itself gets a Bail Application No. (court-case identity). 📗

Teaching point — ¶1 vs ¶3 read as a novice, corrected as a drafter

¶1 recites recovery of 3 gms smack “from his pocket”, then ¶3 flatly says “nothing was recovered”. That is not a contradiction if drafted with discipline: ¶1 must be pleaded as the allegation (“it is alleged that…”) — DU’s own opening words — and ¶3 as the defence to its truth. In exam, write it that way and you earn the mark for “stage vs stance”. A bare ¶3 without the “alleged” frame reads as self-contradiction — the exact slip examiners love.

Proofreading is part of drafting

DU’s own printed text carries “tempering with the prosecution evidence” (tampering) and “reLease on bail” (release), plus “mater of fact” in item 28. Similar slips: d5’s “PARAISE REPLY”. Rule: run a search for the misspelled family of every legal keyword (bail/bailed, custody, undertaking, complainant) before filing — costs nothing, saves credibility.

The jail-attestation rule (the note at the foot of item 27)

“To be supported by affidavit of Pairokar and Vakalatnama duly attested by Jail Authorities.” — An undertrial’s affidavit & vakalatnama are executed inside the jail; the Jail Superintendent / authorities verify the signatures before the advocate files them. Miss this and the application is returned at the filing counter. (Keep the Du–Basu discipline from d5 in mind: every interaction with a person in custody has a procedural formality attached — here, attestation.) Item 28 needs only “affidavit” because the applicant is at liberty; item 27’s applicant is not.

2.4 — Case law for item 27 🌐 supplements (not in DU chapter)

Sanjay Chandra & Ors. v. Central Bureau of Investigation — (2012) 1 SCC 40 🌐
Facts
Appellants Sanjay Chandra, Vinod Goenka, Gautam Doshi, Surendra Pipara and Hari Nair were accused in the 2G spectrum allocation scam (2008) — IPC cheating, forgery and Prevention of Corruption Act charges alleging criminal conspiracy and loss to the exchequer. In judicial custody for months; bail denied by lower courts on (i) gravity of offence and (ii) possible witness-tampering. Trial had not even framed charges; evidence voluminous, 17 accused.
Issues
(1) Can pre-trial detention be justified merely by the seriousness of the accusation? (2) What must the prosecution actually show to resist bail?
Arguments — appellants
Bail is the rule, denial the exception; personal liberty on a “very high pedestal”; they cooperated and were never arrested during investigation; no flight/tampering material; trial judge lacked power to remand a person appearing on summons (ss. 87/88 CrPC); seriousness must be gauged by maximum punishment (7 years), not alleged monetary loss.
Arguments — prosecution
Offence hits the economic fabric of the country; quantum of punishment is not the only measure; relevant consideration is society’s interest; earlier SLP of co-accused Sharad Kumar was refused.
Decision
Bail granted — personal bond + two solvent sureties of ₹5 lakh each, conditions: not to induce/threaten anyone connected with the case; to appear on dates fixed. Investigation complete; no material on absconding or tampering; continuing detention risked exceeding any eventual sentence; earlier refusal was at a pre-charge-framing stage and did not bind.
Principle
“Bail is the rule and committal to jail is an exception.” Detention before conviction is not a punishment; gravity of allegation alone cannot defeat bail — the court must see concrete risk of flight/tampering/influence, balancing Art. 21’s presumption of innocence and speedy trial against securing attendance. This is the doctrinal home of DU ¶¶ 8–11.
Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar — (2014) 8 SCC 273 🌐

Facts: arrests made mechanically in cases (notably matrimonial cruelty under s. 498A IPC) without applying the statutory filters. Issue: whether the power to arrest must be exercised automatically.

Held: for offences punishable up to 7 years — no automatic arrest; police must first issue s. 41A CrPC notice where arrest is not necessary, record reasons in writing when arrest does occur; Magistrates must independently satisfy themselves before authorising detention.

Principle: arrest is not the default — it is a guarded power. Pairs with DU ¶1 (arrest recited) by asking: was the stage-recital even lawful?

Section 3 · Item 28 — pp. 78–79

Application for the Grant of Anticipatory Bail Pre-arrest protection — Sessions Judge (District), Delhi — s. 482 BNSS (= s. 438 CrPC)

📘 DU Item 28 — Anticipatory Bail 📗 KAMKUS pp. 75–77 (object + pleading standard) BNSS s. 482 · s. 483 ladder ⟲ s. 438 CrPC PYQ: DU 2019 Part A (Form 28) Case law: Sibbia · Mhetre · Sushila Aggarwal

3.1 — Theory before the drafting (KAMKUS) 📗

Provision & object. KAMKUS: “Sec. 438 CrPC 1973 says the issuance of a direction that in the event of arrest of the application he shall be released on bail. Sec. 438 confers power on the High Court as also the Court of Session … which is called anticipatory bail.” Object, in its words: if the person already holds an order “he would be released immediately without having to undergo the rigours of jail even for a few days which would necessarily be taken up if he has to apply for bail after arrest.” (Today: s. 482 BNSS, same twin forum; refusal ladder via s. 483.)

“It is unrealistic to expect the applicant to draw up his application with the meticulousness of a pleading in a civil case and such is not the requirement of the section. But specific events and facts must be disclosed by the applicant in order to enable the court to judge the reasonableness of his belief … facts on the basis of which a direction under s. 438(1) is sought must be clear and specific, not vague and general — it is only by the observance of that principle that a possible conflict between the right of an individual to his liberty and the right of the police to investigate … can be avoided.” KAMKUS, p. 75 — the single most quotable anticipatory-bail line in the uploaded corpus. It tells you why DU ¶¶3–6 exist.
“Rule of prudence requires that notice should be given to the other side before passing a final order [in] anticipatory bail so that [a] wrong order is not obtained by a party placing incorrect or misleading facts or suppressing material facts.” KAMKUS, pp. 75–76 — notice-to-PP/prosecution prudence (contrast: s. 478 bailable cases need none).

What KAMKUS says courts weigh. Same M-A-T trio (flight, tampering/influence, repetition) + reasonableness of apprehension; no provision compels notice, but the court may direct it; in bailable cases (right) notice to PP has no scope. Offence-specificity — KAMKUS doesn’t say it, but the Constitution Bench in Sibbia does (§3.4): an order must specify the offence(s) it covers, otherwise it is “neither a passport to the commission of crimes nor a shield against any and all kinds of investigation”. 🌐

3.3 — Case law for item 28 🌐 supplements (not in DU chapter)

Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia v. State of Punjab — (1980) 2 SCC 565 (Constitution Bench, 5 Judges) 🌐
Facts
Anticipatory-bail protection had been sought in Punjab at the pre-FIR stage and granted; the litigation that followed (registration of FIR, conditions imposed, scope of the order) put the constitutionality and contours of s. 438 CrPC before a five-Judge Bench.
Issues
(1) Validity of s. 438; (2) whether AB can be granted before an FIR is registered; (3) whether s. 437’s conditions (gravity, offence class) should be read into s. 438; (4) duration and conditions of the order; (5) whether the order must specify offences.
Arguments
For the remedy: Art. 21 liberty, anticipatory bail is a constitutional adjunct of the right not to be arrested unlawfully; rigid rules defeat its object. Against / restrictive view: such orders obstruct investigation, shelter offenders from lawful arrest.
Decision
s. 438 upheld; AB can issue without a registered FIR where apprehension is genuine; s. 437 conditions are not to be mechanically imported; the order must be offence-specific — “the Court … must take care to specify the offence or offences in respect of which alone the order will be effective”; an order “is neither a passport to the commission of crimes nor a shield against any and all kinds of investigation”; bail conditions must not be so onerous as to become illusory; ordinarily protection runs until conclusion of trial unless the court limits it.
Principle
Anticipatory bail is a judicially-discretionary, liberty-securing device — broad, fact-sensitive, offence-specific, conditioned so as not to be oppressive: “Bail is not a privilege, it is the rule” even before arrest. Map: DU ¶8 (apprehension) + ¶10 (undertaking) + offence-specific heading = Sibbia compliance in drafting form.
Siddharam Satlingappa Mhetre v. State of Maharashtra — (2011) 1 SCC 694; AIR 2011 SC 312 🌐

Facts: anticipatory bail granted by the Sessions/HC in contested circumstances was challenged; stringent conditions and the fear of arrest despite orders pressed on the court — a review of s. 438 six decades after the CrPC.

Issue: how to balance individual liberty against public interest and investigation needs in AB matters.

Held / principle: AB must be granted judicially on facts, not mechanically; liberty-first balance: “individual freedom should not be restricted unless absolutely required”; protection ordinarily continues through trial; courts should not impose conditions that make the order hollow; no rigid formula can substitute for reasons.

Use in exam as the “post-Sibbia restatement” — Mhetre = discretion + reasons; Sibbia = foundation + offence-specificity.

Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi) — (2020) 5 SCC 1 (Constitution Bench) 🌐

Issue: must an anticipatory-bail order necessarily last until the end of trial, or can courts limit its life?

Held: no rigid rule either way — the court granting AB may limit the period of protection on reasons recorded, or leave it running until trial concludes; what is impermissible is automatic extension or automatic cut-off. Protection is offence-specific and court-shaped.

Practical drafting cue: in the prayer, say what duration you seek (“till completion of trial” / “for 90 days”) — Sushila Aggarwal makes silence expensive.

Together: Sibbia (foundation) → Mhetre (discretion) → Sushila Aggarwal (duration) — the three-judge-lineage every AB answer should name.

Object of s. 138 🌐

The cheque, as a negotiable instrument, was losing credibility through drawer irresponsibility; Ch. 4.4 — Case law for item 29 🌐 supplements (not in DU chapter)

K. Bhaskaran v. Sankaran Vaidhyan Balan — (1999) 7 SCC 510 🌐
Facts
A Kerala cheque-dishonour prosecution in which the components of the alleged offence — drawing, presentation, return unpaid, demand notice, failure to pay — straddled different territorial locations; the court had to decide where the complaint could be laid.
Issue
Whether the “cause of action” u/s 138 is a single locus or a chain of five acts — and what that does to territorial jurisdiction.
Arguments
Complainant: each act is an ingredient; competent court at any place where an ingredient occurred. Drawer: single offence must be tried at one natural place, not wherever the opponent chooses.
Decision
Five components → five possible venues (drawn / to be paid / presented / dishonoured / notice served) — complaint maintainable at any of them; components may be spread across courts’ limits.
Principle
A s. 138 “offence” is committed through a sequence, and jurisdiction follows the sequence — the generosity that produced nationwide forum-shopping and Dashrath’s correction (Fig. m6e).
Rangappa v. Sri Mohan — (2010) 11 SCC 441 🌐
Facts
Cheque given in a transactional relationship was dishonoured; in the prosecution the drawer admitted the signature but contested the underlying debt/liability and tried to keep the complainant to proof of consideration.
Issue
Scope of the s. 139 presumption: does it cover only the issuance of the cheque, or also the existence of a legally enforceable debt?
Arguments
Complainant: presumption + admitted signature = prima facie case complete; onus on drawer. Drawer: presumption confined to receipt of cheque; debt must be independently proved by complainant beyond the civil standard.
Decision
Once signature is admitted/proved and the presumption under s. 139 applies, it covers the debt/liability too; burden shifts to the accused, who must discharge it on the preponderance of probabilities using material on record.
Principle
Reverse onus with a civil-law standard — the cheque complaint is not a civil suit in disguise, but the balance tilts the moment issuance + transaction context are on record. Drafting lesson: DU’s ¶2–¶4 are precisely the “record” Rangappa expects.
Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v. State of Maharashtra — (2014) 9 SCC 129 🌐
Facts
Prosecutions under s. 138 in Maharashtra where complainants had sued in courts far from the drawer’s base, relying on Bhaskaran’s five-venue map; the drawer faced litigation in unfamiliar territory.
Issue
Bhaskaran redux: which is the proper territorial court for a s. 138 complaint — any ingredient-place, or the place tied to dishonour/the drawer?
Arguments
Payee: Bhaskaran is binding; every ingredient is an offence-site. Drawer: multiple venues = oppression; nexus with drawer/dishonour should anchor the trial.
Decision
Bench narrowed jurisdiction to the place with which dishonour/the drawer is connected — a dramatic shift away from the payee’s convenience; severe hardship flagged by commentators and Parliament.
Principle / aftermath
Jurisdiction rules in cheque cases are remedial-law choices: within a year, the NI (Amendment) Act, 2015 inserted s. 142A restoring venue around the payee’s bank branch (cheque delivered for collection) — legislative reversal of a judicial narrowing. This is why DU ¶8 has a drafting instruction, not a sentence.
Short cards — the rest of the s. 138 bench 🌐

MSR Leathers v. S. Palaniappan — AIR 2014 SC 642: second/successive dishonour prosecutions permissible so long as each satisfies the proviso; right to re-present within validity survives (KAMKUS-era doubt-buster).

Dalmia Cement (Bharat) Ltd. v. Galaxy Traders — AIR 2001 SC 676: complainant must prove the ingredients — including receipt of the notice under proviso (b); a notice sent but not shown received sinks the case; no fresh notice after inaction on the first.

Econ Antri Ltd. v. Rom Industries Ltd. — (2014) 11 SCC 769: for the 1-month clock, exclude the day the cause of action arose.

Bir Singh v. Mukesh Kumar — (2019) 4 SCC 197: signed blank cheque voluntarily handed over → holder may complete it; presumptions apply; payee’s return-non-disclosure is not per se fatal.

Kusum Ingots — ingredients framework for quash of s. 138 cases · Meters & Instruments Pvt. Ltd. v. Kanchan Mehta — (2017) 1 SCC 234: early compounding/closure where relationship permits — cue for settlement paragraphs in notices.

Time-barred debt: SC has held a criminal prosecution u/s 138 does not lie on a time-barred debt (no “legally enforceable” liability) — a standard drawer defence from the reply-notebook above.

Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum — (1985) 2 SCC 556 (1985 Cr LJ 346) 🌐
Facts
Mohd. Ahmed Khan divorced his wife Shah Bano by triple talaq after ~40 years of marriage; she was left with five children. He paid a nominal amount during iddat and no more. She moved the Magistrate under s. 125 CrPC; the trial court and High Court ordered maintenance (₹25/month raised to ₹179.20/month), the husband challenging it on Muslim Personal Law; the matter reached the Supreme Court.
Issue
Whether a divorced Muslim woman is a “wife” within s. 125 who can claim maintenance beyond iddat when she cannot maintain herself; whether s. 125 (a secular provision in the Criminal Procedure Code) can override personal-law command.
Arguments — husband
Muslim Personal Law governs; iddat maintenance is the whole obligation; a religious code sanctified by Art. 25 cannot be displaced by general criminal law; policy arguments about impact on community practices.
Arguments — State / wife
s. 125 is a provision of general application enacted for protection of destitute women; Parliament intended reach beyond religion; Art. 44’s uniform-civil-code direction urges it; personal law cannot denude a remedial statute.
Decision
Maintenance under s. 125 allowed — a divorced Muslim woman unable to maintain herself is entitled; “we must remember that the religion of the respondent is personal matter which does not defeat her right to maintenance from a husband who possesses the means.” Parliament’s response came in 1986 (Muslim Women Act) — the controversy that followed is itself part of the case’s history.
Principle
s. 125/now s. 144 BNSS is secular and remedial: poverty is the only qualifying fact; personal law, status or label cannot contract it. For DU item 30: ¶8 (“no independent source of livelihood”) is the load-bearing element — exactly Shah Bano’s battleground.
Noor Sabha Khatoon line of cases — Supreme Court, 2024 🌐

Held (per reports of the July 2024 ruling and the Noor Sabha Khatoon matter): children of Muslim parents are entitled to claim maintenance under s. 125 CrPC until they attain majority or can maintain themselves, whichever is earlier; daughters until marriage; and s. 125 relief coexists with rights claimed under the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act — the routes are cumulative, not mutually exclusive. Broader signal: a married woman (regardless of religion) may invoke s. 125 — the provision remains religion-neutral.

KAMKUS’s unit between “Complaint” and “Bail” is the Criminal Miscellaneous Petition — the vehicle for quashing, and the home of this corpus’s three cited cases. Its spine: State prosecutes (no private petition needed to convict); Art. 226 gives High Courts the writ lens, Art. 227 the supervisory lens, and s. 482 CrPC (now s. 528 BNSS) the inherent-power lens — to “prevent abuse of the process of any court or to secure the ends of justice”, exercised “only in the exceptional cases”.

R.P. Kapur v. State of Punjab — AIR 1960 SC 866 📗 cited in KAMKUS
Facts
Prosecutions in Punjab that the High Court quashed in exercise of its inherent/quashing jurisdiction; the State challenged such quashings before the Supreme Court, putting the legitimate scope of s. 561-A-era inherent powers (now s. 482 → s. 528) to the test. (The uploaded KAMKUS text carries the citation and the propositions; finer record detail sits outside the corpus.)
Issue
When can a High Court terminate criminal proceedings in its inherent power — a power always at risk of becoming a backdoor appeal?
Arguments
State: quashing interferes with investigation/prosecution policy; courts should try charges, not pleadings. Respondents: where the instrument on its face cannot produce a conviction, continuing it is abuse of process.
Decision / categories
The Court approved quashing where: (i) legal bar against institution or continuance; (ii) allegations in the FIR/complaint do not constitute the offence; (iii) no legal evidence, or evidence that manifestly fails to prove the charge.
Principle
Inherent power is a face-of-the-record doctrine: if the complaint, read as a whole, cannot make out the offence — or the proceeding is barred — the court may (and should) end it; otherwise it may not. This triad is the paragraph you cite in every criminal misc petition. 📗
Amar Chand v. Shanti Bose — AIR 1973 SC 799 📗 cited in KAMKUS

Proposition carried by KAMKUS: “Inherent power [under s. 482] should be exercised only in the exceptional cases.” — i.e., the section is a safety valve, not a second appeal; routine grievances must travel by appeal/revision.

Issue: how easily may an accused short-circuit a pending case? Answer: rarely — the party must show abuse of process or ends of justice, not mere defect of evidence (defects → revision/appeal routes).

Facts beyond the citation are not in the uploaded corpus (badge honesty). In answers, pair: Amar Chand = restraint; R.P. Kapur = the exceptions.

Mohd. Yunus v. Mohd. Mustquim — AIR 1984 SC 38, 40 📗 cited in KAMKUS

Proposition carried by KAMKUS: under Art. 227 “the High Court does not act as a court of appeal. It cannot … review or reweigh the evidence … [Supervisory jurisdiction is] limited to seeing that the inferior court … functions within limits of authority and not to correct an error of law.”

Issue: may revision/supervision be used to re-appreciate evidence and correct findings? Answer (per KAMKUS’s citation): no — supervision watches process and jurisdiction, not correctness of conclusions.

Practical: it tells a criminal misc petitioner whether to plead Art. 227 (jurisdictional/territorial abuse) or reach for s. 482 quash (triad) — wrong lens = dismissed petition.

The Complaint Masterclass Ten Commandments · minimum requirements · format a–g · cognizance & limitation doctrine

📗 KAMKUS pp. 69–72 “(I) COMPLAINT” Applies to item 29 (s.138) as its general law Cognizance: s. 190 CrPC (🌐) · ss. 200/202/204 procedure Limitation: ss. 467–473 CrPC (KAMKUS’s cite)

7.1 — Before the form: what a complaint legally is

The private-prosecution frame (KAMKUS): serious offences are investigated and prosecuted by police; but “there are certain private complaints which may be taken direct to the Metropolitan Magistrate with or without the participation of the police” — defamation and malicious-prosecution-type matters are classic private examples — and crucially: “the complainant must have reported the nature and the facts of the case to the local police and … obtained a receipt thereof which must form a part of the complaint.” 📗

Cognizance (KAMKUS’s mini-treaty): the term is undefined in the Code; literally “knowledge and notice”; it “occurs as soon as a magistrate as such applies his mind to the suspected commission of an offence” for proceeding under ss. 200/202/204 — but ordering investigation u/s 156(3) or issuing a search warrant is not cognizance. 🌐 anchor: cognizance of offences on complaint is the s. 190 route; trial of summons cases follows s. 2(d)/204 machinery — the doctrine explains why a complaint must contain facts constituting the offence, not merely an allegation: “To entitle a magistrate to take cognizance there should not [only] be a complaint … but it must contain facts which constitute the offence … the path and substance of primary facts.”

“The complaint is in the nature of an indictment. Therefore averments in a complaint must be established and properly proved by evidence. Before anyone can be convicted on charges formulated in a complaint, all those charges must be fully and properly proved in accordance with … the law of evidence.” KAMKUS, pp. 71–72 — why DU attaches affidavit + witness list: pleading standard ≠ proof standard, but the pleading frames the proof.

7.2 — The “Ten Commandments” of complaint drafting 📗

KAMKUS’s checklist (verbatim, numbered)
  1. Be brief
  2. Be positive
  3. Be precise
  4. Be relevant
  5. Plead fact and not evidence
  6. Plead fact, not law
  7. Do not plead what the law or the court takes for granted, or what the other side has got to prove
  8. Give particulars of fraud etc.
  9. Do not change your terminology; do not use fine language or words you don’t understand
  10. Do not use passive voice, periphrases, pronouns or any sort of ambiguity
How each commandment reappears in the DU four
  • 1–4: item 27’s 13 tight paras (no story-padding) vs item 29’s flat compliance digest ¶7.
  • 5 (facts not evidence): item 30 pleads “Wing Commander” + “DPS fees” (facts) — not pay-slips (evidence).
  • 6–7 (facts not law): DU leaves legal conclusions to the prayer (“liable to be prosecuted” appears only after facts are laid, ¶6).
  • 8 (particulars): item 29’s cheque no./date/amount/bank — particularity is the section.
  • 9–10 (language): consistency of party labels — “Applicant” throughout AB; “Complainant” throughout item 29.

7.3 — Minimum requirements + the formal skeleton (KAMKUS format a–g)

Law’s minimum (no prescribed form)
  1. Must be addressed to a Magistrate;
  2. Must state facts fulfilling the ingredients of the offence complained of;
  3. Must pray for action against the offender for punishment.

Everything else (verification, annexures, witness list) is practice-plus-statute — e.g. item 29’s affidavit note + witness list; s. 142 requires a written complaint by payee.

The practice skeleton (KAMKUS a–g)
a. Name of the court
b. Criminal case no. of the court
c. Name, description, age, occupation & residence of complainant
d. “Versus” / “vs”
e. Name(s) of accused with address
f. Heading showing the section(s) constituting the offence & prescribing punishment
g. Body/substance — opening options: (i) “The complainant begs to state as follows”; (ii) “on oath or solemn affirmation”; (iii) bare third person — then prayer · place/date · signature/thumb-impression · list of witnesses

Overlay for s. 138 complaints: item 29 adds jurisdiction strip (P.S. line), annexure lettering A–E, affidavit note, and a four-entry witness list — general form + statute-specific extras. KAMKUS also warns against “exaggeration” and details that “widen the scope of cross-examination”: in a cheque case, dates and amounts are ingredients — narrative colour is not.

Cognizance clock + preliminary objections (KAMKUS’s tactical advice) 📗

Limitation: cognizance only within the periods of ss. 467–473 (KAMKUS’s reference to the general clauses — short-offence 3-year bar, long offences differently treated, extension powers in s. 473). The accused “is entitled to raise an objection to the maintainability … on the ground of limitation or of jurisdiction or any other analogous ground” — and KAMKUS urges the preliminary-points-first discipline: objections going “to the very root of maintainability” should be raised and decided at the earliest, saving court time and litigant expense; those that don’t go to the root may be refused at that stage. In the s. 138 world this becomes: challenge the 1-month clock and s. 142A venue on the first day — both are root-objections.

Complaint vs Application — the confusion item 30 corrects

A s. 138 grievance becomes a complaint (KAMKUS form a–g; summons follow). A maintenance grievance becomes an application (DU item 30 caption, affidavit + witness note) — different header, different relief (“summon & punish” vs “monthly allowance”), but the same drafting DNA: jurisdiction line → facts-as-ingredients → quantified prayer → verification. KAMKUS’s Ten Commandments govern both; its format a–g governs only the first.

Bail (regular)

Release from custody of law into custody of sureties (KAMKUS). Non-bailable = discretion (s. 480) with M-A-T balancing; bailable = right (s. 478). Prayer: “during the pendency”.

Anticipatory bail

Direction for release in event of arrest, Sessions/HC only (s. 482); needs specific facts + undertakings; offence-specific; duration per court (Sushila Aggarwal). Prayer: “in the event of arrest” + directions to IO/SHO.

s. 138 complaint

Payee’s private prosecution for cheque dishonour: debt → present ≤3mo → dishonour → notice ≤30d → pay ≤15d → complaint ≤1mo (s. 142(b)); presumptions ss. 118/139; jurisdiction s. 142A; relief = summon.

Maintenance

s. 144 BNSS: sufficient means + neglect/refusal + claimant unable → monthly allowance (no ceiling) + interim + costs; disentitlements: adultery / mutual separation / refusal to cohabit; enforcement s. 147.

The one-paragraph takeaway

A criminal pleading is a statutory machine drawn in prose: the caption installs the forum, the paragraphs satisfy the ingredients and disarm the risk-factors, the annexures make the story checkable, the prayer matches the posture, and the notes (affidavit, witnesses, jail attestation) guarantee the file survives contact with the registry. DU’s four models are four configurations of the same machine — bail = risk balance; anticipatory = documented fear; s. 138 = a clock with receipts; maintenance = means vs need — and KAMKUS supplies the grammar (Ten Commandments) while the case law supplies the standards (Sibbia, Sanjay Chandra, Rangappa, Shah Bano). Build the machine correctly and the court has little left to invent.
