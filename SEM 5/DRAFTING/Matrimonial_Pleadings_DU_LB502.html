1.4 · The s. 14 gate DU flags

DU: "By virtue of Section 14 [HMA], the petition for divorce cannot be presented within one year of marriage unless leave is taken from the court… on the ground of exceptional hardship."

Section 14 (as it stands): no court shall entertain a divorce petition unless one year has elapsed since the date of the marriage. Proviso: on application made per High Court rules, leave may be granted earlier where the case is one of exceptional hardship to the petitioner or exceptional depravity of the respondent; if leave was obtained by misrepresentation/concealment, the court may either (i) pronounce a decree not to take effect until one year from the marriage, or (ii) dismiss the petition without prejudice to a fresh petition after the year on the same facts. On the leave application the court must have regard to the interests of children of the marriage and reasonable probability of reconciliation.

  • Drafting consequence 1 — check dates before drafting: petition filed at 11 months → either attach a s. 14 leave application (with particulars of exceptional hardship/depravity — often pleaded in a sealed cover to avoid insulting the other side) or hold the draft.
  • Drafting consequence 2 — s. 14 ≠ the s. 13B(1) one-year-separation requirement: courts have treated them as independent (one Delhi HC view extends the s. 14 proviso route to the 13B(1) separation in rare cases — MAT.APP.(FC) 111/2025 — while the MP HC has read s. 14 as incapable of diluting s. 13B(1)). Exam-safe answer: plead both periods honestly.
  • Drafting consequence 3 — RCR (s. 9) and JS (s. 10) petitions have no such bar; only divorce petitions do. That is a real strategic difference between Forms 14/15 and Form 16.

Petition for Restitution of Conjugal Rights under s. 9, HMA 1955
2.1 · The section — s. 9 HMA (verbatim core)

"When either the husband or the wife has, without reasonable excuse, withdrawn from the society of the other, the aggrieved party may apply, by petition to the district court, for restitution of conjugal rights and the court, on being satisfied of the truth of the statements made in such petition and that there is no legal ground why the application should not be granted, may decree restitution of conjugal rights accordingly. Explanation — Where a question arises whether there has been reasonable excuse for withdrawal from society, the burden of proving reasonable excuse shall be on the person who has withdrawn from the society."

Inserted 1955; Explanation added by the Marriage Laws (Amendment) Act, 1976 (the old clause (ii) — wife's exemption when she was in the husband's house at his pleasure — was omitted). Sister provisions: SMA s. 22 (identical wording for SMA marriages); Indian Divorce Act s. 32; Parsi Marriage & Divorce Act s. 36. Remedy's Indian origin: Moonshee Bazloor v. Shamsoonaissa Begum (1867) 10 MIA 406 (PC).

2.4 · After the decree — enforcement (most-missed exam point)
  • Execution route: O. 21 Rr. 32 & 33 CPC (via s. 21 & s. 28A HMA) — R. 32 for acts directed (return/join), R. 33 gives the executing court discretion. Detention available under s. 51 CPC, but see the proviso limits for restitution decrees.
  • Hard limit: courts consistently hold a spouse cannot be physically compelled to resume cohabitation — execution runs only to the extent the rules indicate; refusal itself matures into a fresh ground (see Rinju v. Santhosh (2018) Kerala HC: "his/her spouse cannot be compelled to restore conjugal rights… unless and until he/she is willing").
  • The strategic payoff: non-compliance for 1 year or upwards after the RCR decree = independent divorce ground — s. 13(1A)(ii) HMA. Drafters plead the earlier RCR history in Form 16's prior-proceedings table (para 8/10) precisely for this.
  • Distinguish the traps: (i) CPC s. 144 ("restitution when decree reversed or modified") ≠ s. 9 RCR — KAMKUS's interlocutory-application list means the CPC provision; (ii) s. 144 BNSS (ex-s. 125 CrPC) is criminal maintenance — not restitution at all.
  • Constitutional note (1-mark): s. 9 has survived Art. 14/21 challenges, but commentators still flag forced-cohabitation as Art. 21 tension (JURIST commentary, 2023) — mention in "current debates" answers.
2.5 · DU companion's real RCR-flavoured nugget

In the companion's s. 125 reply–rejoinder chain (full dissect in § 9), the wife's rejoinder pleads that "she… persuaded this Hon'ble Court to direct the respondent to keep her (petitioner) in his matrimonial company, but instead of honouring the same he again kicked her out" — i.e., a court order to resume matrimonial life already existed and was breached. Real files generate RCR-like directions inside maintenance litigation, and that breach then feeds cruelty/divorce pleadings. In drafting terms: always ask for the full order-history table (DU para 8) — it is the spine of every later petition.

Companion's also reproduces a full RCR model petition under "Marriage and Divorce Laws" (11 paras: marriage → she left for mother's illness → waited a fortnight → went to her parents' house, she refused → entitlement → no collusion → no delay → no impediment → "not guilty of any such act or omission which should preclude him" → jurisdiction → prayer directing her "to return and live with him as husband and wife"). Compare: KAMKUS adds the powerful para 9 "clean-hands" averment (petitioner not guilty of acts precluding relief) which DU's Form 14 leaves to para 7 — add it in exams.

Petition for Judicial Separation under s. 10, HMA 1955
3.1 · Section 10 HMA — both limbs

"(1) Either party to a marriage… may present a petition praying for a decree for judicial separation on any of the grounds specified in sub-section (1) of section 13, and in the case of a wife also on any of the grounds specified in sub-section (2) thereof, as grounds on which a petition for divorce might have been presented. (2) Where a decree for judicial separation has been passed, it shall no longer be obligatory for the petitioner to cohabit with the respondent, but the court may, on application by petition of either party… rescind the decree if it considers it just and reasonable."

Reading rule: s. 10 is a pick-up provision — every divorce ground (and the wife's special grounds) becomes a JS ground. The DU direction in para 4 ("any one or more grounds available under section 10 may be pleaded here… matrimonial offences in separate paragraphs with times and places") means you must plead the s. 13 sub-clause expressly (e.g., "the respondent has treated the petitioner with cruelty within s. 13(1)(ia) read with s. 10").

4.2 · Conditional clauses DU insists on (paras 5 & 6)
  • Para 5 (adultery ground): "The petitioner has not in any manner been accessory to or connived at or condoned the act(s) complained of." — These are the three s. 23 HMA defences; condonation = forgiveness + restoration of the offending spouse (the Aneel Kaur HC applied this against the husband because a fourth child was born during the dispute — SC later restored the divorce; § 8).
  • Para 6 (cruelty ground): "The petitioner has not in any manner condoned the cruelty." — DU mandatory statement (c). Add, in good drafts: petitioner's own clean-hands averment ("not guilty of any act or omission which should preclude relief" — the KAMKUS RCR model's para 9), because s. 23 asks whether any legal ground exists — including the petitioner's own proved cruelty.
  • Delay is a "legal ground": para 8 (no unnecessary/improper delay) exists because condonation can be implied from delay + continued cohabitation. If there are gaps in cohabitation, plead them honestly with explanations.

5.1 · Section 13B — both sub-sections (what DU's title says: "UNDER SECTION 13-B(1)")

(1) "A petition for dissolution of marriage by a decree of divorce may be presented to the District Court by both the parties to a marriage together, whether such marriage was solemnized before or after the commencement of the Marriage Laws (Amendment) Act, 1976, on the ground that they have been living separately for a period of one year or more, that they have not been able to live together and that they have mutually agreed that the marriage should be dissolved."

(2) "On the motion of both the parties made not earlier than six months after the date of presentation of the petition under sub-section (1) and not later than eighteen months after the said date, if the petition is not withdrawn in the meantime, the court shall, on being satisfied, after hearing the parties and after making such inquiry as it thinks fit, that the averments in the petition are true, pass a decree of divorce declaring the marriage to be dissolved."

A. Jayachandra v. Aneel Kaur
(2005) 2 SCC 22 · Supreme Court (also reported 2005 (1) RCR (Civil) 309; 2005 (2) LWN 149)
Ground: cruelty s. 13(1)(ia) · procedural: evidence & s. 13A

Facts: Both parties were medical doctors, co-students who married on 10-10-1978 ("love marriage" — Telugu Brahmin husband, Sikh wife); two children (a fourth child's birth during litigation became a condonation argument). They worked in the hospital run by the husband's father, Dr. A. Ram Murthy. The wife's behaviour towards the husband's family was allegedly insulting/obnoxious/humiliating; no physical contact or sharing of bed for over two years. Husband's notice dated 05-03-1997 proposed divorce by mutual consent; failing response, he filed a s. 13 HMA petition in the Family Court, Hyderabad alleging mental cruelty and desertion (two years). On 18-06-2001 the FC found cruelty proved but passed a decree of judicial separation instead of divorce, taking note of the children's welfare. Both appealed; the High Court (Division Bench) set aside even the JS decree, holding the record insufficient — no hospital witnesses examined, therefore adverse inference — and dismissed the husband's appeal while allowing the wife's.

Issues: (1) Whether the wife's conduct constituted mental cruelty under s. 13(1)(ia); (2) whether the HC was right to discard the FC's reasoned findings on the specious ground of non-examination of hospital staff; (3) whether irretrievable breakdown could itself yield divorce.

Husband's arguments: HC never analysed the evidence; FC had examined everything and found cruelty; non-examination of hospital staff cannot negate cogent material; wife's own testimony showed directives aimed at female staff exposing distrust of his fidelity; continuing hostility (injunctions, execution, caveats with wrong addresses); marriage beyond repair → divorce at least on breakdown.

Wife's arguments: No particulars of cruelty pleaded — vague misbehaviour allegations; evidence scanty (the S. Hanumantha Rao v. S. Ramani (1999) 3 SCC 620 threshold unmet); normal wear and tear till 1993; even if breakdown, breakdown is no ground; condonation — harmony restored, fourth child born 12-05-2000 (forgiveness + restoration).

Decision: HC judgment set aside as "clearly indefensible"; SC held the FC had analysed the record in detail and its cruelty finding was sound; wife's testimony and later conduct showed "indelible mental agony"; decree of divorce restored in favour of the husband. On breakdown: acknowledged in substance but the decree rested on the proved statutory ground.

Principles: (i) Cruelty = "wilful and unjustifiable conduct of such character as to cause danger to life, limb or health, bodily or mental, or as to give rise to a reasonable apprehension of such a danger"; must be grave and weighty — more than ordinary wear and tear; where the conduct is per se unlawful, impact need not be separately enquired; mental cruelty includes filthy/abusive language destroying mental peace; (ii) an appellate court must engage with the evidence, not discard findings for missing formal witnesses; (iii) condonation = forgiveness + restoration (sexual relations/childbirth during dispute can evidence it — plead non-condonation carefully, DU para 6).

Drafting lesson: Every cruelty incident needs date/place/particulars (DU para 4 direction), and para 6 non-condonation must be a positive averment — because conduct-based condonation (resuming cohabitation/childbirth) is the respondent's best defence.

Naveen Kohli v. Neelu Kohli
(2006) 4 SCC 558 · Supreme Court (judgment 21-03-2006)
Ground: mental cruelty + irretrievable breakdown

Facts: Married 20-11-1975; three sons; husband built three factories and a bungalow for the family; sons educated at a Nainital public school. Discord intensified by 1994 — parties living separately since May 1994 (10+ years). Husband alleged the wife filed a "very large number" of criminal and civil cases against him, caused police harassment, published newspaper advertisements branding him merely an "employee" of the factory (she being shown as proprietor), withdrew ₹9.5 lakhs from bank accounts, and sought his arrest repeatedly; allegations of adultery against him with one "Shiva Nagi". Husband filed divorce on cruelty (s. 13(1)(ia)); wife denied being quarrelsome/cruel, said she never refused cohabitation, alleged he lived adulterously and assaulted his own son. Family Court granted divorce + ₹5 lakh livelihood allowance (deposited). Allahabad High Court reversed, finding cruelty unproved (evidence not properly appreciated). Husband's SLP succeeded at the SC.

Issues: (1) Do the wife's litigation-terror + public disparagement amount to mental cruelty? (2) Can a court dissolve where the marriage has irretrievably broken down though breakdown is not a statutory ground?

Husband's arguments: HC ignored FC findings and misread evidence; catalogue of proceedings/advertisements/bank withdrawals = mental, physical and financial cruelty; separation since 1994 — bond beyond repair; living together impossible.

Wife's arguments: No cruelty — her complaints and advertisements were legitimate protective steps against an adulterous, violent husband; denial of refusal to cohabit; contested divorce; sought maintenance.

Decision: HC judgment set aside; SC catalogued 17+ proceedings, the newspaper advertisement, ₹9.5 lakh withdrawal, arrest attempts — cumulative effect = grave mental cruelty; marriage "broken down irretrievably… no chance of coming together"; divorce restored; permanent alimony directed (courts below's ₹5 lakh plus reports of ₹25 lakh total maintenance as carried in commentary). Lord Pearce's classic test on cruelty quoted.

Principles: (i) Cumulative harassment — repeated false litigation, public disparagement and financial coercion aggregate into mental cruelty even without physical violence; (ii) prolonged separation (10 yrs+) + mutual infidelity allegations make cohabitation impossible — the "relationship beyond repair" lens; (iii) the Court urged Parliament to amend the HMA to add irretrievable breakdown (71st Law Commission Report) — legislative-gap dialogue that later flowered in Shilpa Sailesh; (iv) mere allegations/counter-allegations or delay alone don't grant divorce — "extraordinary features" are needed.

Drafting lesson: In Form 16 para 4, tabulate the harassment chronologically (dates, case numbers, publications) — "cumulative effect" is pleaded as a running account, plus the separation-period narrative that maps onto the breakdown argument.

Samar Ghosh v. Jaya Ghosh
(2007) 4 SCC 511 · Supreme Court (26-03-2007; B.N. Agrawal, P.P. Naulekar, Dalveer Bhandari JJ)
Ground: mental cruelty · degrees of withdrawal · breakdown factors

Facts: Marriage followed by deep disagreement over "love, affection, future planning and normal human connections"; wife's alleged inhuman behaviour, refusal to look after the husband during his prolonged illness, unilateral reproductive decisions (refusal to have children / insistence on contraception measures without consent). Parties not living together at least since 27-08-1990 — 16+ years of separation by trial. Husband filed for divorce; trial court granted; High Court reversed; Supreme Court restored the decree.

Issues: (1) Whether the wife's conduct constituted mental cruelty under s. 13(1)(ia); (2) how courts should approach a marriage admittedly dead for 16 years when "irretrievable breakdown" is not in the statute.

Husband's arguments: Refusal of care during illness, denial of children, unilateral decisions = conduct making cohabitation impossible; separation since 1990 — no emotions left; HC erred in reverting to formalism.

Wife's arguments: (as recorded) no deliberate cruelty; ordinary marital friction; no statutory breakdown ground; divorce cannot follow merely from separation length.

Decision: Divorce restored. The Court held: "parties living independently of each other for an adequate period and filing of petition conveys the assumption that marriage has broken down… impossible to preserve or save the marriage"; surveyed the English Divorce Reform Act 1969, Matrimonial Causes Act 1973, Law Commission's 71st Report (7-4-1978) (fault vs breakdown theories) and Indian precedent (Chetan Dass, Parveen Mehta), and stated factors for examining irretrievable breakdown — commonly listed as: (a) length of separation; (b) efforts at reconciliation (mediation/counselling failed); (c) criminal/other proceedings between spouses; (d) allegations in pleadings admitting no togetherness; (e) no possibility of reunion.

Principles: (i) Mental cruelty definition adopted: conduct inflicting mental pain making it "not possible to live together" — of such a nature that parties cannot reasonably be expected to live together; intention is not essential; (ii) the Court articulated degrees of withdrawal from society (A–E spectrum) — from withdrawal within the same home, to withdrawal of consortium, to physical separation, to withdrawal with intention to erode the marriage — used to locate each case; (iii) long separation + failed reconciliation + pleadings = breakdown proof-points that support cruelty findings; (iv) fault theory vs breakdown theory mapped for reform.

Drafting lesson: For JS/divorce on cruelty+desertion, plead the degree of withdrawal expressly (which rung of the A–E ladder the respondent left at) and the reconciliation-failure trail (notices, mediation, counselling) — those are the exact factors courts inventory.

Sureshta Devi v. Om Prakash
(1991) 3 SCC 251 · Supreme Court
Ground: s. 13B consent persistence

Facts: Marriage broke down; a petition for dissolution by mutual consent under s. 13B was presented in which the wife was a signatory/consenting party. By the time the court was moved to pass the decree, the wife declined to persist — asserting she had not freely consented / wished not to proceed (her position hardened that the mutual route was not to be pressed). The husband contended that once consent was on record, the court could proceed regardless of the later posture.

Issue: Whether a decree under s. 13B can be pronounced when one party's consent does not subsist at the stage of the decree — is consent a one-time input or a continuous requirement?

Husband's arguments: Consent was given when the petition was filed; the statutory scheme once activated should run to decree; withdrawal at the last stage defeats the legislative purpose of quick no-fault exits.

Wife's arguments: Mutual consent is the bedrock of s. 13B; a decree without her continuing agreement would violate the section itself; she is entitled to refuse throughout.

Decision: No decree could be passed. Consent must exist at both stages — presentation and decree; a decree on withdrawn consent would be without jurisdiction in substance. The Court also examined whether the parties were "living separately" as required and laid the groundwork for the two-motion consent doctrine.

Principles: (i) Matrimonial consent is continuous — it can be withdrawn until the decree; (ii) s. 13B is not a procedural formality but the jurisdictional foundation; (iii) institutional caution: courts must independently satisfy themselves that consent is free — DU's para 6 (force/fraud/undue influence) and separate Form 18 affidavits operationalise exactly this.

Drafting lesson: In 13B work, never treat the second motion as routine — re-verify consent, refresh affidavits, and if consent lapses, pivot the client to a s. 13 contested petition (or Art. 142 route per Shilpa Sailesh when breakdown + fairness are clear).

Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur
(2017) 8 SCC 746 · Supreme Court (12-09-2017)
Ground: waiver of s. 13B(2) cooling-off

Facts: The couple (with children) had been living apart for years — reported ~8 years — and filed a joint s. 13B petition before a Delhi Family Court (filed 08-05-2017), with all incidental matters (custody, alimony, dowry claims) settled and consent decreed in essence. Because s. 13B(1) itself required one year's separation plus the six-month gap, the parties moved to waive the six-month waiting period so the second motion could be taken up at once. The question reached the Supreme Court (reference on whether the period is mandatory or directory).

Issue: Is the six-month cooling-off under s. 13B(2) mandatory, or directory/waivable by the court?

Parties' arguments: Petitioner husband (and effectively both): marriage dead for 8 years — waiting period only prolongs agony; settlement complete; no chance of reconciliation. Respondent's position: no opposition to divorce as such (the dispute was on timing/mechanics).

Decision: Held: the six-month period is directory, not mandatory. The Court framed waiver conditions: (1) separation period under s. 13B(1) already undergone before first motion (1 year + 6 months often already spent apart); (2) attempts at mediation/reconciliation have failed and chances nil; (3) all incidental matters settled (alimony, custody, etc.). The Court added that the Court should ascertain a genuine settlement free consent; s. 13B imposes no fetters on the court's power to dissolve; the period's purpose (reconsideration opportunity) is exhausted where the marriage is beyond repair. Art. 142 noted as the upper-level safety valve.

Principles: (i) Cooling-off is a reconciliation opportunity, not a jurisdictional incantation — waiveable where its object is spent; (ii) the deeper requirement is voluntary, informed, settled consent; (iii) courts must not "stretch an already disintegrated marriage".

Drafting lesson: Attach to a waiver application: separation-date computation sheet, failed-mediation/counselling note, and a signed settlement sheet — the three Amardeep conditions in exhibit form. This is exactly what the DU 2017 Q3 answer must show (§ 11).

Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan
Constitution Bench, 01-05-2023 · Transfer Petition (Civil) No. 1118 of 2014 (S.K. Kaul, Sanjiv Khanna, Abhay S. Oka, Vikram Nath, J.K. Maheshwari JJ) · reported (2023) 9 SCC 371
Ground: Art. 142 — irretrievable breakdown as independent dissolution route

Facts: The parties had moved the Supreme Court (via transfer petition) seeking divorce by mutual consent with waiver of statutory periods — one party in Canada, on medication for depression; husband complained of loneliness and absence of cohabitation amounting to mental strain; efforts by counsellors, mediators, family/panchayat and even courts failed; consent posture fluctuated (initial mutual-consent stance, later opposition). The matter was referred from a 2-judge bench (June 2016) to the 5-judge Constitution Bench; delivered 01-05-2023.

Issues: (1) Contours of Art. 142(1) ("complete justice") in matrimonial matters; (2) whether the SC can itself dissolve a marriage on irretrievable breakdown even where (a) s. 13B conditions/periods are not fully met and (b) one party's consent is wanting; (3) whether s. 13B(2) waiting period can be dispensed.

Arguments — those seeking dissolution: marriage "totally unworkable, emotionally dead, beyond salvation"; years of failed reconciliation; statutory fault framework leaves gap cases unrelievable; Art. 142 exists precisely to fill such gaps; financial interests of the opposing spouse can be protected within the same order. Arguments against free-standing Art. 142 divorce: (court's own concern, also from earlier stage) breakdown is not a parliamentary ground; Art. 142 cannot override legislative policy wholesale; consent-based dissolution must not become unilateral divorce by judicial fiat; safeguards for the vulnerable spouse are essential.

Decision (unanimous): The SC can grant divorce on irretrievable breakdown under Art. 142(1) where the marriage has completely failed and continuation of the formal relationship is unjustified — doing complete justice, with fault-theory dilution allowed in "rare and exceptional" cases; the six-month/s. 13B machinery may be bypassed; but the power is discretion, not right — parties must ordinarily first approach the Family Court; the Court listed factors: length of cohabitation, nature/seriousness of allegations, attempts at reconciliation, period of separation (long separation — 6+ years — a strong indicator), and economic security of the wife/partner must be ensured (grave financial hardship can defeat the relief); connected proceedings (incl. BNS/498A-type) may be quashed in the same order.

Principles: (i) Irretrievable breakdown is now a judicially recognised dissolution route (via Art. 142, not via s. 13) — the biggest doctrinal shift since 1976; (ii) constitutional justice supplements but respects statutory schemes (restraint + deference to legislature); (iii) protection-not-punishment orientation — financial welfare woven into the decree; (iv) forum discipline — FC first.

Drafting lesson: Even a s. 13B draft should carry breakdown-proof facts (separation length, failed reconciliation, settlement) — they serve (a) Amardeep waiver, (b) Art. 142 fallback, (c) s. 13 cruelty corroboration. Plead them once, use them thrice.

Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra
(2002) 4 SCC 388 · AIR 2002 SC 177 · 5-judge Constitution Bench, 10-04-2002 (B.N. Kirpal CJI, G.B. Pattanaik, H.K. Sema, S.N. Variava, Brijesh Kumar JJ) — CITED IN DU COMPANION'S CURATIVE DRAFT
Matrimonial dispute · birth of the curative petition

Facts: The parties married per Hindu rites at Ahmedabad; differences from early on — wife left the matrimonial home in 1983; mutual-consent divorce proceedings followed; in 1986 the wife withdrew her consent and prayed for dismissal, while the husband maintained consent could not be recalled. Litigation wound upward: a Gujarat HC single-judge order of 17-02-1995 (in the husband's application for the decree) was carried up; the Supreme Court upheld the divorce decree (reportedly in 1997); the wife's review petition under Art. 137 was dismissed. She then moved under Art. 32 alleging gross miscarriage of justice (procedural irregularities/non-consideration of fraud material; also that she was not adequately heard). A 3-judge bench framed constitutional questions and referred to a 5-judge bench.

Issue: After review is dismissed, does any remedy remain against a final SC judgment — and if so, on what conditions?

Wife's (applicant's) arguments: Finality must not immunise gross injustice; natural justice was violated (notice/hearing defects); court's inherent power under Art. 142 should permit reopening in such rare cases; she proposed a remedy structure.

Husband's arguments: Consent decree/judgment is final; review was already refused; unlimited reopening would destroy res judicata and flood the court.

Decision: The petition's facts did not warrant reopening (dismissed), but the Court laid down the curative jurisdiction: to prevent abuse of process and cure gross miscarriage of justice, SC may reconsider its judgment in exercise of inherent power — via a "curative petition": (1) ground = violation of principles of natural justice (no notice/not heard; judge's undisclosed connection raising reasonable apprehension of bias) affecting the petitioner; (2) grounds must have been taken in the review petition dismissed by circulation; (3) certification by a Senior Advocate of fulfilment of requirements; (4) circulated first to the three senior-most judges + judges who passed the impugned order, if available; rehearing only if a majority agrees; (5) no time limit, but no merits reopens except to cure the stated defect.

Principles: (i) Finality yields to natural justice at the extreme margin — a fourth-instance cure exists but is deliberately narrow; (ii) the dissent-vs-relief balance: jurisdiction announced even though this petition failed; (iii) matrimonial connection matters for DU: the entire architecture emerged from a divorce-consent withdrawal fight — the same policy thread as Sureshta Devi (consent persistence) running through procedural law.

Drafting lesson: The DU companion's curative proforma (questions on irretrievable breakdown, res judicata, desertion proof, refusal of divorce where both sides' advocates agreed) shows how matrimonial merits get repackaged as curative questions — and requires the Senior Advocate certificate + Rupa averments verbatim (grounds already taken in review; dismissed by circulation). Use it as the model when asked "draft a curative petition" (a recurring DU-family question).

Mohinder Kaur v. Major Singh
AIR 1972 P & H 184 (Division Bench: Pandit & Gopal Singh JJ) — cited in DU law-faculty syllabus material
Defence limits in RCR

Facts: Parties married (per the Act); in the husband's RCR petition, the wife sought to resist restitution by pointing to a contravention of s. 5(iii) HMA (marriage within the prohibited degree/sapinda-type condition — as noted in the DU syllabus extract, the contravention concerned a s. 5 condition).

Issue: Can breach of a s. 5 condition be pleaded as a defence to an RCR petition when it is not itself a ground for JS, nullity or divorce in the pleaded form?

Arguments: Wife — marriage contravenes s. 5, so no conjugal obligation can be enforced; Husband — grounds for judicial separation, nullity and divorce are exhaustively given by ss. 10, 11 and 13; a defence outside those provisions cannot defeat s. 9.

Decision: Held: the marriage in contravention is not a nullity in the manner pleaded; contravention cannot be used as a defence to the RCR petition because the grounds are those enumerated in ss. 10, 11, 13 — "if it is not [a ground for those], then it cannot be pleaded in defence" to s. 9.

Principles: (i) The HMA's remedies are statutorily enumerated — creative defences outside ss. 9–13's architecture fail; (ii) s. 5 breaches feed specific routes (s. 11 void/s. 12 voidable/s. 18 punishment), not free-floating equities; (iii) for RCR specifically, the defence universe is essentially "reasonable excuse" (proven on the respondent's burden) + s. 23's legal grounds.

Drafting lesson: For a client resisting RCR: build the reply around documented reasonable excuse (cruelty, adultery, forced-immobility demands, dowry persecution) — with particulars — rather than abstract illegality-of-marriage pleas; and counterclaim what the Act does allow (s. 23A relief, divorce cross-claim where facts justify).

The material-facts doctrine — as it bites in matrimonial pleadings
10.1 · The master rule (KAMKUS, prescribed book — reproduced faithfully)

"In a matrimonial petition, it is not enough to state that the respondent is guilty of cruelty towards the petitioner-wife and that she is entitled to divorce. The petitioner must state all those facts which establish cruelty on the part of the respondent." — then KAMKUS lists what those facts look like: husband drunkard coming home intoxicated and inflicting injuries with dates specified; abuse/beating in presence of friends and relations; not allowed to visit parents after marriage; forcing parting with dowry with threats of beating; "immediately after her marriage till date the respondent did not even talk to her nor he cohabited with her". "It is such facts which can establish physical or mental cruelty."

KAMKUS's contra-indicator — Example I of "Bad Pleading": "The respondent has deserted the petitioner for a statutory period of one year and above, and hence the petitioner is entitled to divorce. Here the legal inferences have been pleaded." Fix: plead the leaving facts, the date, the declaration of intent (animus), the refusal to return — then the statute computes the period for itself.

Also from KAMKUS: "In matrimonial matters custom has been recognised… [unless the custom] has repeatedly received judicial recognition, [it] must be pleaded" — i.e., any custom-based plea (or its absence) belongs in the pleading itself. And: never jump to legal conclusions — "the inference of law… should be left to the court"; DU repeats this in Form 16's para 4 direction ("facts… as distinctly as the nature of the case permits").

  • Conclusion-paragraphs — "the respondent is guilty of cruelty, hence I am entitled to divorce" (KAMKUS Example I). Zero marks for pure inference.
  • Offences not separated — DU twice insists: matrimonial offences in separate paragraphs with time and place. One paragraph = one ground = one incident-cluster.
  • Missing mandatory statements — collusion, non-condonation (cruelty/adultery), delay, legal ground, prior-proceedings, status grid, children. These are DU's (a)–(f): answer them in order.

DU · 2017 · Q3 Draft a petition for dissolution of marriage by a decree of divorce by mutual consent under s. 13B(1), HMA 1955, along with the affidavits.

1 · Cause title — "IN THE COURT OF PRINCIPAL JUDGE, FAMILY COURT (DISTRICT ___), ___ COURT, DELHI · HMA PETITION NO. ___ OF 20___ · IN THE MATTER OF: X … PETITIONER NO. 1 AND Y … PETITIONER NO. 2 · JOINT PETITION FOR DISSOLUTION OF MARRIAGE BY A DECREE OF DIVORCE BY MUTUAL CONSENT UNDER SECTION 13B(1) OF THE HINDU MARRIAGE ACT, 1955".

2 · Body — the 11 DU paragraphs in order: (1) marriage per Hindu rites on date at place + register extract + attested affidavit; (2) status/age/residence grid (before marriage & at filing); (3) last-resided-together place + children's particulars; (4) living separately since ___ and not able to live together since then + brief reasons (+ settlement mention); (5) mutual agreement to dissolve; (6) consent not obtained by force, fraud or undue influence; (7) not in collusion; (8) no undue delay; (9) no other legal ground; (10) jurisdiction — plead how (solemnisation/last cohabitation residence, s. 19); (11) court-fee affixed.

3 · Prayer — decree of divorce on mutual consent dissolving P1–P2 marriage + residuary relief · Signatures of BOTH petitioners through counsel · Verification signed by both.

4 · Affidavits — "Separate affidavits of Petitioner No. 1 and Petitioner No. 2" (DU's note): Form 18 skeleton ×2 — solemn affirmation; competence; marriage; no collusion; no delay; consent free of force/fraud; incorporation of petition contents read-over clause naming s. 13B(1); verification "true… nothing material concealed".

5 · Bonus (separately awarded in DU marking) — one-line note that under s. 13B(2) the second motion follows after 6 months (waivable per Amardeep Singh conditions); terms sheet (custody, alimony, stridhan, pending cases) annexed.

KAMKUS · Q.29 Draft a petition on behalf of the wife for judicial separation on the ground of desertion under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955.
Model answer (built on DU Form 15):

Title: "PETITION FOR JUDICIAL SEPARATION UNDER SECTION 10 OF THE HINDU MARRIAGE ACT, 1955" · Court: Principal Judge, Family Court · Wife = petitioner.

Paras: 1 — marriage (Hindu rites, date/place, registration, extract + affidavit); 2 — status grid (her status/age/residence before & at filing); 3 — children; 4 — grounds, in separate paragraphs: "the respondent has deserted the petitioner for a continuous period of not less than two years immediately preceding this petition, within s. 13(1)(ib) read with s. 10" + facts per paragraph: (4.1) he left the matrimonial home on ___ from ___; (4.2) statements to neighbours/relatives that he would not return (animus deserendi — KAMKUS material-facts instance); (4.3) demands to rejoin on dates → refusals; (4.4) without reasonable cause / without her consent — no cruelty, no adultery by her; (4.5) computing the continuous two years; — 5/6: non-accessory/non-condonation (where grounds touch s. 13(1)(i)/(ia)); 7 — no collusion; 8 — no delay; 9 — no other legal ground; 10 — prior-proceedings table (if any); 11 — venue facts (s. 19); 12 — jurisdiction; 13 — court-fee.

Prayer: decree of judicial separation under s. 10 + residuary. Verification (knowledge/information split) + note: petitioner's affidavit appended.

Do-nots: don't write "deserted for one year" (divorce needs 2 years under s. 13(1)(ib); JS ground imports the same clause — plead the continuous period honestly); don't fold multiple grounds into one paragraph; don't skip the status grid.
