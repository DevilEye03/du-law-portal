Why are these three grouped as “Other”?

They are the petitions that do not fit the other tracks: item 31 is a quasi-judicial tribunal complaint (consumer commission, not a civil court — a KAMKUS “special enactment” petition); item 32 is a petition to enforce a court’s own order (the court as its own enforcer — Arts. 129/215); item 33 is a statutory application with five inbuilt remedies (magistrate, hybrid civil-criminal machine). Between them they exercise three different drafting muscles: claim-quantification (31), record-defiance (32) and relief-menu (33) — which is exactly how §6 dissects them.

2.1 — The statute the complaint drives (CPA 2019 in one deck) 🌐 verified

The offence-words you must plead (definitions)
  • Consumer — s. 2(7): buys goods / hires services for consideration, incl. user with approval; excludes goods/services for resale or commercial purpose; Explanation expressly covers e-commerce, teleshopping, direct selling, MLM.
  • Defect — s. 2(10): fault/imperfection/shortfall in quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard (goods).
  • Deficiency — s. 2(11): fault/shortcoming/inadequacy in the quality, nature and manner of performance of a service — includes negligence/omission causing loss or injury and deliberate withholding of relevant information.
  • Service — s. 2(42): “service of any description … banking, financing, insurance, transport, processing, supply of electrical or other energy, telecom, boarding or lodging, housing construction, entertainment …” — but not service free of charge or under a contract of personal service (ICSI flags this exclusion too 📗).
  • Goods — s. 2(21) · Unfair contract — s. 2(46) · unfair trade practice / restrictive trade practice defined nearby.

DU ¶1 (“subscriber of telephone”) → consumer + telecom service; DU ¶7 (“dereliction of duty and negligence … loss and injury”) → deficiency language lifted from s. 2(11) itself. Drafting rule: plead the statutory adjective, not just the grievance.

The machinery around the complaint
  • Three tiers: District Commission (s. 34 jurisdiction) · State Commission (s. 47) · National Commission (s. 58) — plus appeals: DC → State 45 days (s. 41) → National 30 days → SC 90 days (s. 67).
  • Limitation — s. 69: complaint must be filed within 2 years of cause of action; delay condonable only with recorded reasons.
  • Manner — s. 35: complaint by the consumer (or via agent/recognised consumer org etc.), fee as prescribed, electronic filing enabled (E-Daakhil portal — live in 544 commissions per PIB).
  • Procedure: s. 36 (proceedings before DC) → s. 38 (on admission) → s. 39 (findings) → s. 40 (review in certain cases) → s. 41 (appeal).
  • Speed & settlement: endeavour = dispose in 3 months (no testing) / 5 months (testing) [PIB]; mediation by consent — s. 80; enforcement of orders — s. 71.
  • Around the Act: Central Consumer Protection Authority (misleading ads, product-liability class action — Rules 2020); product liability Ch. VI (ss. 84–93); consumer rights: safety · information · choice · to be heard · redressal · education.

Two teaching tensions inside DU’s own text

(1) 60 vs 69 days: ¶11 says the system remained disputed for “60 days”, while ¶9 compensates for “69 days” of paralysis (and ¶2–¶4’s 24-days fault + repeated outages feed it). A drafter must reconcile the headline number with the computation — plead the calendar: outage dates → total days = 69 (say so), and keep ¶11’s “cause of action arose on ___” as a date, not a day-count. (2) “Forum” vs “Commission”: the caption says Commission; ¶12 and prayer say “Forum” (1986 habit). Fix nomenclature throughout — mixed labels are exactly what d1’s consistency rule forbids.

DU literally leaves “Section…. of the Act” blank. 2.3 — Case law for item 31 🌐 supplement (not in DU chapter)

Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. Gupta — (1994) 2 SCC 1 (decided 1993; R.M. Sahai J.) 🌐
Facts
M.K. Gupta paid the full price and completed all formalities for an LDA flat; possession was not handed over. He complained before the District Forum. LDA’s jurisdiction plea — that consumer redressal authorities cannot touch “construction activity” — was rejected; the Forum directed possession. On appeal, the State Commission added interest on the deposit and ordered LDA to hand over after construction or pay the estimated cost of deficient/incomplete construction. The National Commission overruled LDA’s jurisdiction plea, dismissed its appeal, and awarded ₹10,000 compensation for harassment, mental torture and agony. LDA finally reached the Supreme Court.
Issues
(1) Are statutory authorities like LDA/DDA/BDAs constituted under State Acts for planned development amenable to the Consumer Protection Act — is housing construction by them a “service”? (2) Can the functionaries of a statutory body, acting capriciously, be made to compensate allottees personally?
Arguments — LDA
Construction activity is outside the commissions’ jurisdiction; statutory planning bodies are not service providers in the commercial sense; findings of the State/National Commissions should not be sustained.
Arguments — Gupta / consumer side
Full payment + formalities done, possession denied → denial of facility/benefit = deficiency; the Act is social-welfare legislation to be read generously; jurisdiction cannot be ousted because the “service” relates to immovable property.
Decision
Appeals dismissed. Housing construction/allotment by a statutory body = “service” under the Act; substandard material or misleading representation about the house = denial of facility + harassment. LDA must fix responsibility of the erring officers; the ₹10,000 award recovered from them proportionately from salaries, plus ₹5,000 to the consumer. All services are covered except those free or under personal-service contracts. Court’s acid line: “How ironical it is that official or semi-official bodies … claim exclusion when it comes to accountability from [the] operation of the Act.”
Principle
Public or private, if it charges you, it serves you. Statutory bodies enjoy no immunity; compensation for harassment/mental agony is a normal head (exactly DU ¶7/¶9 heads (a)-(b)); and the officers behind the institution can be made to pay — the accountability spine of every deficiency complaint filed after 1994. Map to DU: ¶13’s “duty cast upon the District Manager” is Gupta’s duty-talk in pleading form.
Short cards to bolt onto a consumer answer 🌐

Punjab Urban Planning / HUDA line: pure sale-and-purchase transactions or “no-interest” allotment conditions can defeat “deficiency” — know the boundary: not every bad deal is a deficiency (HUDA-type conditions accepted by applicants bind).

Telecom/PSU-service line: PSU utilities (railways, electricity, BSNL) are service providers post-1993 amendment and Gupta — DU’s own facts (government telecom) sit on this authority.

Practice trio for the answer: jurisdiction (Gupta) · limitation (s. 69 + condonation) · quantum (itemised heads + harassment compensation) — DU’s ¶9, ¶11, ¶12 respectively.

3.1 — The law of contempt, assembled (every section you can be asked) 🌐 verified

Constitutional frame

Art. 129 — Supreme Court as a court of record with power to punish for contempt; Art. 215 — High Courts equally “courts of record” with such power. The 1971 Act defines and limits that power (its stated purpose on enactment, 24-12-1971, replacing the 1952 Act) — s. 22: the Act is “in addition to, and not in derogation of, other laws relating to contempt”. ICSI’s framework note 📗: “All the High Courts have the power to pronounce punishment for contempt of court” — and dis-obedience of a writ (e.g. habeas) itself “attracts punishment for contempt under the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971”.

The two species — s. 2 (verbatim spine)

s. 2(a) — Civil contempt: “wilful disobedience to any judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or other process of a court or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court.”

s. 2(b) — Criminal contempt: publication (words/written/visible representation) that “scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court; or prejudices, or interferes or tends to interfere with, the due course of any judicial proceeding; or interferes or tends to interfere with, or obstructs or tends to obstruct, the administration of justice in any other manner.”

DU item 32 cites both ss. 11 and 12: the facts sound in s. 11 (defiance of status-quo), but because the demolition also “obstructs the administration of justice” in the pending writ, practitioners plead the pair — as DU does.

3.3 — Case law for item 32 🌐 supplements (not in DU chapter)

In re: Prashant Bhushan — contempt of the Supreme Court (2020) 🌐
Facts
The Supreme Court took suo motu cognizance of two June-2020 tweets by senior advocate Prashant Bhushan: one on the role of the last four Chief Justices (“the project of destroying democracy”), another showing the then CJI on a motorcycle during lockdown while citizens faced hearings. A three-Judge Bench (Justices Arun Mishra, B.R. Gavai, Krishna Murari) heard the matter; Bhushan filed a 134-page affidavit explaining intent and moved to recall the notice. On 14 August 2020 he was held guilty of criminal contempt; sentencing followed on 20 August.
Issues
(1) Do the tweets fall within s. 2(b) — scandalising/lowering the authority of the Court? (2) Is “fair criticism” (s. 5) or opinion a defence? (3) What punishment, given the apology/constitutional backdrop?
Arguments — Bhushan
Tweets = protected opinion/comment under Art. 19(1)(a); no obstruction of justice resulted; 134-page affidavit explained bona fides; earlier judicial statements (2018 press conference by judges) went further with impunity; the “scandalising” head should be read narrow/down.
Arguments — Court (then majority)
Attack on judges qua judges adversely affects administration of justice and “undermines public confidence in the judiciary”; citing Baradakanta Mishra (1974): scandalising is a species of contempt, its common form being vilification of the judge — test = vilification of judge as judge (court’s power) vs as individual (private remedies only). Fair criticism exists, but only if “made in good faith in public interest”; these were “malicious, scurrilous, scandalous”.
Decision
Guilty of criminal contempt. Sentence: symbolic fine of Re. 1, with default of 3 months’ imprisonment and 3-year debarment from practice; fine paid. Review/constitutional challenge landscape + massive public debate followed; the Law Commission had earlier been tasked (2018) with re-examining s. 2’s scandalising head; the UK abolished scandalising in 2013 (Crime and Courts Act) — the comparative note every answer should carry.
Principle
Scandalising remains cognisable in India; fair, good-faith, public-interest criticism of decided matters is protected (ss. 5 + 13(b)), but social-media vilification of the institution qua institution is not; punishment is discretionary and can be symbolic — apology (s. 12 proviso) must be genuine to discharge. For DU item 32: Bhushan supplies the criminal-contempt pole; the DDA petition supplies the civil-contempt pole — plead the right one first.
Baradakanta Mishra v. The Advocate General (1974) — the doctrine Bhushan ran on 🌐

Held (as summarised in Bhushan): scandalising the court is a species of criminal contempt; its common form is vilification of the judge. The dividing line: is the vilification of the judge as a judge (contempt — public confidence in administration of justice harmed) or of the judge as an individual (no contempt — private remedies: defamation etc.)?

Principle: contempt law protects the office’s authority, not the office-holder’s vanity — a sentence you can hang any criminal-contempt discussion on.

Context cards 🌐

Validity (1969): the Supreme Court dismissed the constitutional challenge to the Contempt of Courts Act — statute held substantively valid (Arts. 129/215 backbone).

2006 Amendment: truth — if bona fide and in public interest — became a defence (s. 13(b)); the Bhushan bench’s refusal to admit that defence on the facts became the controversy.

2018 Law Commission reference: government asked the Commission to re-examine the s. 2 definition — specifically whether “scandalising” should survive at all (reform horizon for exam “comment” answers).

International contrast: UK abolished scandalising contempt (Crime and Courts Act, 2013) — one line of comparative depth.

The respondent-definition flip ⟲ → 🌐

4.3 — Case law for item 33 🌐 supplements (not in DU chapter)

Hiral P. Harsora & Ors. v. State of Maharashtra (2016) 9 SCC 1 (three-Judge Bench, 30 Sept 2016) 🌐
Facts
Petitions (including by Harsora) attacked PWDVA’s s. 2(o) definition — “respondent” limited to the “adult male person” living in a shared household — as discriminatory. Family Court/Magistrate practice had begun issuing notices to adult female relatives too.
Issue
Is the “adult male” limitation in s. 2(o) (read with the Act’s premises) violative of Arts. 14 and 15 of the Constitution?
Arguments
Petitioners: excluding women respondents from the definition is sex-classification without intelligible differentia; daughters-in-law can be equally abusive; the object (protecting from domestic violence) is defeated by a definition that shields female abusers. State/opposing: the class of women in households needs protection — legislative balancing; the definition is purposive.
Decision
The words “adult male” in s. 2(o) were struck down as violative of Arts. 14 & 15 — judgment of Misra C.J. with concurring judgments of Banumathi & Amitava Roy JJ.; a Constitution Bench was to resolve the remaining questions (“shared household” — s. 2(s) — left un-defined, with interim directions to keep the machinery running). Post-judgment the respondent can be any person — male or female, adult or minor.
Principle
PWDVA reliefs are gender-neutral on the respondent side once violence is established; pleading practice must stop at “adult male” — but DU/companion’s printed respondents show the transitional drafting era. For exams: quote the section, then cite Harsora as the current position.
S.R. Batra v. Taruna Batra (2007) 3 SCC 353 🌐
Facts
Married daughter-in-law, Taruna, living with husband (and child) in a flat owned by her husband’s father, S.R. Batra — father-in-law sued/defended claiming exclusive ownership; wife claimed residence under s. 19 PWDVA.
Issue
Does a woman living with her husband in the husband’s self-acquired/shared-property flat (owned by the father-in-law) have a right to reside under s. 19 PWDVA — does “shared household” mean a household where she has a share?
Arguments
Wife: s. 17 residence right (not evicted/disturbed) + shared-household status; Father-in-law: no joint family, he is absolute owner, no share of the wife in the property, daughter-in-law cannot claim residence by mere marriage.
Decision
SC allowed the father-in-law’s appeal: the wife had no independent right to reside in the father-in-law’s house when she could live with her husband — s. 19(1) directs relief where she is “entitled to reside” in a shared household; mere relationship insufficient absent a share/ownership. 🌐
Principle
s. 19 residence ≠ ownership; entitlement matters. But note: s. 19(1)(f) allows the magistrate to direct alternative accommodation — the escape valve DU’s relief (II) wisely pleads (“right to residence in the shared household … and no eviction … or any other order”). Draft the residence prayer with the alternative-accommodation fallback — Batra teaches why.
Short cards + quick hits 🌐

V.D. Bhanot v. Savitri Bhanot (2012): the Court distinguished “injunction” from the Act’s orders: under PWDVA the magistrate may pass an order under s. 18 even absent a formal application where the court perceives the need — non-application is no bar; orders protecting against domestic violence can be suo motu-flavoured. Practical stamp: courts may act beyond the four corners of the prayer (so DU’s omnibus prayer matters).

Karishma Sharma v. Sumit Sharma (2019): even without a family relationship, a live-in partner can invoke PWDVA (s. 2(f) “respondent” via s. 2(q) dictionary — relationship in the nature of marriage). 🌐

Indra Sarma v. V.K.V. Sarma (2013): live-in outside “relationship in the nature of marriage” (long separation, existing spouse of partner) → PWDVA relief refused — boundary case.

Virender Kumar v. Rahul Bansal (2019): no restriction on relief under PWDVA even where an FIR/NDPS matter is pending — the Act runs independently.

Hema v. Ravi Prakash (2020): live-in partner living apart = relationship continues for PWDVA (s. 2(f)) until legal termination.

6.1 — Six techniques DU quietly teaches

1 · The statutory-adjective rule

DU ¶7 borrows s. 2(11)’s own words (“dereliction of duty and negligence … loss and injury”); ¶13 of item 33 says the conduct “clearly fall within … PWDVA”. Never describe wrongs in loose adjectives when the statute supplies the exact word — the magistrates read for classification, not prose.

2 · The twin-party array (item 31)

Officer who neglected the line (OP-1) + officer who forced the double payment (OP-2). Service deficiencies have institutional faces — array every functionary whose act composes the deficiency, exactly as contempt arrays R-5/6/7 by name.

3 · The demand-notice paragraph

Item 31 ¶8 (registered-post demand, no reply) and KAMKUS’s lawyer’s notice (acknowledged 22.06.1994) are not jurisdictional — they are merits amplifiers: they prove the other side knew, ignored, and thereby made litigation necessary (costs head + delay-condonation shield + mens rea flavour for contempt’s knowledge paragraph).

4 · Annexure chains as narrative

Item 32 runs A-1→A-3 (orders) → A-4 (plan, red-marked plots) → A-5 (+ English translation — multilingual evidence discipline!) → A-6 (aftermath). Each annexure is cited in its own paragraph; the reader’s proof path is linear. Consumer docs (¶10) and DV affidavits follow the same discipline.

5 · The inside-the-caption bracket

Item 31 ¶12’s “Section…. of the Act” and its “below ₹___” bracket; item 33’s relief figures — DU prints hidden instruction brackets (d1’s zone-of-hesitation concept in miniature). Strategy: fill the bracket and write the sentence that explains it (s. 34 + Rules 2021 = Fig. m7b).

Two-stage pattern across all three: qualitative ¶s first (¶1–8), then the number paragraph (item 31 ¶9 heads × 69 days; companion’s ₹15,000 + ₹10,000). Never open with money — open with duty, chronology, orders; money is arithmetic applied to accepted facts.

MODEL ANSWER 4 — Frame F4 (theory): “Civil vs criminal contempt; punishments; defences; limitation.”

Model answer: (1) Definitions (s. 2): civil = wilful disobedience of judgment/decree/direction/order/writ/process or wilful breach of undertaking (s. 2(a)); criminal = publication or act scandalising/lowering authority, prejudicing proceedings, or obstructing administration of justice (s. 2(b)). (2) Constitutional frame: Arts. 129/215 courts of record; Act 1971 = “in addition to, not in derogation of” other laws (s. 22); s. 10 empowers SC/HC. (3) Punishment (ss. 11–12): simple imprisonment ≤ 6 months and/or fine ≤ ₹2,000; civil cases may substitute civil-prison ≤ 6 months where ends of justice demand; s. 12 proviso — discharge/remit on a genuine apology acceptable to the court. (4) Defences: ss. 3–9 (innocent publication; fair & accurate report; fair criticism of a case heard and finally decided, facts truly stated, good faith) + s. 13(b) truth (2006) if bona fide and in public interest; s. 14 in-face procedure; s. 15 needs AG/SG/AG consent; s. 17 notice + affidavit defence; s. 18 two-judge bench for s. 15 cases; s. 19 appeals (single judge → division bench 30 days; division bench → SC 60 days; only the convicted person is “aggrieved”). (5) Limitation: s. 20 — one year from the alleged act. (6) Apply: DU item 32 = civil pole (status-quo defiance, knowledge via A-5, refusal as CoA); Prashant Bhushan 🌐 = criminal pole (scandalising, fairness defence failed, symbolic Re. 1 + default 3 months, apology debate), Baradakanta 🌐 draws judge-as-judge vs judge-as-individual, UK abolished scandalising in 2013.
