Exam lens
A probate/L/A/succession-certificate question is a form question with a statute-shaped answer: caption court + statutory words in the heading + paragraph checklist + verification trio. Marks live in (1) correct court, (2) correct section cited in the heading, (3) every statutory particular of ss. 276/278/372 appearing as a paragraph, (4) the verification formula.
Vocabulary trap
"Probate" (s. 2(f)) is a copy of the will under seal + grant of administration — only for the executor named in the will (s. 222). "Letters of administration" is the court's grant to an administrator — with the will annexed (executor missing/refused/died) or without it (intestate). A "succession certificate" (Part X) is neither — it is a summary credential for collecting debts & securities, not a grant of the estate.
Three sentences DU's whole unit rests on

(i) No right as executor or legatee is established in court without probate or L/A with the will annexed, wherever s. 213 applies. (ii) No right of an intestate successor is established without L/A where s. 212 applies. (iii) No debt of the deceased is recovered in court except through the holder of probate, L/A or succession certificate (s. 214) — that third limb is Item 21.

Why a grant is needed — Part VIII bars (the spine of the unit)
SectionRuleDrafting consequence in Items 19–21
s. 211Property of a deceased vests in the executor (the will is his title — "creature of the will").Probate petitioner sues "as named executor" (Item 19, para 11).
s. 212No right to property of a person who died intestate can be established in court unless letters of administration are granted (subject to the Act's scheme of exceptions).Intestate heirs need an L/A grant — DU's companion form (son applies) and ICSI's "without will annexed" form.
s. 213No right as executor or legatee can be established unless a court in India has granted probate or L/A with the will annexed. Territorial scope (KAMKUS p. 117): wills by Christians & Jews (all India); by Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs, Jains within former presidency towns/West Bengal or immovable property there; by Parsis within the old Chartered High Court limits.Item 20's caption is a petition "of the Will" — L/A with will annexed by a beneficiary where s. 213 bites. Both Items 19–20 recite "no application to any other court" (s. 279).
s. 214No debt owing to the deceased can be recovered in court except by the holder of probate, letters of administration or a succession certificate.The bridge to Item 21: banks/tenants/debtors pay the certificate-holder — that is the whole practical purpose of s. 372 petition.
s. 216After any grant, only the grantee may sue or act as representative of the deceased until the grant is revoked.Why caveats and revocation practice matter (§ 6).

The will itself — DU's own drafting chapter (primary pp. 101–103) + prescribed book

DU lists the drafting points: no statutory standard form; clear, unambiguous language; complete clarity of property descriptions; drafted in first person; testator's details (name, age, occupation, address); bequeathment details (to whom, which property); executor's details; previous testaments; sound-mind recital; signature & attestation with witness details; no stamp duty on a will; registration optional. KAMKUS adds (p. 116): testimonium + attestation clause are "the most important parts"; every page signed by testator & both witnesses; scribe may also be an attesting witness; deposit of wills possible under Registration Act ss. 42–46.

DU specimen skeleton (p. 102): "THIS IS THE LAST WILL … of me, Sh. XYZ s/o Sh. ABC, R/O 13, PQS Appartments, Rohini, Delhi-110085 … made at __ on __. That life is uncertain … I bequeath voluntarily and out of my own free will in a sound state of mind, my self-acquired properties …" → family WHEREAS recitals → self-acquisition declaration → IMMOVABLE / MOVABLE schedules → "I HEREBY WISH that my property … would devolve … absolutely and unconditionally" (wife/son; daughter "well settled at her matrimonial home" → excluded with reasons) → "contents of my will have been read out to me in my own vernacular" → sound-mind declaration → attestation formula: "Signed by the above named testator in our presence at the same time and each of us has in the presence of the testator signed our name hereunder as an attesting witness" + two witness slots.

ICSI's "simple will giving all property to wife" and the detailed 9-clause family will (joint executors, charge for daughters, jewellery to wife — pp. 199–200) show the same architecture with fuller operating clauses.

Validity checklist — Part VI sections actually tested with these forms (KAMKUS pp. 114–117 + verified headings)
RequirementSectionRule as the uploads state it
Capacitys. 59Sound mind, not a minor, may dispose by will. Married woman may will whatever she could alienate in life. Deaf/dumb/blind not incapacitated if they know what they do. Intervals of sanity suffice; no will in intoxication/illness-induced unawareness. (KAMKUS)
Free wills. 61Will (or part) caused by fraud or coercion taking away free agency is void.
Revocabilitys. 62Will revocable/alterable at any time the maker is competent — "an irrevocable will is no will" (ICSI characteristic (d)).
Execution (unprivileged)s. 63(a) testator signs/marks, or another in his presence and by his direction; (b) signature placed so as to indicate intention to give effect as a will; (c) attested by 2+ witnesses, each seeing the signing or the acknowledged signature — need not be together; no particular form. The 14 paragraphs of DU's probate petition — content + statutory hook (s. 276 petition particulars read with ss. 279–281)

Prayer (DU verbatim structure) + closing

“The Petitioner therefore prays that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to order: (a) That Probate of the said Will of the deceased be granted to your petitioner; (b) Pass any other order as may be deemed fit and proper in the facts and circumstances of the case.” — signed “PETITIONER, THROUGH ADVOCATE”, Delhi, dated.

Note below the signature (DU's own): “To be supported by affidavit.” — the petition's statements are deposed to in a separate affidavit; the signature-block verification that follows is additionally the statutory verification.

The THREE verifications — Item 19's signature move

1 · Petitioner — "I, X the above named Petitioner, do hereby declare that what is stated therein is true to the best of my information and belief. Last para is true to my knowledge.”

2 & 3 · Witness No. 1 / Witness No. 2 — “I, … one of the witnesses of the Will, do hereby declare that what is stated therein is true to the best of my information and belief. I was present and saw the said Testator affix his signature … and acknowledge the writing … to be his Last WILL.”

Why three: the petition's facts are verified by the petitioner (information-and-belief), while the fact of execution is verified by each attesting witness — the DU pre-echo of s. 281 (probate petition “to be verified by one of the persons who attested the will”; ICSI's model prints the witness declaration with the express marginal note “see s. 281”). Court-fee & duty box (pleaded in paras 8 & 14)

Principle: fee on probate/L/A petitions is ad valorem on the value of the estate — Court Fees Act, 1870 as amended by each State (Delhi: Court Fees Act 1870 & amendments). I Art. 6: 2% (₹1k–5k) / 3% above ₹5k, computed on market value at death if filed within a year.

When letters of administration are needed — the working list (ICSI p. 197 + KAMKUS)

1. Testator failed to appoint an executor; 2. appointed executor refuses to act; 3. executor dies before or after proving the will but before administering the estate; 4. intestacy — where s. 212 (and practice) requires a grant for the heir to establish rights; 5. non-universal/residuary legatee applying — after citation (s. 235); 6. where the estate must be sued upon/collected by someone other than a named executor (s. 216: only the grantee may act).

Doctrine: what the grant gives (and does not give)

s. 220 — letters of administration entitle the administrator to all rights the intestate had. s. 221 — but they do not validate intermediate acts that diminish/damage the estate (contrast s. 227: probate validates the executor's intermediate acts). s. 211 vs 218 — executor is a creature of the will; administrator is a creature of the grant. s. 291(1) — every administrator (except s. 241 grants) must execute a bond with sureties for due administration; s. 291(2) — the judge may demand a like bond even from a probate-holder in Hindu/Muslim/etc. estates. s. 298 — power to refuse L/A (fitness, conflicts). s. 299 — appeal from the District Judge's order to the High Court.

What can a certificate cover? s. 370(2)'s "security" + the debt side
s. 370(2) — "security" means
(a) any promissory note, debenture, stock or other security of the Central or a State Government;
(b) any bond, debenture or annuity charged on the revenues of India (UK-Act heritage);
(c) any stock or debenture of, or share in, a company or other incorporated institution;

"Debt" is not defined in the Act — courts determine whether the claim is a "debt or security" for Part X (Govt. reading-material note on s. 370). Jurisdiction drill — the two limbs of s. 371 (real case, Gujarat HC 2023)

Limb 1: District Judge in whose jurisdiction the deceased ordinarily resided at death. Limb 2 (only if no fixed residence): where any part of the property is found.

Real illustration: in a 2023 petition (Gujarat HC, Art. 227 — succession-certificate matter from Vadodara), the petitioner invoked the second limb ("some movable property within Vadodara jurisdiction") while the deceased plainly had an ordinary residence elsewhere. The court held the limbs are joined by "or" with the second available only when the first is exhausted — you cannot straightway jump to the property-based forum. Plead residence first; property-based jurisdiction only residually. (Uploads contain no s. 371 case — web-verified.)

Lawgist examples on s. 371 (illustrative): "Raj lived in Mumbai and died there → Mumbai DJ has jurisdiction"; "Anita had no fixed home but owned a Delhi house → Delhi DJ"; court-fee/venue always follows the deceased's position, not the petitioner's.

Why the certificate exists — the s. 214 engine

s. 214: no debt owing to the deceased can be recovered in any court except by the holder of probate, L/A or a succession certificate. Banks won't break fixed deposits; share registrars won't transfer stock; insurers won't pay — without one of the three. The certificate is the cheapest and fastest of the three: summary (s. 373), no will to propound, no citation regimen of s. 235, granted on prima facie best title where questions get intricate.

What it does NOT do: it is not conclusive proof of heirship or title to the estate — only a credential for the specified debts/securities. Sawarni v. Inder Kaur, (1996) 6 SCC 223 (SC): unless set aside in accordance with law, a certificate and the rights flowing from it cannot be ignored while adjudicating claims to the deceased's property — yet that recognition does not elevate it to conclusive proof of title (full card § 7).

H. Venkatachala Iyengar v. B. N. Thimmajamma & Ors.AIR 1959 SC 443 · 1959 Supp (1) SCR 426 · Constitution Bench · 13 March 1959
WEBThe fountainheads. 63 ISA + Evidence s. 68

Facts: The propounder of the will of one Lakshmamma (H. Venkatachala Iyengar) sought probate; he had taken a prominent part in the execution of the will and received substantial benefit under it. The respondents contested: the execution was surrounded by circumstances exciting suspicion, and questions also arose about ownership of the properties bequeathed and the testator's capacity. A related passage of the judgment discusses the classic illustration of Shama Charn — the propounder claiming to be the adopted son, appointed one of three executors, taking substantial benefit while the testator left a widow and two daughters.

Issue: What is the onus on a party propounding a will, and how do "suspicious circumstances" alter it?

Arguments — Appellant (propounder): relied on statements/authorities concerning wills that are free from suspicion; the propounder's involvement and benefit should not by themselves damn a duly executed instrument; presumption of due execution assists him. Respondent No. 1 (through Mr. Viswanatha Sastri): those statements refer to wills free from suspicion and cannot be invoked where execution is surrounded by suspicion; where a party writes or prepares a will under which he takes a benefit, that circumstance ordinarily excites the court's suspicion and calls for vigilant and zealous examination of the evidence.

Decision (three-stage test): (1) the will must be proved like any other document — due execution and attestation per s. 63 ISA read with s. 68 Evidence Act; (2) testamentary capacity — the testator was of sound and disposing mind and understood the nature and effect of the dispositions; (3) the propounder must satisfy the court's conscience — any suspicious circumstances must be removed by cogent and satisfactory evidence. Where the propounder took a prominent part in execution and got substantial benefit, "that itself is generally treated as a suspicious circumstance" requiring explanation. Standard of proof remains the civil standard (preponderance), but judicial vigilance rises. Failure to dispel → will not accepted.

Principle: Onus probandi lies on the party propounding a will to satisfy the conscience of the court that the instrument is the last will of a free and capable testator; suspicion shifts a heavier burden onto the propounder — including the suspicion built of self-benefit and self-involvement.

Kavita Kanwar v. Pamela Mehta & Ors.(2021) 11 SCC 209 · Supreme Court of India
WEBPropounder = chief beneficiary

Facts: A will under which the propounder took a substantial share while dispositions were unnatural and unfair on the face of it; the testatrix was unwell at execution; her thumb impression was awkwardly placed; doubts existed whether she understood the contents; the propounder had taken a prominent part in arranging execution.

Issue: Does the propounder's dual role (organiser + beneficiary) itself count as a suspicious circumstance? Arguments: for the propounder — execution proved, no plea of fraud/coercion formally raised, dispositions are the testator's choice to make; against — unnatural disposition + placement of impression + comprehension doubt + procurement role form a constellation the propounder must answer.

Decision: Where the propounder takes a prominent part in execution and is a substantial beneficiary, that fact is itself a circumstance the propounder must affirmatively dispel; combined with the unnatural and unfair disposition and the comprehension doubts, the suspicion stood unrebutted → will refused probate. An honestly procured and properly proved will can still be admitted — the role is a red flag, not an automatic bar.

Principle: Self-benefit plus self-involvement is a classic Venkatachala-category suspicion; the court reads unnatural dispositions together with execution circumstances, not in isolation.

Sardari Lal v. Bishan Dass & Ors.2026 INSC 669 · Supreme Court of India · 06 July 2026 (as reported by legal portals)
WEBRecent · s. 63 + s. 68 applied

Facts: Registered will dated 06.11.1974 of Chhajju Ram, an illiterate agriculturist who affixed his thumbmark; the will disinherited his wife (his only Class-I heir) in favour of distant relatives without cogent reasons and contained incorrect recitals; execution was deposed to by an attesting witness (DW-2 Hariram).

Issue: Whether execution proved through the attesting witness (ss. 63 ISA / 68 Evidence Act) is enough when the dispositions and recitals raise suspicion.

Arguments — Appellants (contestors): burden lies on the propounders (defendants); suspicious circumstances — unnatural disposition against the wife, incorrect recitals, registration irregularities — unexplained; rely on H. Venkatachala Iyengar, Rani Purnima Debi, Kavita Kanwar. Respondents (propounders): attesting witness proved execution to s. 63/s. 68 standards; mere disinheritance of a wife or timing of execution is no infirmity — the testator may choose his beneficiaries freely.

Decision: Reaffirmed — the propounder must not only prove execution per s. 63 (with s. 68) but also explain the suspicious circumstances to the satisfaction of the court's judicial conscience; failure risks rejection of the will despite regular attestation.

Principle: Due attestation is necessary but not sufficient: suspicious dispositions and recital errors must be explained by the propounder (2026 restatement of the Venkatachala doctrine).

Ramabai Padmakar Patil v. Rukminibai Vishnu Vekhande(2003) 8 SCC 537 · Supreme Court

Duty of the propounder to remove all suspected features — but there must be "real, germane and valid suspicious features and not fantasy of the doubting mind." Companion: Vrindavanibai v. Ramachandra, AIR 1995 SC 2086 — a will is proved like any other document, except that the testator can no longer testify. Principle: Two-sided doctrine: heavy burden on the propounder, but suspicion must be real.

Krishna Kumar Birla v. Rajendra Singh Lodha & Ors.Supreme Court of India · S. B. Sinha & Harjit Singh Bedi, JJ · 31 March 2008
WEBCaveatable interest · ss. 283(1)(c), 284 ISA

Facts: A protracted testamentary contest over the wills and estate of a business-family principal (the "Birla" probate matters): probate petitions and caveats criss-crossed — caveators claimed interest as executors and legatees, as heirs who would take if the grants were refused, and under a mutual will; an executor nominated in place of a pre-deceased executor (YB) was also in the mix, and advocates connected to the litigation had witnessed the wills. The Probate Court held several parties had no caveatable interest; the High Court agreed in part (including that a mutual-will caveat could arise only after a suit for enforcement was decreed). The Supreme Court examined the doctrine end-to-end.

Issues: (i) Who may enter a caveat / receive citation under s. 284 & s. 283(1)(c)? (ii) Is "caveatable interest" to be read widely? (iii) Does executorship or a mutual will by itself create it?

Arguments — Appellants (caveators): s. 284 prescribes no qualifications for a caveator; "caveat" means a right to oppose; appointment as executor confers standing; the statute should be read purposively so interested persons are heard. Respondents (probate side): a caveator must have a real interest in the estate — one derivable if the grant is refused; busybodies cannot be heard; probate courts must not be turned into title suits; appointment as executor does not automatically clothe one with caveatable interest in the circumstances; a mutual will gives no present interest absent a decreed enforcement suit.

Decision & holdings (paras 56–58, 84, 94, 106–112, 128, 166, 183): (a) the person cited or caveating must have some interest in the estate; one who would otherwise have succeeded ordinarily has caveatable interest, others must show special interest; (b) no for "a busy body or an interloper having no legitimate concern" — a contrary reading would make ss. 283(1)(c)/284 anomalous; (c) caveatable interest is not synonymous with contention, and is not to be construed "very widely"; (d) a probate proceeding should not be converted into a title suit nor become an unchartered field for the unaffected; (e) an agreement to purchase estate property creates no caveatable interest; (f) existence of a mutual will or a mere filed suit does not create caveatable interest — it arises only after the enforcement suit is decreed (High Court affirmed on this limb); executorship analysed on its own facts.

Principle: Caveatable interest is a real, defined stake in the estate — presumptively the interest of one who would take on intestacy/refusal; it is not opened to all comers, and probate remains a summary testamentary proceeding, not a trial of title.

Kusum Bharat Asarpota & Anr. v. Jagdish Damodardas Asarpota & Anr.Bombay HC · 2015 (4) ALL MR 142
WEB

Facts: Grandchildren of the testator, claiming under a settlement deed revoked by the testator, were served/caveated against the probate; the appellant argued they had no caveatable interest because even if probate failed they would not inherit. Held: rejected — respondents were grandchildren who would take in the alternative; "a person who has even a slight interest in the estate of the testator is entitled to file caveat and contest the grant"; service before final order is mandatory; revocation-side maintainability affirmed. Distinguished: sale-agreement purchasers get no interest (para 85, following SC). Principle: Slight interest suffices; the bar falls on strangers, not on near heirs out of possession.

Tusharbhai Natwarlal Patel v. Rakshit Natwarlal PatelGujarat HC · 24 July 2015 (OJCA/371/2015 — caveat judgment)
WEB

Objector appeared without lodging a caveat (Sch. V / s. 284): the court walked through Rules 24–28 of the testamentary rules (affidavit in support → proceedings numbered as suit → issue whether caveator has interest → discharge or trial) and the Krishna Kumar Birla propositions before deciding standing. Principle: Procedure is the gate: no caveat (or no supporting affidavit in time) means no seat at the probate table.

Sawarni v. Inder Kaur & Ors.(1996) 6 SCC 223 · Supreme Court of India
WEB / Govt. reading materialSuccession certificate

Unless set aside in accordance with law, a succession certificate and the rights flowing from it cannot be ignored while adjudicating claims to the deceased's property — but such recognition does not elevate the certificate to conclusive proof of title. Applied alongside in the Govt. reading material: Rameshwari Devi v. Raj Bali Shah, 1987 SCC OnLine All 568 (same proposition). Principle: The certificate binds debtors and pays the estate's bills (s. 379), yet heirship/title remain triable separately.

Sachidanand v. BichitranandAIR 1990 Orissa 172
Govt. reading materials. 373 summary trial

Cited for the summary and limited nature of Part X proceedings — the s. 373 procedure does not preclude a sufficient examination when required, but the certificate track is not a full title suit. Companion: Ramchandra Ramratan v. Ramgopal, 1956 SCC OnLine MP 80 — circumstances in which the court may demand security under s. 375. Principle: Summary ≠ superficial; security is the court's safety valve.

Delhi HC — son's challenge to father's 1984 will fails (recent illustration)Delhi High Court, reported by rawlaw.in (probate granted 02.09.2008; HC upheld on challenge)
WEBTestamentary capacity on real facts

Facts: Testator executed a will in hospital days before death (asthma; hospitalised); executed through a nurse as independent attesting witness, with the wife herself attesting and a notary present; brothers subsequently occupied the demarcated portions; family settlement acknowledged the will; one daughter gave an NOC and did not contest; the appellant-son later denied his own signatures. The Probate Court granted probate on 02.09.2008; the son appealed/challenged ~20 years later.

Decision/Principle: Physical illness ≠ testamentary incapacity — asthma or weakness, without more, does not displace a sound disposing mind (a contemporaneous letter five days before death showed clarity); no suspicion arose from the ~20-year delay in obtaining probate; consistent post-death conduct, independent attestation and the NOC corroborated execution. Capacity is mental and volitional — hospitals, delay and infirmity are context, not invalidity.

Somasundera Mudaliar v. Ganga Bissen Soni28 Mad 386
UPLOAD — ICSI p. 197

Construction: where several parts of a will are absolutely irreconcilable, the later part prevails — statutory anchor s. 88 ISA ("the last of two inconsistent clauses prevails"). Principle: Reconcile if possible (s. 82, s. 85); if not, the last voice wins.

CIT v. Indian Sugar Mills Association(1974) 97 ITR 486 SC
UPLOAD — ICSI p. 197

Same s. 88 rule restated in the tax context: on repugnancy between clauses of a will, the last word shall prevail. Principle: Drafting lesson — put the operative intent last; the Act's default runs against earlier clauses.

How to write these in the DU answer sheet (8-mark sketch): Facts (2 lines: who propounded, what looked odd) → Issue (1 line: onus/caveatable interest/certificate effect) → Competing arguments (2 lines each — in will cases: "execution formally proved" vs "circumstances excite suspicion") → Holding (2 lines with section refs) → Principle (1 italic sentence). KAMKUS · Important Questions · Q.8 What do you mean by a Codicil?
Best model answer (from DU primary p. 101 + KAMKUS p. 114 — both uploads):

Definition: "Codicil" is defined in s. 2(b) of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 as "an instrument made in relation to a will, and explaining, altering or adding to its disposition, and shall be deemed to form part of the will." (GCA, 1873 s. 3(64) also folds codicils into "will".)

Nature: a codicil is a supplement to an already-executed will — it explains, alters or adds to the will's dispositions and is legally part of the will. When the changes are minor, a codicil is the proper instrument; when the changes are substantial, the correct practice is to execute a fresh will and revoke the earlier one.

Rules: (i) it requires the same formalities as a will — writing, signature, attestation by two witnesses (s. 63); (ii) on inconsistency between will and codicil, the codicil prevails; (iii) it speaks from the testator's death; (iv) revoked by the same modes as an unprivileged will (s. 70).

Practical/pleading link: the DU companion's probate petition is taken out for "the said Last Will … together with a Codicil" — proof through separate attesting-witness declarations for each instrument, prayer for probate of "Will together with the Codicil", and even an executorship claimed "as sole executor named in the codicil" (codicil can appoint/alter executors). In the courtroom: probate of a codicil discovered after the original grant has its own machinery (s. 225; s. 262 for L/A with will annexed).

Likely variant frames Not on the recorded papers — but the same unit's muscle memory.

Frame A — "Draft a petition for grant of succession certificate": use § 4's checklist — heading must recite s. 372, 11 paras, split verification, prayer (i) s. 374 powers + prayer (ii) s. 375 security exemption.
Frame B — "Draft a caveat against grant of probate": separate skill — O. XL CPC + s. 284 ISA, Schedule V form, with affidavit of caveatable interest in 8 days (§ 6) — do not confuse with the s. 148A CPC caveat of DU Form 10 (file 1).
