1.1 — Definition & the statutory anchor

DU’s working definition: the art of conveyancing is of English origin; “to convey” = to transfer or to make over; conveyancing means “an instrument or deed through which one or more living person transfer his or their interest in present or in future in or upon an immovable property to one or more living persons”. In other words, conveyance = the act by which property is voluntarily transferred from one person to another by means of a written instrument and other formalities”. 📘 p.92

📘 Section 2(10), Indian Stamp Act, 1899 — “Conveyance includes a conveyance on sale and every instrument by which property, whether movable or immovable, is transferred inter vivos and which is not otherwise specifically provided for by Schedule I.”

Why the Stamp Act defines it: stamp duty is chargeable on “every instrument” (s. 3) unless Schedule I exempts it — so the definition of the transaction decides the duty. DU opens Part B with this definition for exactly that reason: conveyancing = duty-bearing instruments.

1.2 — History (the DU narrative, compressed)

England

Deed-writing was optional until King Charles II; the Statute of Frauds, 1677 first required writing for creation/transfer of interests in landed property — except leases for less than three years. The Real Property Act, 1845 required all grants of landed interest in writing — that written form came to be known as “conveyancing”. Present-day forms rest on the Conveyancing of Land Act 1845 + Law of Property Act 1925. 📘

India

“No legislation in India has ever been passed on the law of conveyancing” — the art is judge- and practice-made. But conveyancing is not unknown: Qabuliyatnama, Jagirdar, Muafidar, Charpatra (redemption of rent) appear in Indian literature from ancient days; Charpatra, Jagir grants and Qabuliyats were all unilateral, under the grantor’s seal = Indian Deed Polls. 📘

Drafting in India

Presidency towns (Bombay, Calcutta, Madras) kept conveyancing with solicitors/barristers trained on English lines; in the mofussil towns the work fell to “deed writers”, “scribes” or “scribers” with no legal knowledge — so mofussil deeds “generally and commonly suffer from so many defects and sometimes these defects become incurable”. That sentence is DU’s justification for studying components letter-by-letter. 📘

1.3 — Deed, Deed Poll, Indenture (DU pp. 93–94)

Deed — broad vs legal

Broad sense: “something done or performed” — synonym of act. Legal sense: “a solemn act denoting document… an instrument written on parchment or paper executed, signed, sealed and delivered by the executant”. DU: a document “through which a present or future interest in an immovable property is transferred by one or more living persons to another living person(s)” — called a deed because it is “the most solemn and authentic act that a person can possibly perform in relation to his property”. Statements made in deeds may amount to admission and operate as estoppel. 📘

Halsbury’s definition (in DU): “an instrument written on parchment or paper expressing the intention of some persons named therein who make assurance of some interest in property, or of some legal or equitable right, title or claim, or undertake or enter into some obligation, duty or agreement enforceable at law or in equity, or to do some other act affecting the legal relation or position of a party.” 📘

KAMKUS cross-check 📗: deed = non-operative part (name, date, parties, recitals) + operative part (testatum, consideration, habendum, exceptions, covenants) + formal part (testimonium, signatures, attestation, parcels) — the same skeleton as DU’s A–N, regrouped in three buckets (§2.5).

Deed Poll vs Indenture — the English twin

Deed Poll: old practice — a document polled (cut straight) at top/bottom, executed unilaterally, written in the first person. Opens “Know All Men By These Presents” / “To whomsoever it may concern” / “I, so and so, Send These Presents”. Examples: bond, power of attorney, will — and DU adds mortgagor’s one-sided forms: mortgagors say mortgagors, sales and gifts too “can also be unilateral and so these are Deed Polls”. 📘

Indenture: old practice — two copies on one parchment, cut in indented/jagged line so halves interlock (the cut identified executants). Bilateral/multilateral, third person. Opens “This Indenture of… / This Deed of… / This Instrument of…”. DU’s examples: Lease (lessor + lessee both must execute), Mortgage deed, Partnership deed = indentures. 📘

Modern position (DU ¶6–7): today “there is no such difference and both are indiscriminately used for each other — the difference is only for phraseology but of no practical importance”. And the English historic difference never found place in India (indenture required a seal in England; India never required that).

Object & function of conveyancing (DU pp. 95)

Why it exists: movables pass by delivery; immovables cannot — so conveyancing “is that branch of the law of transfer of property which deals with the mode and form of transfer” agreed by transferor and transferee. Its main object: enable owners to make voluntary transfers of right, title and interest for a specific purpose/period — “such transfers are not otherwise possible than by conveyancing”. Four functions: (1) expresses intention — doubt/dispute/ambiguity is resolved from the words, phrases and expressions used; poor expression = defective deed, benefits may be lost to the transferee, and an adverse claimant may take legal possession while a willing transferor cannot help; (2) helps courts determine disputes later; (3) protects both sides — transferee from prior/subsequent acts of transferor (and those claiming through him), transferor from subsequent acts of transferee; (4) it is a document of title, the basis of the government’s record of rights, and documentary evidence. 📘

KAMKUS’s four drafting principles 📗 (add to any “how to draft” answer): ① clarity of expression — short, unambiguous, everyday words, no foreign/technical terms the parties don’t know; ② design of the draft — parts not prescribed by law, but a logical serial design so nothing material is omitted and nothing irrelevant added; ③ precision — familiar > technical, concrete > abstract, short > long, simple > complex sentences, active > passive voice; ④ communicability of intention — honestly expressed so the deed is operative and readable by the public who may deal with the property.

The two definitions (verbatim from DU) 📘
📘 s. 2(h), Indian Succession Act, 1925 — “Will means the legal declaration of the intention of a testator with respect to his property which he desires to be carried into effect after his death.”

📘 s. 2(b), ISA — “Codicil is an instrument made in relation to a will, and explaining, altering or adding to its disposition, and shall be deemed to form part of the will.”

Three essentials of a will (SC formulation, 🌐): (i) legal declaration of testator’s intention; (ii) with respect to his property; (iii) desire that it be carried into effect after death — an instrument “in its own nature ambulatory and revocable” during the testator’s lifetime. Testamentary vs inter vivos: a gift/settlement takes effect immediately and is (broadly) irrevocable; a will speaks only from death. 🌐

DU’s eleven drafting bullets (the checklist DU itself prints) 📘
  • No standard form prescribed by law — statute prescribes validity, not wording.
  • Language clear and unambiguous.
  • Properties described with complete clarity.
  • Will is drafted in first person (deed-poll style).
  • Testator’s details: name, age, occupation, address.
  • Details of bequeath: to whom, which property.
  • Details of the executor, if any.
  • Previous testaments, if any (revoke/mention).
  • Sound state of mind of testator.
  • Signature and attestation — testator + 2 witnesses with details.
  • No stamp duty · registration optional (not compulsory).

Definition + general vs special 📘
📘 DU quotes the Powers of Attorney Act, 1882: “Power-of-Attorney includes any instruments empowering a specified person to act for and in the name of the person executing it.”

DU’s own contrast: a General PoA “covers more than one subject matter”; a Special PoA “relates to a specific subject matter, though it may contain several powers relating to the same subject matter”. Stamping: “Power of Attorney is required to be stamped but need not be mandatorily registered” (DU’s general position — read with the State-amendment caveat below 🌐).

Agency backbone (Contract Act, Ch. 5.1 — DU’s heading theory (p. 107) + the statutory frame

📘 s. 54, Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (as DU prints it) — “Sale is a transfer of ownership in exchange for a price paid or promised or part-paid or part-promised.”
DU’s five bullets before the specimen 📘
  • The contract for sale of immovable property must be in writing.
  • s. 55 TPA lays down duties/obligations and rights of vendor and purchaser — under an agreement of sale and for sale — in the absence of a contract to the contrary.
  • Sale deed is chargeable with stamp duty.
  • Where value > ₹100, compulsory registration required.
  • Agreement + sale deed must clearly show: parties (Vendor/Vendee or Seller/Buyer), subject matter, intention to sell/buy, price + how paid, and other terms. A sale is usually preceded by an agreement for sale.
The statutory machine behind those bullets 🌐 verified
  • s. 54 proviso: for property ≥ ₹100, sale can only be effected by a registered instrument; a contract for sale creates no interest in the property (contract = mere right to sue).
  • s. 55 (ICSI’s summary 📘): vendor must (a) disclose material defect in property/title, (b) give title documents for investigation, (c) answer buyer’s questions, (d) execute conveyance on payment, (e) pay public dues till conveyance + protect title as an ordinarily prudent man. Buyer must (a) disclose his interest likely to affect price, (b) pay/tender price, (c) bear risk once ownership passes, (d) pay public dues from transfer. Rights: vendor keeps rents/profits till ownership passes + has a charge on property in buyer’s hands for unpaid consideration + interest; buyer gets benefit of improvements. All modifiable by contract to the contrary.
  • Reg. Act s. 17: sale > ₹100 (b); gift (a); leases ≥ 1 yr (d); non-testamentary instruments creating interest > ₹100 (c); s. 17(1)(A) (2001): s. 53A contracts-to-transfer with possession must be registered or they lose s. 53A protection.
  • s. 53A part performance — shield for possession-taking transferee, not title; s. 52 lis pendens.
  • Stamp: agreement = Stamp Act Sch. I Art. 5 (KAMKUS/ICSI); conveyance/sale = Art. 23 (KAMKUS 📗: “Stamp duty in a sale deed is chargeable under Art. 23, Schedule I”); duty on market value (State schedules).

Case in depth — Suraj Lamp & Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana (2012) 1 SCC 656 📘 quoted inside uploaded ICSI Lesson 6 🌐 citation verified
Facts / context
The practice of transferring immovable property through “SA/GPA/Will” combos instead of a registered conveyance was rampant — to evade stamp duty, registration charges, capital-gains tax, urban land-ceiling / development-authority restrictions and to launder unaccounted money. The petitioner (a company) challenged these transactions; States of Delhi, Haryana, Punjab, UP responded. The Court also examined whether States/Union had done enough to curb the practice. (ICSI’s uploaded summary 📘: “it has become common practice to effect transfers … by way of GPA sales or sale agreement, GPA or will transfers in order to evade … duties, taxes and other fees.”)
Issues
(1) Do SA/GPA/Will transactions transfer title / are they valid “sales”? (2) Are they used to evade revenue laws and breed black money/land-mafia? (3) Are existing State measures adequate?
Arguments
For invalidity: s. 54 requires a registered instrument for sale ≥ ₹100; GPA = agency, revocable, dies with principal; agreement creates only contractual rights; will speaks from death; combinations deliberately circumvent compulsory registration (Reg. Act ss. 17, 49) and s. 53A is only a shield. Defenders of practice: genuine family/development transactions use similar papers; some States’ attempts at validating/curbing were inconsistent.
Decision
(R.V. Raveendran J, 11 Oct 2011; (2012) 1 SCC 656) — SA/GPA/Will transfers do not convey any title and are not valid substitutes for a registered deed of conveyance; held illegal and not recognizable under law; directions to all State Governments to instruct registration departments, municipal corporations and revenue authorities not to mutate or recognize ownership based on GPA sales; sale/transfers of immovable property only by duly stamped registered sale deed; prospective from the October-2011 order (completed earlier transactions protected); genuine family arrangements and development agreements expressly preserved.
Principle
Title is a registered instrument, full stop. GPA is “merely an authorisation… revoked automatically by death… cannot sell property to himself… cannot operate as a conveyance”; agreement-to-sell = contractual right, not proprietary right; will = no lifetime effect. For every other form in this unit the case is the companion rule: when in doubt, register the actual operative deed. Map to DU: the GPA (Form 3) is drafted so that the attorney executes the sale deed — the specimen itself obeys Suraj Lamp.

Drafter’s craft — DPC Part VI, Rules 29–35 (practical checklist) 📘 uploaded DPC
  • 29 · Spell names and places correctly — verify names, addresses, property numbers, village/town names, dates, document numbers, registration details; same spelling throughout. (DU’s sale deed doc-no. 5560 / plan 400/B/85 are exactly this rule applied.)
  • 30 · Don’t keep changing the description of the parties — pick “the vendor/lessee/donor” and never oscillate (partner vs partners in DU Form 8 ¶3 = the error class); with multiple parties, distinguish by name: “releasor Avadh”, “releasee Ragini”.
  • 31 · Avoid ambiguous pronouns — “He told him that he would pay him when he received it” is fatal in a deed; repeat the party term (“the mortgagor shall… the mortgagor shall…”).
  • 32 · Use the language of the document where important — when quoting the charge sheet/policy/earlier deed, reproduce its wording, don’t paraphrase away a term of art (why ¶6 of DU’s sale deed quotes the sale-deed clauses of the agreement).
  • 33 · Avoid “ifs and buts” — plead/execute positively: “The purchaser paid ₹2,00,000 on…” not “may have paid perhaps”.
  • 34 · Don’t repeat facts — one fact, one home paragraph; later paragraphs reference, don’t recycle.
  • 35 · One factual proposition per paragraph — the numbered-clause architecture of every DU specimen depends on it.
Conveyancing-specific habits distilled from this unit 📘+📗+📘 ICSI
  • Read the title before the draft — schedule from the earliest registered deed: “X date, amount, document no… Addl. Bk… at pp…” (DU’s habit, every specimen).
  • Same building, same names — M-170 GK-II appears in the sale deed AND the lease: cross-document consistency is the poor-man’s title audit.
  • Define terms once, use them mechanically (“the Attorney”, “Movable Property”, “FIRST PART”) — components as software functions.
  • Every operative clause gets a warranty and a remedy — sale ¶5 indemnity; lease ¶7(iii) indemnity; mortgage ¶7 costs. A promise without a fallback clause is unfinished drafting.
  • Date, place, receipt, jurat — component B + G + M are not decoration: they’re the proof apparatus (KAMKUS: “details are mostly mentioned in recitals”, “signature and attestation are the most important parts of the deed”).
  • Stamp → register → possess → deliver documents — the four-step close in every DU form (and the s. 59/s. 54/s. 123 gates where the statute says so).
  • When statutes collide, the stricter form wins — PoA needs s. 59-style attestation only if it creates the mortgage, but a mortgage PoA presented at registration needs s. 33 authentication: draft to the intersection.
  • Never let a specimen’s error survive your pen — DU Form 13’s duplicated ¶2/20-day line, Form 6’s “Agreement” testatum, Form 7’s missing attestation block: reproduce with a drafting note, always.

KAMKUS Q.2: “What do you mean by deed? Discuss in brief its components.” 📗 Def. A deed is the most formal species of writing — an instrument under seal effecting a transfer/right, executed with the ceremonies of delivery & (where required) attestation. DU’s usage throughout: title “DEED OF…”, date/place, parties, recitals, operative words, covenants, schedule, testimonium. Components (full credit = list + one line each, per DU p.94 + KAMKUS (I)): A title&date/place · B parties&their descriptions · C recitals (agreement-clause!) · D introductory/“whereas” (insolvency/title facts) · E attestation (s. 3 TPA: two witnesses, each saw sign/acknowledgment & signs in executant’s presence; not needed for bonds? → cite as KAMKUS lists: bonds, gift s. 123, mortgage s. 59, will s. 63) · F commencement&testatum · G money&receipt · H operative words (differ per deed — compare) · I subject matter/parcels/boundaries · J rights, duties&obligations&restrictions · K consideration&expenses · L covenants for title · M “in witness whereof”/signature&seal · N technical interpretation. KAMKUS Q.4: “What do you mean by attestation?” 📗 Meaning: “to attest = to witness”; statutory definition in s. 3 TPA (quoted by KAMKUS): attested = by two or more witnesses, each of whom (i) has seen the executant sign/affix mark, or seen another sign in the executant’s presence and by his direction, or received personal acknowledgement; and each of whom signed in the executant’s presence; not all need be present together; no particular form. Required for: gift (s. 123 TPA), mortgage ≥₹100 (s. 59 TPA), wills (s. 63(c) ISA), bonds (KAMKUS cites s. 265 Stamp — printed-error flag: wills are exempt; verify before relying 🚩). Rules: party cannot be an attesting witness; witnesses need not know contents; proof = Evidence Act ss. 68–92 (KAMKUS prints “58/59” = ss. 68/69 🚩): one witness called to prove execution (Janki Narayan Bhoir: he must prove each attestation); if none alive → handwriting proof (s. 69); admission by party = sufficient (s. 70). Practice: attest everything — witnesses can always be called (KAMKUS).
KAMKUS Q.7: “What do you mean by Deed Poll and deed indenture?” 📗 Deed poll = single party, one party’s act, polled (cut even) top edge — wills (first person), GPA by one executant, declaration-style deeds: “I, AB, do hereby…”. DU Form 1/2 are deed-polls. Indenture = executed by all parties affected, indented (wavy/cut-and-thatched) edge historically — sale (both sign), lease (DU: both Lessor & Lessee), partnership, mortgage (mortgagor+mortgagee both execute DU Form 7). Drafting difference: poll = one voice (“I/we” declarant), other side defined but not executing; indenture = reciprocal rights on every signatory + (usual) mutual covenants + counterpart clauses. Exam tip: give the history (indent/poll edges) + modern usage + 2 specimen examples each. (Fig. m8c in §1.)
KAMKUS Q.8: “What do you mean by a Codicil?” 📗 s. 2(b) ISA (DU’s own quote): “an instrument made in relation to a will, and explaining, altering or adding to its disposition, deemed to form part of the will.” Functions: alter/share-redistribute, add new legacies, appoint/change executor, revoke earlier codicils — all without re-executing the will. Requirements: same execution formalities as a will (s. 63 — signature + 2 attesting witnesses, testator’s intention acknowledged in their presence); revocation logic: later will revokes earlier wills and codicils (unless words show contrary intention); date/place essential (codicil chronology decides which disposition governs). Drafting note: refer to the will by its date/registration number; state precisely which clauses are altered (“Clause 4 be read as…”). DU’s bullet 8 (previous testaments) is the hook.
KAMKUS Q.16: “What do you mean by Conveyancing?” 📗 Meaning: the act/power of passing/delivering property from one to another; conveyancing = drafting/engrossing/executing the instruments that transfer, charge, lease or otherwise deal with property — sale, mortgage, lease, gift, exchange, relinquishment, POAs, wills. DU’s own frame: history (Charpatra → Qabuliyatnama → indenture), the deed as the vehicle, s. 2(10) Stamp Act context, functions of recitals (agreement, title, procedure). Scope in syllabus: component parts (Part A) + forms 1–13 (Part B). Modern practice: search + draft + stamp + register + handover of documents (schedules “safety of original documents”); Suraj Lamp rule = conveyancing’s golden thread: only registered conveyance moves title. Answer: meaning + history + instrument families + modern close (4 steps) + one case.
