2. The dilemma of causation — Barnes & Teeters, “The Eternal Quest for the Causes of Crime”

DU Case Material p. 35 This is the first prescribed reading of Topic 2 and it is the correct opening paragraph for any theory answer, because it tells the examiner that you know the limits of what follows.

2.1 Crime is eternal — Tannenbaum

Verbatim — Frank Tannenbaum, quoted at DU Case Material p. 35 “Crime is eternal — as eternal as society. So far as we know, human fallibility has manifested itself in all types and forms of human organization. Everywhere some human beings have fallen outside the pattern of permitted conduct. It is best to face the fact that crime cannot be abolished except in a non-existent utopia. Weakness, anger, greed, jealousy — some form of human aberration — has come to the surface everywhere, and human sanctions have vainly beaten against the irrational, the misguided, impulsive, and ill-conditioned. For reasons too subtle and too complex to understand, the ordinary pressures and expectancies that pattern the individual’s conduct into conformity break down in given instances. They have always done so: they always will. No way of drawing the scheme of the good life has yet been discovered which will fulfill the needs of all human beings at all times.

Crime is therefore an ever-present condition, even as sickness, disease, and death. It is as perennial as spring and as recurrent as winter. The more complex society becomes, the more difficult it is for the individual and the more frequent the human failures. Multiplication of laws and of sanctions for their observance merely increases the evil. Habituation becomes more difficult in a complex society, and the inner strains grow more obvious.”

Barnes & Teeters then note that the Italian criminologist Giorgio Florita goes further: “like sin, crime is normal in society and it is our sanctions and laws, made by man, that are abnormal” — a “fascinating, but not necessarily novel thesis (Durkheim, the French sociologist, held a similar view)”. This is your bridge from reading 1 to reading 6.

2.2 The rejection of the single-factor (“unilateral”) theory

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 36 “It has been popular to attribute criminal behavior to one cause or factor, or to one set of factors. It is equally popular, especially among the ‘experts,’ to construct a ‘system’ or ‘frame of reference’ that will explain delinquency and crime, suicide, desertion and divorce, or any other form of human pathology. Students in the behavioral sciences are constantly on the alert, hoping to discover the ‘open sesame’ to the riddle of human misconduct. Yet this riddle of crime causation remains to bedevil society. No unilateral theory, however profound, whether it is nurtured and expounded by biologists, psychologists, psychiatrists, or sociologists, can ever hope to answer the question of the totality of criminal behavior.”

They catalogue the competing claimants: the socio-economic approach (dominant in America); biological or constitutional factors (dominant “on the European continent and in Latin America”); the geographical determinists, who espouse “weather, the seasons, topography, humidity”; and “the psychiatrists, the sociologists, the endocrinologists, the psychologists and the religionists, as well as the racists and the eugenists”. The honest conclusion: “The writers of this book have no such theory to advance, but will make whatever attempt to synthesize various theories as these may justify.”

2.3 What “cause” can honestly mean

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 36–37 “…we can never be sure that a given set of factors will always produce a crime or a delinquent act. As a result, some writers on criminal behavior state that it is futile to discuss causes at all. This is as untenable as the position of others who write assuredly about causes, as if they were discussing the metered energies in a physical experiment.

As a first step to a better understanding of crime — and its causes — it is well to cease discussing crimes and criminals in any general sense and concentrate solely on individual crime situations and individual criminals. … But such study will have little permanent value unless we discover from it the personal and social situations most likely to produce crime or most favorable to crime. … Investigation of an individual prison inmate may help to rehabilitate him but it will be of no general social importance unless it helps to tell us what personal and social factors encourage antisocial behavior.

Without contending that they will inevitably cause crime, it is apparent that certain conditions are more favorable to crime than others. Bad heredity, physical defect, mental imbalance, mental defect, emotional insecurity, a slum environment, poor education, criminal associates, extreme poverty, an environmental stimulation to crime are obviously more favorable to crime than their opposites. It is true that any or all of these unfavorable conditions will not inevitably drive a given person to commit a crime under all circumstances. Conversely, any one or all of the favorable conditions listed above may not be gilt-edge insurance against a person committing a crime. Hidden factors that may tip the scale either way can never be eliminated from specific situations by all the theory in the world.”
How to use this reading Open every Topic-2 answer with a two-sentence version of Barnes & Teeters: (a) crime is a normal and permanent feature of every society (Tannenbaum, Durkheim); (b) no single-factor theory can explain it, so what follows is an account of partial explanations, each of which illuminates a different class of offence. Close the answer by returning to the multiple-factor approach and naming the theories that best fit the particular crime the question is about. This structure alone separates a 60% answer from a 75% answer.
The counter-position — the “multiple factor” debate. Barnes & Teeters’ eclecticism was attacked by Sutherland himself (you will meet him at §12), who argued that a mere list of correlated factors is not a theory at all: a scientific theory must state conditions that are present when crime is present and absent when crime is absent. Albert Cohen’s essay “Multiple Factor Approaches” (1951) called the approach a confusion of causes with correlates. In an answer, present Barnes & Teeters as the honest statement of the problem and Sutherland as the attempt at a solution; that is exactly how the DU material sequences the two readings.

3. Pre-classical (demonological) thought — the spiritual explanation of crime

DU Case Material pp. 38–39 Vold, Bernard & Snipes begin not with Beccaria but with what Beccaria was protesting against.

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 38–39 “Classical criminology emerged at a time when the naturalistic approach of the social contract thinkers was challenging the spiritualistic approach that had dominated European thinking for over a thousand years. … One of the most important sources for these spiritual explanations of crime was found in the theology of St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)… Aquinas argued that there was a God-given ‘natural law’ that was revealed by observing, through the eyes of faith, people’s natural tendency to do good rather than evil. The criminal law was based on and reflected this ‘natural law.’ People who commit crime (i.e., violate the criminal law) therefore also commit sin (i.e., violate the natural law). Aquinas held that crime not only harmed victims, but it also harmed criminals because it harmed their essential ‘humanness’ — their natural tendency to do good.

… Because crime was identified with sin, the state had the moral authority to use many horrible and gruesome tortures on criminals. That was because the state claimed that it was acting in the place of God when it inflicted these horrible punishments on criminals.”

The sentence on Jean Calas (1762) — the image to reproduce in the answer

“… in a chemise, with head and feet bare, (Calas) will be taken in a cart from the palace prison to the Cathedral. There, kneeling in front of the main door, holding in his hands a torch of yellow wax weighing two pounds, he must … (ask) pardon of God, of the King, and of justice. Then the executioner should take him in the cart to the Place Saint Georges, where upon a scaffold his arms, legs, thighs, and loins will be broken and crushed. Finally, the prisoner should be placed upon a wheel, with his face turned to the sky, alive and in pain, and repent for his said crimes and misdeeds, all the while imploring God for his life, thereby to serve as an example and to instil terror in the wicked.” Beirne, quoted in the DU Case Material at p. 39 — two years before Beccaria published.

The material draws the moral expressly: “The extensive religious symbolism in the manner of execution clearly suggests that crime is intertwined with sin, and that in punishing crime, the state is taking the part of God.” (The Calas affair — a Huguenot merchant broken on the wheel for the supposed murder of his son to prevent a conversion to Catholicism — became Voltaire’s great cause; Calas was posthumously exonerated in 1765.)

3.1 The characteristics of the demonological school

Explanation
Crime is the work of the devil, or possession by evil spirits, or a breach of divine command. The offender is a sinner, not a rational actor.
Proof
Trial by ordeal (fire, water, hot iron), trial by battle, and compurgation — all appeals to divine judgment rather than to evidence. India’s parallel: the divya (ordeals) described in the Manusmriti and Yajnavalkya Smriti — agni, jala, tula (balance), visha (poison) and kosha (sacred water).
Punishment
Expiatory, public, bodily, infinitely variable at the will of the judge — burning, breaking on the wheel, mutilation, branding, the pillory.
Its residue today
The survival of witch-hunting in parts of India (the Prevention of Witch (Daain) Practices Act, 1999 — Bihar; Jharkhand Prevention of Witch Practices Act, 2001; Chhattisgarh Tonahi Pratadna Nivaran Act, 2005; Rajasthan Prevention of Witch-Hunting Act, 2015; Assam Witch Hunting (Prohibition, Prevention and Protection) Act, 2015) shows that the demonological explanation has not disappeared — it has itself become an object of criminal law. Statute
Real-world exampleThe NCRB has consistently recorded deaths attributed to witchcraft accusations (roughly 80–100 murders a year through the 2010s, concentrated in Jharkhand, Odisha, Bihar, Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh). Superstition-driven killing is thus simultaneously (a) evidence of surviving demonological belief and (b) a crime explained best by Sellin’s culture conflict and conflict theory — the belief system of a marginal community collides with the conduct norms embodied in the State’s law, and the accusation typically falls on widows and single women with land.

4. The Classical School — Cesare Beccaria

4.1 The social-contract background

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 39 “Beginning with Thomas Hobbes (1588–1678), ‘social contract’ thinkers substituted naturalistic arguments for the spiritualistic arguments of people like Aquinas. While Aquinas argued that people naturally do good rather than evil, Hobbes argued that people naturally pursue their own interests without caring about whether they hurt anyone else. This leads to a ‘war of each against all’ in which no one is safe because all people only look out for themselves.

Hobbes then argued that people are rational enough to realize that this situation is not in anyone’s interests. So people agree to give up their own selfish behavior as long as everyone else does the same thing at the same time. This is what Hobbes called the ‘social contract’ — something like a peace treaty that everyone signs because they are all exhausted from the war of each against all. But the social contract needs an enforcement mechanism in case some people cheat… This is the job of the state. According to Hobbes, everyone who agrees to the social contract also agrees to grant the state the right to use force to maintain the contract.”

“Other social contract philosophers such as Locke (1632–1704), Montesquieu (1689–1755), Voltaire (1694–1778), and Rousseau (1712–1778) followed Hobbes in constructing philosophies that included a natural and rational basis for explaining crime and the state’s response to it.” But — and this is the point students miss — “they did not represent the thinking of the politically powerful groups that ruled the various states in Europe. Those ruling groups still held to the spiritual explanations of crime… Consequently, the criminal justice systems of the time tended to impose excessive and cruel punishments.”

4.2 The man and the book

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 40 “Cesare Bonesana, Marchese de Beccaria, was an indifferent student who had some interest in mathematics. After completing his formal education, he joined Allessandro Verri, an official of the prison in Milan, and his brother Pietro Verri, an economist, in a group of young men who met regularly to discuss literary and philosophical topics. Beccaria was given an assignment in March 1763 to write an essay on penology, a subject about which he knew nothing. With help from the Verri brothers, the essay was completed in January 1764, and was published under the title Dei delitti e delle pene (On Crimes and Punishments) in the small town of Livorno in July of that year, when Beccaria was 26 years old.”

The group was the Accademia dei Pugni (“Academy of Fists”). Beccaria “objected especially to the capricious and purely personal justice the judges were dispensing and to the severe and barbaric punishments of the time.”

4.3 Beccaria’s TEN PRINCIPLES — reproduce these in full

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 40–42 (the ten numbered principles) 1. Legislatures define crimes and punishments. “The role of the legislatures… should be both to define crimes and also to define specific punishments for each specific crime. This contrasted with the practice of Beccaria’s time when legislatures passed very general laws and left the implementation up to the vast discretion of the judges.”

2. Judges determine guilt only — no discretion in sentencing. “The role of judges should be solely to determine guilt… Once that determination was made, then the judge should follow the strict letter of the law in determining the punishment. Instead of vast discretion, Beccaria argued that judges should have no discretion whatsoever: ‘Nothing is more dangerous than the popular axiom that it is necessary to consult the spirit of the laws. It is a dam that has given way to a torrent of opinions…’”

3. Seriousness is measured by HARM, not by intent. “The seriousness of a crime should be determined solely by the extent of the harm that it inflicts on society. Beccaria argued that other factors were irrelevant in determining seriousness, including the intent of the offender: ‘Sometimes, with the best intentions, men do the greatest injury to society; at other times, intending the worst for it, they do the greatest good.’”

4. Punishment must be proportionate, and its purpose is deterrence. “‘It is to the common interest not only that crimes not be committed, but also that they be less frequent in proportion to the harm they cause society. Therefore, the obstacles that deter man from committing crime should be stronger in proportion as they are contrary to the public good, and as the inducements to commit them are stronger.’”

6. Punishment beyond what deterrence requires is tyranny. “‘For punishment to attain its end, the evil which it inflicts has only to exceed the advantage derivable from the crime; in this excess of evil one should include the certainty of punishment and the loss of the good which the crime might have produced. All beyond this is superfluous and for that reason tyrannical.’”

7. Excessive severity increases crime. “‘The severity of punishment of itself emboldens men to commit the very wrongs it is supposed to prevent; they are driven to commit additional crimes to avoid the punishment for a single one. The countries and times most notorious for severity of penalties have always been those in which the bloodiest and most inhumane of deeds were committed, for the same spirit of ferocity that guided the hand of the legislators also ruled that of the parricide and assassin.’”

8. Punishment must be PROMPT. “‘The more promptly and the more closely punishment follows upon the commission of a crime, the more just and useful will it be. I say more just, because the criminal is thereby spared the useless and cruel torments of uncertainty… the promptness of punishment is more useful because when the length of time that passes between the punishment and the misdeed is less, so much the stronger and more lasting in the human mind is the association of these two ideas, crime and punishment.’”

9. Punishment must be CERTAIN. “‘The certainty of a punishment, even if it be moderate, will always make a stronger impression than the fear of another which is more terrible but combined with the hope of impunity; even the least evils, when they are certain, always terrify men’s minds.’”

10. Prevention is better than punishment. “‘It is better to prevent crimes than to punish them. That is the ultimate end of every good legislation. … Do you want to prevent crimes? See to it that the laws are clear and simple and that the entire force of a nation is united in their defense, and that no part of it is employed to destroy them. See to it that the laws favor not so much classes of men as men themselves. See to it that men fear the laws and fear nothing else. For fear of the laws is salutary, but fatal and fertile for crimes is one man’s fear of another.’”
Note on the numbering. The DU case material’s printed list jumps from 4 to 6 (there is no separately numbered principle 5). Reproduce the substance, not the numeral; examiners are concerned with the ten ideas, not the typography of the reprint.

Beccaria’s further demands, and his summary

DU Case Material p. 42 “Beccaria also emphasised that the laws should be published so that the public may know what they are and support their intent and purpose; that torture and secret accusations should be abolished; that capital punishment should be abolished and replaced by imprisonment; that jails be more humane; and that the law should not distinguish between the rich and the poor.”

“In order for punishment not to be, in every instance, an act of violence of one or of many against a private citizen, it must be essentially public, prompt, necessary, the least possible in the given circumstances, proportionate to the crimes, dictated by the laws.” Beccaria’s own summary of On Crimes and Punishments — DU Case Material p. 42. Memorise this sentence; it is the single most quotable line in Topic 2.

4.4 The reception, condemnation and triumph of the book

DU Case Material p. 42 “Beccaria’s ideas were quite radical for his time, so he published his book anonymously and defended himself in the introduction against charges that he was an unbeliever or a revolutionary. In fact, the Roman Catholic Church condemned the book in 1766 because its rationalistic ideas abandoned the spiritualistic frame of reference. The book was placed on the church’s Index of Forbidden Books where it remained for over two hundred years. But despite Beccaria’s fears and some opposition, his ideas were extremely well received by his contemporaries.”

Earlier, at p. 40, the material explains why the book travelled: “Even more important for the book’s acceptance… was the fact that the American Revolution of 1776 and the French Revolution of 1789 occurred soon after its publication in 1764. These two revolutions were both guided by naturalistic ideas of the social contract philosophers. To these revolutionaries, Beccaria’s book represented the latest and best thinking on the subject of crime and criminal justice. They therefore used his ideas as the basis for their new criminal justice systems. From America and France, Beccaria’s ideas spread to the rest of the industrialized world.”

Beccaria in the Indian statute book — where to point the examiner
  • Legality / nullum crimen sine lege: Art. 20(1) of the Constitution (no ex post facto criminal law, no penalty greater than that prescribed at the time of the offence) is Beccaria’s principle 1 constitutionalised.
  • Definitions and graded punishments: the entire architecture of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 — an offence defined, followed by a punishment section — is Beccaria’s design, transmitted through Macaulay’s IPC of 1860, which was itself drafted on Benthamite/classical lines.
  • Proportionality: the grading of theft (s.303 BNS), extortion, robbery (s.309) and dacoity (s.310); of hurt (s.115), grievous hurt (s.117) and culpable homicide (s.105) by harm caused.
  • Certainty and celerity: BNSS 2023 — s.173 (registration of information), s.193 (90-day status report to the informant), the 90/60-day limits on investigation, the requirement to frame charges within 60 days (s.251), and the mandate to pronounce judgment within 45 days of conclusion of trial (s.392). Beccaria’s celerity argument is the philosophical justification for all of these.
  • Publication of laws: Art. 13; s.3(58) General Clauses Act; the rule that ignorance of law is no excuse presupposes publication. Compare Harla v. State of Rajasthan, AIR 1951 SC 467 — a law of which the public has no means of knowing cannot be enforced.
  • Against torture and secret accusation: Art. 20(3), Art. 22, ss.35–38 BNSS (grounds of arrest; information to relatives), D.K. Basu v. State of W.B. (1997) 1 SCC 416, and the Law Commission’s 273rd Report (2017) recommending a Prevention of Torture Bill.
  • Against capital punishment: Law Commission of India, 262nd Report (2015) recommending abolition for all offences except terrorism and waging war — Beccaria’s argument, two and a half centuries later.
  • “Laws should favour not classes of men but men themselves”: Arts. 14 and 15.

5. Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) — utilitarianism and the felicific calculus

The syllabus expressly names Bentham alongside Beccaria. Bentham is the classical school’s engineer: where Beccaria supplied the principles, Bentham supplied a calculus and a building.

The principle of utility
“Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure… They govern us in all we do, in all we say, in all we think.” (Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, 1789.) The measure of right action is “the greatest happiness of the greatest number”.
Hedonistic / felicific calculus
Punishment is to be measured by seven dimensions of pleasure and pain: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (nearness), fecundity, purity and extent. Crime is a rational choice; make the pain marginally exceed the gain and the rational actor abstains.
Rules for punishment
Punishment is itself an evil and is admissible only “in as far as it promises to exclude some greater evil”. It must never be inflicted where it is groundless, inefficacious (infancy, insanity, intoxication, unintentionality, duress — an early general part!), unprofitable (too expensive) or needless.
The Panopticon
Bentham’s circular prison with a central inspection tower — “a new mode of obtaining power of mind over mind”. Foucault made it the emblem of modern disciplinary power in Discipline and Punish (1975). Its Indian descendants are the radial jails at Alipore, Tihar and the Cellular Jail, Port Blair.
Codification
Bentham coined “codification” and campaigned for complete, published, rationally arranged codes. Macaulay’s Indian Penal Code (1860) — drafted by the First Law Commission (1834–37) — is the most successful Benthamite code ever enacted; James Mill and John Stuart Mill were Bentham’s disciples at East India House. The IPC is therefore a classical-school document, and the BNS 2023 inherits its structure.
Against natural rights
“Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense — nonsense upon stilts.” Bentham’s hostility to rights-talk is the classical school’s weakness: a purely utilitarian criminal law can justify punishing the innocent if the aggregate happiness increases — the standard retributivist objection (Kant; and in India, the “rarest of rare” safeguards of Bachan Singh).

6. The Neo-Classical School — the return of the person

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 42 “Following the French Revolution of 1789, Beccaria’s principles were used as the basis for the French Code of 1791. Consistent with his arguments, the judge was only an instrument for applying the law, and the law undertook to prescribe an exact penalty for every crime and every degree of crime. The greatest practical difficulty in applying this code came from ignoring differences in the circumstances of particular situations. The code treated everyone exactly alike, since only the act, not the intent, was considered in determining the punishment. Thus first offenders were treated the same as repeaters, minors were treated the same as adults, insane the same as sane, and so on. The set, impersonal features of this code then became the point of attack for a new school of reformers who complained about the injustice of a rigorous code and championed the need for individualization and discriminating judgment to fit individual circumstances. Ultimately, these reactions resulted in revisions to the code so that judges were able to exercise discretion in considering factors such as age, mental condition, and extenuating circumstances. These practical revisions of classical criminology constitute what is often called the ‘neoclassical’ school.

As such, the neoclassical school represented no particular break with the basic doctrine of human nature that made up the classical tradition… The doctrine continued to be that people possess free will and that they are guided by reason and self-interest. They therefore can be controlled by their fear of punishment…”

7. The transition to Positivism — why the classical programme failed

DU Case Material p. 43 This is the hinge of the whole reading, and examiners love it because it shows that positivism was not a whim but a response to data.

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 43 “Beccaria’s theory changed criminal justice policies, especially in France, and led to the expectation that crime would soon decrease. But there was really no way to find out whether this occurred, since there were no annual crime statistics to measure whether crime was going up or down.

The first annual national crime statistics were published in France in 1827, about sixty years after Beccaria wrote his book. It soon became clear that these crime statistics were astonishingly regular. The rates of crime in general and of particular crimes such as murder and rape remained relatively constant from year to year. In addition, some places in the nation had higher crime rates while others had lower, and these differences remained relatively constant from year to year.

Today, we take such regularity in crime statistics for granted, but at that time, those who held a ‘free will’ theory of crime expected random changes in the number of crimes, especially in the number of unpremeditated crimes such as passion murders. The regularity of crime statistics suggested that … rather than being entirely the product of free will, crime must be influenced by factors in the larger society. …

But the new crime statistics also made it clear that crime rates were going up, not down… Even more distressing, these statistics suggested that recidivism was going up. People who had received the prompt, proportionate punishments provided by the new French code were committing more new offenses rather than fewer. This suggested that Beccaria had been wrong to argue that changes in punishment policies alone could reduce crime.

The new crime statistics clearly revealed the failure of classical punishment policies, while at the same time suggesting that other social factors might influence the level of crime in society. This gave rise to a new brand of criminology, which eventually became known as positivism. Its goal was to study the causes of crime either in the larger society or in the individual.”

7.1 The cartographic / moral-statistics school — Guerry and Quetelet

DU Case Material p. 38 “About fifty years after Beccaria wrote, the French scholar Andre-Michel Guerry (1802–1866) and the Belgian scholar Adolphe Quetelet (1796–1874) began to study the social characteristics of political units such as provinces and nations in order to determine what might cause their high or low crime rates.”

A.-M. Guerry, Essai sur la statistique morale de la France (1833)
A lawyer in the French Ministry of Justice; drew shaded maps of France by department for crimes against the person and against property. Found, contrary to expectation, that property crime was highest in the rich, industrial north and violent crime highest in the poor, agricultural south. Conclusion: it is not poverty as such but opportunity and inequality — “the wealth of a region is less important than the contrast between rich and poor within it”. This is the direct ancestor of modern relative deprivation theory.
Adolphe Quetelet, Sur l’homme (1835)
Astronomer and statistician; invented the “average man” (l’homme moyen) and the Body Mass Index. Found crime rates to be as regular as the tides and wrote the famous line: “Society prepares the crime, and the guilty is only the instrument by which it is executed.” Identified age and sex as the strongest correlates of crime — a finding that has survived 190 years of research — and formulated the thermic law of delinquency (violent crime peaks in hot climates and summer; property crime in cold climates and winter).
Why this matters for the answer. The moral statisticians are the true founders of the scientific study of crime — a full forty years before Lombroso. David Garland (Topic 1 reading) calls the belief that criminology began with Lombroso “the lombrosian myth”. If the question asks about the origins of positivism, mentioning Guerry and Quetelet before Lombroso will immediately mark your answer out.

8. Cesare Lombroso (1835–1909) — the born criminal

8.1 The atavism thesis

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 43–44 “Cesare Lombroso (1835–1909) was a physician who became a specialist in psychiatry, and his principal career was as a professor of legal medicine at the University of Turin. His name came into prominence with the publication of his book, L’uomo delinquente (The Criminal Man), in 1876. In that book Lombroso proposed that criminals were biological throwbacks to an earlier evolutionary stage, people more primitive and less highly evolved than their noncriminal counterparts. Lombroso used the term atavistic to describe such people.

The idea of evolution itself was relatively recent at the time, having first been proposed by Darwin in his book, On the Origin of Species (1859). … Even the idea that some individuals might be reversions to an earlier evolutionary stage had been originally suggested by Darwin, who had written: ‘With mankind some of the worst dispositions which occasionally without any assignable cause make their appearance in families, may perhaps be reversions to a savage state, from which we are not removed by many generations.’”

The “stigmata” — Lombroso’s physical markers of the born criminal

Lombroso claimed the atavistic type could be recognised by anomalies of the body and the mind. The DU material for female criminality (p. 109) lists his female stigmata directly: “large hand size; low voice pitch; having moles; being short; darker skin; having dark hair; being fat”. His general list included an asymmetrical skull, receding forehead, enormous jaws, high cheekbones (the phrase that survives in the DU solved paper: “enormous paws, high cheekbones”), large ears, prehensile foot, excessive arm length, supernumerary nipples/fingers/toes, insensitivity to pain, acute eyesight, tattooing, use of argot, and love of orgies. He famously narrated the moment of insight: the autopsy of the brigand Giuseppe Villella, in whose skull he found a median occipital fossa and “the problem of the nature and of the origin of the criminal seemed to me resolved”.

8.2 The mature Lombroso — the point that wins marks

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 44 “Lombroso, as a founder of the positive school of criminology, is something of an anomaly. Lombroso is known principally for the earliest formulation of his theory of the atavistic criminal. The real basis of the positive school, however, is the search for the causes of criminal behavior. That search is based on the conception of multiple factor causation, in which some of the factors may be biological, others psychological, and still others social.

Lombroso did much by way of documenting the effects of many of these factors. As his thinking changed over the years, he looked more and more to environmental rather than biological factors. This change and growth in his thinking was evidenced by the increases in the number of pages in successive editions of L’uomo delinquente. In its first edition in 1876, Lombroso required 252 pages to explain his theory of evolutionary atavism as the cause of crime. Twenty years later, in the fifth edition of his book, he needed over 1,900 pages to include all the items that appeared to be related to crime causation. Those included such things as climate, rainfall, the price of grain, sex and marriage customs, criminal laws, banking practices, national tariff policies, the structure of government, church organization, and the state of religious belief.

Lombroso’s last book, Crime, Its Causes and Remedies, was a summary of his life work specially prepared for American readers. Published in 1911, two years after Lombroso’s death, it includes discussions of many factors related to crime causation, of which by far the largest numbers are environmental rather than biological.”

8.3 Lombroso’s three classes of criminal

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 44 “He maintained that there are three major classes of criminals: (1) born criminals, to be understood as atavistic reversions to a lower or more primitive evolutionary form of development, and thought to constitute about one third of the total number of offenders; (2) insane criminals, i.e., idiots, imbeciles, paranoiacs, sufferers from melancholia, and those afflicted with general paralysis, dementia, alcoholism, epilepsy, or hysteria (strange bedfellows, to be sure); and (3) criminaloids, a large general class without special physical characteristics or recognizable mental disorders, but whose mental and emotional makeup are such that under certain circumstances they indulge in vicious and criminal behavior. Lombroso conceded that well over one half of all criminals were ‘criminaloids,’ so that they were not ‘born criminals’ or ‘insane’ in the sense that he used those terms.”

(In other writings Lombroso added a fourth and fifth category — the criminal by passion, including political criminals, and the occasional criminal or pseudo-criminal. The DU solved paper for 2017 Q.3 uses the four-fold list: born criminals, insane criminals, occasional criminals/criminaloids, and criminals of passion “who commit crimes because of anger, love or honour”.)

8.4 The collapse of the theory

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 45 “By the time of Lombroso’s death in 1909 it was evident that his theories were too simple and naive. Anthropology abandoned the conception of uniform, linear evolution with humans as the most highly evolved animal (and the English gentleman as the most highly evolved human), whereupon his notion of the atavistic criminal as a less evolved person became quite meaningless. Psychiatry and psychology were already marshaling evidence to show that the relationship between crime and epilepsy, or between crime and insanity, was much more complex and involved than Lombroso assumed.

Despite these criticisms, Lombroso’s theory of the atavistic criminal received enormous public attention at the time. As a result, for most of the twentieth century, Lombroso was described in criminology textbooks as the first criminologist to search for the causes of crime and therefore as the founder of positivist criminology.”

The five standard criticisms — set them out as a list

  1. No scientific basis. “The assumption regarding some sort of connection between atavism and criminal behaviour has no scientific basis. He ignored the view that a person’s physique may be affected because of sickness also.” 2017 Q.3 model answer
  2. No control group / defective sampling. Lombroso compared convicts with soldiers, not with a properly matched non-criminal population. His female sample was “a small unrepresentative sample of incarcerated female offenders who showed signs of physical deterioration that were partially a result of criminal justice system ‘treatment’ rather than a cause of criminality” (DU material, p. 113, summarising Pollak’s critique).
  3. Ignored economic and social conditions. “Crime being of social origin is of changing nature and cannot be explained with reference to atavism. Further, he did not explain the lower rate of criminality among women having the same stigmata.”
  4. Charles Goring’s refutation. The English Convict: A Statistical Study (1913) compared 3,000 English convicts with undergraduates, soldiers and hospital patients and found no significant physical difference — “there is no such thing as a physical criminal type”. (Goring did, however, find convicts slightly shorter and lighter, and substituted “defective intelligence” for atavism — so he replaced one form of biological determinism with another. Garland makes much of this in the Topic-1 reading.)
  5. Faulty assumptions delayed the subject. “Critics believe [Lombroso’s] assumptions hindered the progress of scientific criminology for about 50 years” — Sutherland & Cressey’s charge that Lombroso “delayed for fifty years the work which was in progress at the time of its origin and in addition, made no lasting contribution of his own”. Against this, Donald Taft: “the importance of Lombroso’s work lies in the great influence it had upon criminology and also upon penal practice.”
Lombroso’s Indian afterlife — the point that earns a distinction The most consequential Indian application of the “born criminal” idea was the Criminal Tribes Act, 1871 (extended 1911 and consolidated 1924), under which entire communities were notified as “addicted to the systematic commission of non-bailable offences”, compelled to register, restricted in movement and confined to settlements. The Act was repealed in 1952 and the communities “denotified” — but the stigma was immediately re-imposed by the State Habitual Offenders Acts, and the “history-sheeter”, the “bad character” register and the surveillance registers of the police manuals reproduce the same logic today. See the Idate Commission Report on Denotified, Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic Tribes (2017), and, judicially, Sukanya Shantha v. Union of India, 2024 INSC 753, where the Supreme Court struck down prison-manual provisions that classified persons of denotified tribes as “habitual” and assigned labour by caste — holding expressly that a person cannot be treated as criminal by birth. That case is the clearest judicial repudiation of Lombroso in Indian law, and it belongs in any answer on positivism. Case in DU material list, Topic 8

9. Enrico Ferri (1856–1928) — the law of criminal saturation

Who he was
Lombroso’s most brilliant pupil, a Socialist deputy in the Italian Parliament, and the man usually called the founder of criminal sociology. Principal work: Criminal Sociology (1884). In 1921 he drafted a new Penal Code for Italy — the “Ferri Project” — which was never enacted but remains the most thorough attempt to legislate positivism.
His break with Lombroso
“Through his scholarly researches, Ferri proved that mere biological reasons were not enough to account for criminality. He firmly believed that other factors such as emotional reaction, social infirmity or geographical conditions also play a vital role.”
The Law of Criminal Saturation
“Crime is the synthetic product of three main factors: (1) physical or geographical (climate, soil, seasons, temperature); (2) anthropological (age, sex, physical and psychic constitution, civil status, occupation); and (3) psychological or social (density of population, religion, customs, economic and industrial conditions, public opinion, education, alcoholism, the state of the law). Just as a given volume of water at a given temperature will dissolve a fixed quantity of chemical substance and not an atom more or less, so in a given social environment with given individual and physical conditions a fixed number of crimes, no more and no less, will be committed.” The corollary — the law of criminal super-saturation — is that an exceptional disturbance (war, famine, epidemic, political upheaval) will produce an exceptional burst of crime.
Five-fold classification of criminals
(1) born criminals; (2) occasional criminals; (3) passionate criminals; (4) insane criminals; (5) habitual criminals. (The 2017 DU solved answer lists the same five.)
“Penal substitutes”
Ferri’s most original and most examinable idea: since crime is produced by social conditions, the State should prevent it by altering those conditions rather than by punishing. His sostitutivi penali included free trade, cheap bread, better street lighting, birth control, public recreation, metal money instead of paper, divorce, and the reform of electoral law. This is the intellectual ancestor of modern situational crime prevention and of India’s welfare-based prevention programmes.
Punishment and responsibility
“Ferri emphasised that a criminal should be treated as a product of the conditions which played on his life. Therefore, the basic purpose of a crime-prevention programme should be to remove conditions making for crime.” He favoured the indeterminate sentence “keeping in view the possible chances of the inmate’s re-adjustment in the community”, and in the Penal Project “denied moral responsibility and denounced punishment for retribution and moral culpability”, replacing it with social responsibility and social defence.
Criticism. Critics say the law of criminal saturation “is nothing more than a statement that the law of cause and effect equally applies to criminal behaviour” — i.e., it is an analogy, not a testable proposition; it cannot tell us in advance what the saturation point of a given society is. And by abolishing moral responsibility Ferri’s scheme risks indefinite detention of the “dangerous” — the very objection Taft & England make in the Topic-1 reading (“we cannot push men about like pawns on a chessboard”).

10. Raffaele Garofalo (1852–1934) — natural crime, pity and probity

Summary of the standard Indian text (Paranjape), consistent with the DU solved paper “Raffaele Garofalo was one of the three main exponents of the positive school. Born in Naples in 1852, he started his career as a Magistrate in Italian courts and rose to be Minister of Justice in 1903. He was the only positivist who had varied experience as an eminent jurist, a senator and a professor of criminal law. Rejecting the classical theory of free will as a cause of crime, Garofalo defined crime as an act which offends the sentiments of PITY and PROBITY possessed by an average person and which are injurious to society. He emphasised that lack of pity generates crimes against the person while lack of probity leads to crimes against property.”
“Natural crime”
Garofalo’s attempt to find a definition of crime independent of the accidents of any particular legal system: conduct which violates the two altruistic sentiments of pity (revulsion at the infliction of suffering) and probity (respect for the property rights of others), as those sentiments exist in the average member of the community. Coined the word criminology (Criminologia, 1885).
Four classes of criminal
(1) Murderers — “endemic” or typical criminals, wholly lacking both pity and probity; (2) violent criminals, affected by environmental influences such as “prejudices of honour, politics and religion”; (3) criminals lacking in the sentiment of probity (thieves); and (4) lascivious or lustful criminals who commit crimes against sex and chastity.
Punishment — elimination and social defence
Three modes: death, imprisonment for life or transportation, and reparation. Having “witnessed the total failure of correctional measures in France, Garofalo was not very optimistic about reformation of offenders. He therefore strongly pleaded for elimination of habitual offenders who were incapable of social adaptation, as a measure of social defence.” Garofalo is thus the harshest of the three positivists — a useful contrast with Ferri.
Criticism
“Pity and probity” are themselves culturally variable, so “natural crime” smuggles back the relativity it was meant to escape (cf. Taft: “crime is … an expression of subjective opinion varying in time and place”). Garofalo cannot account for mala prohibita, political crime or white-collar crime, and his elimination thesis slides easily into eugenics.
Garofalo in Indian lawGarofalo’s “natural crime” survives in the distinction between mala in se and mala prohibita which the DU syllabus makes examinable in Topic 3; his pity/probity division corresponds almost exactly to the architecture of the BNS — Chapter VI (offences affecting the human body, ss.100–146: pity) and Chapter XVII (offences against property, ss.301–334: probity). His social defence doctrine, mediated through Marc Ancel, is the philosophy behind preventive detention, the Habitual Offenders Acts and s.394 BNSS (security for good behaviour from habitual offenders).

11. The relation between classical and positivist theories — the DU reading’s own conclusion

This is the section students skip and examiners reward. The material does not present the two schools as enemies.

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 45–46 “Positive criminology might seem opposed to classical criminology, but this is not necessarily the case. Rather, classical theories can be interpreted as implying a theory of human behavior that is quite consistent with positivism. In the past, classical criminologists have assumed that the certainty and severity of criminal punishments could affect criminal behavior, but that other variables in the environment could not. But in his defense of classical criminology, Roshier argues:
“In general, there was nothing inherent in Beccaria’s intellectual position to preclude a consideration of the socio-economic context of crime, any more than there was to necessitate his sole concentration on deterrence… Indeed, it is an oddity that he seemed to see the criminal justice system as being the only aspect of the environment that influences individual decisions about whether it is worthwhile to commit crime or not.”
Classical criminologists therefore could expand their theoretical frame of reference and examine how crime rates are influenced by a wide range of factors outside the criminal justice system, including biological, psychological, and social factors. All these factors could then be described as ‘causes’ of crime. Reflecting this basic position, Beirne argues that the proper place for Beccaria’s theory in the history of criminology lies ‘at the very beginning of the tradition to which it is commonly opposed, namely, positivist criminology.’

A similar point can be made about positive criminology. … in their defense of positive criminology, Gottfredson and Hirschi argue:
“No deterministic explanation of crime can reasonably exclude the variables of the classical model on deterministic grounds. These variables may account for some of the variation in crime. If so, they have as much claim to inclusion in a ‘positivistic’ model as any other set of variables accounting for the same amount of variation.”
Thus, positive criminologists can include the certainty and severity of criminal punishments among the many other factors that might influence criminal behavior.

Positivist and classical criminology therefore are really part of the same enterprise — they both seek to identify the factors that influence the incidence of criminal behavior. The basic controversy between them is EMPIRICAL rather than THEORETICAL: Which factors have more influence on criminal behavior and which have less?”

11.2 The reading’s conclusion — criminology IS a science

12. Learning theories I — Gabriel Tarde (1843–1904) and the laws of imitation

DU Case Material p. 53 Williams opens the differential-association chapter by tracing it back to Tarde: “It has been said to have been conceived out of a refinement of the work of G. Tarde (1843–1904). Tarde’s theory was one of imitation, stating first, that all men imitate each other. Although Sutherland never gave much acknowledgement to Tarde, it seems he probably used this theory of imitation, expanded it, refined it and made it more popular. Instead of imitation, his theory was based upon a wider idea involving all the normal mechanisms involved in the learning [process].”

Who Tarde was
A French magistrate and later head of criminal statistics at the Ministry of Justice; the most formidable critic of the Italian positive school. Principal works: La criminalité comparée (1886) and Penal Philosophy (1890). “He asserted that the influence of social environment was most emphatic on criminal behaviour and that the biological and physical factors had only a casual effect on it.”
The three laws of imitation
(1) Close contact — people imitate one another in proportion to the closeness of their association, so imitation is most frequent and most complete in cities (“fashion” prevails) and slowest in the countryside (“custom” prevails). (2) Imitation of superiors by inferiors — “the subordinate or inferior members have a tendency to imitate the ways of their superiors, just as children imitate their parents”; crimes begin in the upper ranks and percolate downwards. (3) Insertion — new fashions of behaviour displace or reinforce old ones; where two mutually exclusive fashions meet, one is substituted for the other (e.g. the knife replaced by the pistol).
Why it matters for Sutherland
Tarde supplied the crucial idea that criminality is acquired in social interaction — which is the whole of proposition 1 of differential association. Sutherland’s advance was to replace the vague notion of “imitation” with the mechanisms of ordinary learning, and to add the ratio of favourable to unfavourable definitions.
Criticism
“The effect of imitation is still worse on youngsters” — but imitation cannot explain (a) the origin of the behaviour imitated, (b) why some people imitate criminal models and others do not, or (c) the innovative offender. Williams levels exactly these criticisms at Sutherland too (§15).
Real-world exampleTarde’s “imitation of superiors by inferiors” is visible in the Indian jurisprudence of copycat crime and in the recurring argument that televised or filmed depictions of a technique are followed by clusters of similar offences — the argument that persuaded Parliament to insert s.67 and ss.67A–67B of the Information Technology Act, 2000 and the Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995 programme code, and the argument the Supreme Court dealt with in K.A. Abbas v. Union of India, AIR 1971 SC 481 (pre-censorship of films upheld partly on the ground of the medium’s power of suggestion).

13. Sutherland & Cressey — the problem for criminological theory

DU Case Material p. 47 Reading 3 is the theoretical foundation of differential association. Most students jump straight to the nine propositions; the examiner who set the 2017 paper clearly had read the preceding pages, and so should you.

13.1 What a scientific theory of crime must do

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 47 “If criminology is to be scientific, the heterogeneous collection of multiple factors known to be associated with crime and criminality must be organized and integrated by means of explanatory theory which has the same characteristics as the scientific theory in other fields of study. That is, the conditions which are said to cause crime should be present when crime is present, and they should be absent when crime is absent. Such a theory or body of theory would stimulate, simplify, and give direction to criminological research… Furthermore, it would be useful in minimizing crime rates, provided it could be ‘applied’ in much the same way that the engineer ‘applies’ the scientific theories of the physicist.”

This single sentence is Sutherland’s answer to Barnes & Teeters’ multiple-factor eclecticism (§2). A list of correlates is not an explanation; a theory must state a condition that is necessary and sufficient.

13.2 First procedure — LOGICAL ABSTRACTION

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 47 “There are two complementary procedures which may be used to put order into criminological knowledge. The first is logical abstraction. Blacks, males, urban dwellers, and young adults all have comparatively high crime rates. What do they have in common that results in these high crime rates? Research studies have shown that criminal behavior is associated, in greater or lesser degree, with such social and personal pathologies as poverty, bad housing, slum-residence, lack of recreational facilities, inadequate and demoralized families, mental retardation, emotional instability, and other traits and conditions. What do these conditions have in common which apparently produces excessive criminality? Research studies have also demonstrated that many persons with those pathological traits and conditions do not commit crimes and that persons in the upper socio-economic class frequently violate the law, although they are not in poverty, do not lack recreational facilities, and are not mentally retarded or emotionally unstable. Obviously, it is not the conditions or traits themselves which cause [crime]… and they also are sometimes absent when criminality does occur. A generalization about crime and criminal behavior can be reached by logically abstracting the conditions and processes which are common to the rich and the poor, the males and the females, the blacks and the whites, the urban and the rural-dwellers, the young adults and the old adults, and the emotionally stable and the emotionally unstable who commit crimes.

… An explanation of criminal behavior should be consistent with a general theory of other human behavior, but the conditions and process said to produce crime and criminality should be specific. Many things which are necessary for behavior are not important to criminality. Respiration, for instance, is necessary for any behavior, but the respiratory process cannot be used in an explanation of criminal behavior, for it does not differentiate criminal behavior from noncriminal behavior.”
The “respiration test”. Remember this as the test Sutherland applies to every rival theory: does the proposed cause differentiate? Poverty fails the test (most poor people are law-abiding; many rich people offend). Broken homes fail it. Low IQ fails it. Only the content of what a person has learned, he argues, survives.

13.3 Second procedure — DIFFERENTIATION OF LEVELS OF ANALYSIS

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 48 “The second procedure for putting order into criminological knowledge is differentiation of levels of analysis. The explanation or generalization must be limited, largely in terms of chronology, and in this way held at a particular level. For example, when renaissance physicists stated the law of falling bodies, they were not concerned with the reasons why a body began to fall except as this might affect the initial momentum. Galileo did not study the ‘traits’ of falling objects themselves, as Aristotle might have done. Instead, he noted the relationship of the body to its environment while it was falling freely… and it made no difference to his generalization whether a body began to fall because it was dropped from the hand of an experimenter or because it rolled off the ledge of a bridge due to vibration caused by a passing vehicle. Also, a round object would roll off the bridge more readily than a square object, but this fact was not significant for the law of falling bodies…

Much of the confusion regarding crime and criminal behavior stems from a failure to define and hold constant the level at which they are explained. By analogy, many criminologists… would attribute some degree of causal power to the ‘roundness’ of the object in the above illustration.”

The two boys and the length of their legs — the illustration to reproduce

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 48 “A motion picture made several years ago showed two boys engaged in a minor theft; they ran when they were discovered; one boy had longer legs, escaped, and became a priest; the other had shorter legs, was caught, committed to a reformatory, and became a gangster. In this comparison, the boy who became a criminal was differentiated from the one who did not become a criminal by the length of his legs. But ‘length of legs’ need not be considered in a criminological theory because it is obvious that this condition does not determine criminality and has no necessary relation to criminality. In the illustration, the differential in the length of the boys’ legs apparently was significant to subsequent criminality or noncriminality only to the degree that it determined the subsequent experiences and associations of the two boys. It is in these experiences and associations, then, that the mechanisms and processes which are important to criminality or noncriminality are to be found.”
Why this passage is gold in an answerIt simultaneously (a) disposes of biological positivism — the legs are irrelevant as such; (b) concedes that biology may matter indirectly, by shaping who you end up associating with; and (c) foreshadows labelling theory (the boy who was caught and sent to a reformatory became a gangster — Lemert’s secondary deviance, Tannenbaum’s “dramatisation of evil”). Quote it whenever a question asks you to compare biological, learning and interactionist explanations.

13.4 Two types of explanation — mechanistic and historical

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 49 “Scientific explanations of criminal behavior may be stated either in terms of the processes which are operating at the moment of the occurrence of crime or in terms of the processes operating in the earlier history of the criminal. In the first case, the explanation may be called ‘mechanistic,’ ‘situational,’ or ‘dynamic’; in the second, ‘historical’ or ‘developmental’. Both types of explanation are desirable. …

However, criminological explanations of the mechanistic type have thus far been notably unsuccessful, perhaps largely because they have been formulated in connection with an attempt to isolate personal and social pathologies among criminals. Work from this point of view has, at least, resulted in the conclusion that the immediate determinants of criminal behavior lie in the person-situation complex.

The objective situation is important to criminality largely to the extent that it provides an OPPORTUNITY for a criminal act. A thief may steal from a fruit stand when the owner is not in sight but refrain when the owner is in sight; a bank burglar may attack a bank which is poorly protected but refrain from attacking a well-protected bank…”

The DU reading quotes Gibbons in support of the mechanistic view: “In many cases, criminality may be a response to nothing more temporal than the provocations and attractions bound up in the immediate circumstances… Little or nothing may be added to this understanding from a close scrutiny of the early development of the person.” Sutherland nevertheless opts for the developmental explanation, and it is that which the nine propositions state — “on the assumption that a criminal act occurs when a situation appropriate for it, as defined by the person, is present.”

Link to modern theory. Sutherland’s “person–situation complex” and his insistence that the situation matters because it supplies opportunity is the direct ancestor of routine activity theory (Cohen & Felson, 1979: motivated offender + suitable target + absence of a capable guardian) and of situational crime prevention (Ronald Clarke). Use this to add a contemporary layer to any differential association answer.

14. The NINE PROPOSITIONS of Differential Association — verbatim

Learn these nine word-for-wordThey are the single most frequently examined paragraph in the entire DU case material. Reproduce them numbered, in order, and then evaluate. The text below is exactly as printed at pp. 50–51 of the material (Sutherland & Cressey, Criminology, 10th edn., 1978, p. 77), with the material’s own typographical slips silently corrected.
Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 50–51, “DEVELOPMENTAL EXPLANATION OF CRIMINAL BEHAVIOR” “The following statements refer to the process by which a particular person comes to engage in criminal behavior:

1. Criminal behavior is LEARNED. Negatively, this means that criminal behavior is not inherited, as such; also, the person who is not already trained in crime does not invent criminal behavior, just as a person does not make mechanical inventions unless he has had training in mechanics.

2. Criminal behavior is learned in INTERACTION with other persons in a process of COMMUNICATION. This communication is verbal in many respects but includes also ‘the communication of gestures.’

3. The principal part of the learning of criminal behavior occurs within INTIMATE PERSONAL GROUPS. Negatively, this means that the impersonal agencies of communication, such as movies and newspapers, play a relatively unimportant part in the genesis of criminal behavior.

4. When criminal behavior is learned, the learning includes (a) TECHNIQUES of committing the crime, which are sometimes very complicated, sometimes very simple; (b) the specific direction of MOTIVES, DRIVES, RATIONALIZATIONS, and ATTITUDES.

5. The specific direction of motives and drives is learned from DEFINITIONS of the legal codes as favorable or unfavorable. In some societies an individual is surrounded by persons who invariably define the legal codes as rules to be observed, while in others he is surrounded by persons whose definitions are favorable to the violation of the legal codes. In our American society these definitions are almost always mixed, with the consequence that we have culture conflict in relation to the legal codes.

6. A person becomes delinquent because of an EXCESS of definitions favorable to violation of law over definitions unfavorable to violation of law. THIS IS THE PRINCIPLE OF DIFFERENTIAL ASSOCIATION. It refers to both criminal and anticriminal associations and has to do with counteracting forces. When persons become criminal, they do so because of contacts with criminal patterns and also because of isolation from anticriminal patterns. Any person inevitably assimilates the surrounding culture unless other patterns are in conflict; a southerner does not pronounce ‘r’ because other southerners do not pronounce ‘r.’ Negatively, this proposition of differential association means that associations which are neutral so far as crime is concerned have little or no effect on the genesis of criminal behavior. Much of the experience of a person is neutral in this sense, for instance, learning to brush one’s teeth. This behavior has no negative or positive effect on criminal behavior except as it may be related to associations which are concerned with the legal codes. This neutral behavior is important especially as an occupier of the time of a child so that he or she is not in contact with criminal behavior during the time the child is so engaged in the neutral behavior.

7. Differential association may vary in FREQUENCY, DURATION, PRIORITY, and INTENSITY. … Frequency and duration as modalities of associations are obvious and need no explanation. Priority is assumed to be important in the sense that lawful behavior developed in early childhood may persist throughout life, and also that delinquent behavior developed in early childhood may persist throughout life. This tendency, however, has not been adequately demonstrated, and priority seems to be important principally through its selective influence. Intensity is not precisely defined, but it has to do with such things as the prestige of the source of a criminal or anticriminal pattern and with emotional reactions related to the associations. In a precise description of the criminal behavior of a person, these modalities would be rated in quantitative form and a mathematical ratio would be reached. A formula in this sense has not been developed, and the development of such a formula would be extremely difficult.

8. The process of learning criminal behavior by association with criminal and anticriminal patterns involves ALL OF THE MECHANISMS that are involved in any other learning. Negatively, this means that the learning of criminal behavior is not restricted to the process of imitation. A person who is seduced, for instance, learns criminal behavior by association, but this process would not ordinarily be described as imitation.

9. While criminal behavior is an expression of general NEEDS and VALUES, it is NOT EXPLAINED by those general needs and values, since noncriminal behavior is an expression of the same needs and values. Thieves generally steal in order to secure money, but likewise honest laborers work in order to secure money. The attempts by many scholars to explain criminal behavior by general drives and values, such as the happiness principle, striving for social status, the money motive, or frustration, have been, and must continue to be, futile, since they explain lawful behavior as completely as they explain criminal behavior. They are similar to respiration, which is necessary for any behavior, but which does not differentiate criminal from noncriminal behavior.”

14.1 The proposition Sutherland adds after the nine

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 51 “It is not necessary, at this level of explanation, to explain why persons have the associations they have; this certainly involves a complex of many things. In an area where the delinquency rate is high, a boy who is sociable, gregarious, active, and athletic is very likely to come in contact with the other boys in the neighborhood, learn delinquent behavior patterns from them, and become a criminal; in the same neighborhood the psychopathic boy who is isolated, introverted, and inert may remain at home, not become acquainted with the other boys in the neighborhood, and not become delinquent. In another situation, the sociable, athletic, aggressive boy may become a member of a scout troop and not become involved in delinquent behavior. The person’s associations are determined in a general context of social organization. A child is ordinarily reared in a family; the place of residence of the family is determined largely by family income; and the delinquency rate is in many respects related to the rental value of the houses. Many other aspects of social organization affect the associations of persons.”

This is the passage that answers the commonest objection (“why do two brothers turn out differently?”) and that links the micro theory to the macro theory of the next section.

14.2 Williams’ restatement, and the three points she adds

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 53–54 (Williams) “Differential association is a theory of learning. Fundamentally, it asserts that crime is learnt by association with others. … All of these differing and sometimes conflicting definitions are experienced, and will lead to criminality if the individual is more exposed to views which are supportive of crime than to views which are against it. This is the central idea of the theory…

The criminal activity may also be affected by the frequency, duration, priority and intensity of the definitions… The longer and more frequently one is exposed to a particular type of behaviour or attitude, the more effect it is likely to have. The stress on priority is intended to denote that the earlier the attitude is experienced, the more forcefully it is likely to affect later behaviour. Finally, intensity has to do with the prestige of the person portraying a particular type of behaviour and the emotional reactions related both to the source and the content of the information. To an extent, this may explain why policemen and prison officers do not generally become criminal despite being in constant and close contact with criminals — they hold them in very low regard, and therefore only wish to learn from them how to remain as little like them as possible. …

It is important to note that Sutherland does not consider that offenders are driven by different goals and desires from non-criminals, but rather that they choose different MEANS of achieving those ends. Criminality may be entered into for financial gain or happiness, but most people manage to pursue financial gain and possibly even happiness in law-abiding jobs. To explain criminal behaviour by reference to its ends is therefore futile… It was the reason for choosing the particular method of reaching the end that was important.

Since its inception, the theory of differential association has been modified… For example, Glaser (1956) suggested that criminal learning was not only, or even primarily, based on associations between individuals, but involved identification with criminal roles and therefore a desire to emulate them.”

Williams also gives a fine illustration of proposition 5: parents “might not disapprove of certain types of theft: to feed the hungry, to clothe children, or from certain types of victims like large stores or large employers. They might teach sympathy to these whilst still teaching that theft is generally wrong.” This is the mechanism by which an ordinary, non-criminal upbringing can still supply definitions favourable to particular offences — and it is the bridge to Sykes & Matza’s techniques of neutralization (1957): denial of responsibility, denial of injury, denial of the victim, condemnation of the condemners, appeal to higher loyalties.

15. Differential SOCIAL ORGANISATION — Sutherland’s explanation of crime RATES

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 52 “The preceding explanation of criminal behavior purports to explain the criminal and noncriminal behavior of individual persons. … it is possible to state sociological theories of criminal behavior which explain the criminality of a community, nation, or other group. The problem, when thus stated, is to account for variations in crime rates… The explanation of a crime rate must be consistent with the explanation of the criminal behavior of the person, since the crime rate is a summary statement of the number of persons in the group who commit crimes and the frequency with which they commit crimes.

One of the best explanations of crime rates from this point of view is that a high crime rate is due to social disorganization. The term social disorganization is not entirely satisfactory, and it seems preferable to substitute for it the term DIFFERENTIAL SOCIAL ORGANIZATION. The postulate on which this theory is based, regardless of the name, is that crime is rooted in the social organization and is an expression of that social organization. A group may be organized FOR criminal behavior or organized AGAINST criminal behavior. Most communities are organized for both criminal and anticriminal behavior, and in that sense the crime rate is an expression of the differential group organization. Differential group organization as an explanation of variations in crime rates is consistent with the differential association theory of the processes by which persons become criminals.”
Why the change of name matters. “Social disorganization” (the Chicago School term — see §24) implies that the slum has no organisation. Sutherland’s point, made against Shaw and McKay, is that the slum is intensely organised — only organised differently, and partly for crime. This is a one-sentence killer point to deploy whenever a question pairs “social disorganization theories” with Sutherland, as the DU syllabus does.
Real-world exampleThe coal-mafia towns of Dhanbad, the sand-mining belts of the Yamuna and Chambal, and the “liquor mafia” districts of prohibition States are not disorganised. They have sophisticated organisation — logistics, financing, political protection, dispute-resolution — that is organised for crime, coexisting with schools, temples, resident welfare associations and courts that are organised against it. The crime rate expresses the balance. That is precisely the analysis the 2017 examiner wanted when asking which theory best explains organised crime in India.

16. Williams’ EVALUATION of differential association — the criticisms

DU Case Material pp. 54–56 Reproduce these in an ordered list; they are worth as many marks as the nine propositions.

  1. It cannot explain the ORIGIN of criminality. “It is impossible for this theory to explain the origins of criminality, because if the behaviour does not already exist, it cannot be learned by a second person (see Jeffery, 1959).”
  2. It cannot explain NEW and inventive crimes. “There are new and very inventive crimes which occasionally appear; certainly the mechanics for these could not have been learnt by differential association, although of course the desires and motives behind such crimes may have been learnt (see Parker, 1976).”
  3. It cannot explain offenders with no criminal associates. “It does not explain the criminality of those who have never been subjected to criminals or to people who would hold criminal ideas. Such people could not have learnt criminality by interpersonal contact: if it was learnt at all, it would probably have been from the media, which Sutherland relegates to a relatively unimportant position.” Williams’ own answer: criminality may be observed in parents who, “despite perhaps strongly teaching the child that, say, theft is wrong, nonetheless commit some dishonest acts, such as not telling a shop assistant that they have been given the wrong change; or while renouncing violence they take part either in domestic violence, or use overtly violent methods of punishment.”
  4. Wilson & Herrnstein’s objection (1985). “The theory cannot explain why some individuals might learn criminal behaviour from a peer group while failing to learn non-criminal behaviour from a family (or vice versa).”
  5. It cannot explain impulsive and passionate crime. “This approach cannot explain irrational, impulsive, opportunist or passionate criminals, who would then be acting due to one of those factors rather than as a result of anything they have learnt. The implication is that there are probably some crimes which could never be explained by differential association, which cannot therefore constitute an all-consuming theory.”
  6. It is UNTESTABLE — the key methodological objection. “It is virtually impossible to measure the impact of differential association… the key concepts cannot be reduced to quantitative elements. It is, for example, impossible to make any objective measurement of whether there has been an ‘excess’ of definitions favourable to law breaking… It is hard enough, after the event, to try to reconstruct a person’s thoughts and intentions immediately before they commit a crime, but impossible to go through their whole life and assess the effects on their actions of each input, both for and against crime… one can never be sure exactly what is a ‘definition favourable to law breaking’, nor what is an ‘excess’ of such definitions. How much more of one than the other does it take for that activity to take over? Why, even if it could safely be asserted that an individual has an excess of definitions favourable to law-breaking, are most of that person’s activities lawful?”
  7. Chicken-and-egg / selection problem. “It is sometimes difficult to tell whether certain feelings and motives existed before a particular relationship was begun or only arose afterwards, for example did membership of a gang or group come about because of like thinking at the time, or has membership of the group given rise to that type of behaviour being learnt?”
  8. It leaves no room for the individual. “It seems to assume that these people are vessels into which is poured the definitions for and against law violation: their eventual behaviour is then decided by the balance. This allows very little room for the individual to assess these influences… it allows no room for free will, the personality of the individual, or for differences in types of response.” Williams’ example: “finding a purse full of money might receive the response of an opportunity to better one’s position, or could provoke the response of an opportunity to do someone a good deed.”
  9. It does not explain why associations differ. “The theory is criticised because it does not explain why one person has one set of associations and another person has a different set, although the two may come from similar backgrounds. Sutherland considered this to be a red herring, and asserted that the important point was what was learnt from the associations, not why those associations occur. However, a secondary theory which he expounded, differential social organisation, may help to explain these differences.”
Verbatim — the balanced verdict, DU Case Material pp. 55–56 “Despite these great drawbacks, there have been various studies which go some way towards supporting a causal link between differential association and criminality (eg Jensen, 1972; McCarthy, 1996). In most of these studies the main idea was supported in a fairly basic sense, but the research generally showed that differential association was only ONE of several causes of criminality. Factors such as the effects of a broken home, or of lax supervision within the family, or general emotional insecurity, were also present. Sutherland claims that what differential association helps to explain is WHY SOME PEOPLE WHO SUFFER THESE OTHER FACTORS ARE NOT CRIMINAL WHILST OTHERS ARE.”

17. Beyond Sutherland — reinforcement, modelling, cognition, rational choice and the media

DU Case Material pp. 56–73 Williams’ chapter continues well past Sutherland, and the later material supplies the “modern” content that lifts an answer out of the 1930s.

17.1 Differential reinforcement / operant learning (Burgess & Akers, 1966)

Sutherland said learning occurs by “all the mechanisms involved in any other learning” but never specified them. Burgess and Akers supplied operant conditioning: behaviour is repeated if it is positively reinforced (reward) or negatively reinforced (removal of something unpleasant) and is extinguished if punished. The DU material discusses both external and internal reinforcers:

  • External positive reinforcers: money, goods, status among peers, sexual gratification.
  • Internal positive reinforcers: the material notes the “neurological ‘high’ produced by risky and difficult tasks”, thought to be “associated with the dopamine synapse (the same brain functioning associated with amphetamine, cocaine and heroin use) which when activated gives a good feeling. The strength of this effect varies between people, which would partly explain why an external positive reinforcer is enough for one person to commit an offence but not for another.” (Gove and Wilmoth, 1990.) This is the criminological basis of Katz’s “seductions of crime”.
  • External negative reinforcers: “the possibility of arrest, loss of liberty, fear of injuring someone or oneself, fear of being ostracised by family and friends. Unfortunately such effects are often blunted when such crimes are rarely reported (Tombs and Whyte, 2000)” — the material’s example is corporate food-safety offending, where the press framed prosecutions as persecution: “‘Butchers Could Go Bust in Food Safety Clampdown’ (Sun, 9 April 1997)”.
  • The fatal objection: “This theory suffers from at least one very basic problem: it is tautological. The argument is that behaviour is repeated only if there is strong positive reinforcement for it; but the strong positive reinforcement is recognised only because of the repetition.”

17.2 Social learning theory — Albert Bandura

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 58 “Social learning theory is an extension of differential reinforcement theory in that it builds on the operant learning experience and adds cognitive experiences… Its most well known proponent is Albert Bandura… The motivation associated with the theory includes the reinforcements connected with the operant learning theory as well as reinforcement gained from watching others (vicarious), and the sense of pride and achievement in what we do (self-reinforcement). The basis of the theory is that the learned behaviour is a combination of the physical acts and how to perform them (skills) and the attitudes and mental understanding necessary to the behaviour (including social skills, moral considerations and choice).”

The material identifies four components worth listing in an answer:

  1. Motor skills / modelling. “In order to perform a crime one must have the physical skill… These motor skills tend to be learnt from watching, or being taught by, others. Their presence or absence often dictates the type of crime to be entered into rather than whether a crime will occur… constant aggressive models will not transform the behaviour of a physically weak person unless they are also given a way of carrying out the violence, such as a gun.”
  2. Social skills. “Slabby and Guerra (1988) suggest that criminals learn fewer solutions to interpersonal problems and often fall back on verbal or physical aggression.” The material reports “stronger, though still not conclusive, links between lack of social skills and rape; but the strongest and almost irrefutable links are found between lack of social skills and child sexual abuse”.
  3. Moral disengagement. “People label something differently in order to absolve them of the need to feel moral guilt: a terrorist is punishing the enemy, a burglar is carrying out a job. These euphemisms remove the problems to which full moral reasoning capabilities might otherwise give rise (Bandura, 1986).” Also comparative moral reasoning: “They may see white-collar criminals go very lightly punished for stealing large amounts of money and therefore consider the small amounts they have taken as unimportant.” And denial of the victim: “putting the victims into a group with a derogatory label, often racial, or belittling them personally”; and victim-blaming — “in rape cases, ‘She was asking for it’ and ‘She led me on’, and in property offences ‘He should have been more careful where he left the car’.”
  4. Rational choice. “Rational choice assumes that there is a possibility of committing a crime, an opportunity, and then postulates that the offender weighs up the benefits and the dangers and makes a rational decision whether to commit the crime. Those who see rational choice as a distinct theory (Cornish and Clarke, 1987) argue that social learning theory is largely deterministic… Others argue that the learning processes outlined above merely provide tools and that it is then up to the individual how and when to apply these tools.” Note how this closes the circle back to Beccaria: rational choice theory is classicism reborn inside learning theory.

17.3 Media and crime

Sutherland’s proposition 3 expressly demotes “movies and newspapers”. Williams devotes a long section to testing that claim, and it supplies excellent contemporary material:

  • Bandura’s Bobo doll experiments (1961, 1963) — children who watched an adult model beat an inflatable doll reproduced the aggression, and did so more when the model was rewarded: direct evidence of vicarious reinforcement.
  • Desensitisation, disinhibition and imitation are the three mechanisms usually alleged; against them stand catharsis and the strong methodological objection that laboratory aggression is not crime.
  • Wider media effects — the material’s important point is that the media’s real criminological significance lies less in imitation than in the construction of the crime problem: moral panics (Stanley Cohen’s Folk Devils and Moral Panics, 1972), the exaggeration of stranger violence, the invisibility of corporate harm, and the consequent distortion of penal policy — the phenomenon Garland and Lacey discuss in Topic 1.
Real-world Indian exampleAfter the Delhi gang-rape of December 2012 the volume and tone of reporting produced (a) a genuine and overdue reform — the Justice J.S. Verma Committee Report (Jan 2013) and the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013 — and simultaneously (b) a classic moral panic directed at “stranger danger”, even though NCRB data consistently show that in over 94% of reported rape cases the accused is known to the victim. Media effects thus operate less on the offender (Sutherland was largely right) than on criminal policy — which is exactly Lacey’s two-way-traffic point from Topic 1.

17.4 Practical implications of learning theories

The material ends the chapter with the policy pay-off: if crime is learned, it can be unlearned. Hence cognitive-behavioural programmes, anger-management and social-skills training, token economies in institutions, Reasoning and Rehabilitation programmes, and the requirement that juveniles be separated from adults. This is the theoretical justification for:

Statute s.3(xii)–(xiii) and ss.8, 18 of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 — the principles of institutionalisation as a last resort and of diversion, and the menu of non-custodial orders in s.18(1) (advice/admonition, group counselling, community service, fine, probation, placement in a special home); s.10 and s.12 (a child must never be placed in a police lock-up or jail); s.24 (removal of disqualification attaching to conviction); ss.3 & 4 Probation of Offenders Act, 1958; Rule 77 of the Model Prison Manual 2016 (classification and separation of prisoners); Chapter V of the Model Prisons and Correctional Services Act, 2023. Every one of these provisions is differential-association theory translated into statute: keep the learner away from the teacher.

18. White-collar crime — Sutherland, Geis & Goff, and the Indian picture

Reading 7 of the DU list (Geis & Goff’s introduction to the uncut 1983 edition of Sutherland’s White Collar Crime) is not reprinted in the case material, but Williams flags its importance at the very start of the differential-association chapter: “Sutherland was concerned with white-collar crime. He attempted to explain why and how the upper classes turn to criminality, and to show that the same factors acted upon them as upon most other criminals.”

18.1 The concept

Definition
Sutherland, presidential address to the American Sociological Society, Philadelphia, 27 December 1939, published as White Collar Crime (1949): “a crime committed by a person of respectability and high social status in the course of his occupation.”
Why it destroyed the old theories
If crime were caused by poverty, slums, broken homes or feeble-mindedness, the corporate offender would be inexplicable. Sutherland studied the life-records of the 70 largest US corporations and found 980 adverse decisions against them — an average of 14 each — for restraint of trade, misrepresentation in advertising, patent and trademark infringement, unfair labour practices, rebates and financial fraud. Conclusion: crime is distributed throughout the class structure; only its form and its treatment differ.
The Geis & Goff argument
Their 1983 introduction restores the passages Sutherland’s publishers deleted in 1949 (the actual names of the offending corporations were removed for fear of libel) and shows how the concept was diluted after Sutherland’s death — split into “occupational crime”, “corporate crime”, “economic crime” and “crimes of the powerful”. They stress that Sutherland’s point was definitional and political: the conduct of the powerful is processed by administrative and civil procedures which avoid the stigma of conviction, thereby producing the false statistical picture that crime is a lower-class phenomenon.
The Tappan reply
Paul Tappan, “Who is the Criminal?” (1947): only a person convicted by a criminal court is a criminal; to call unconvicted businessmen criminals is to abandon legal certainty for the criminologist’s private morality. (This debate is set out fully in the Topic-1 notes; cross-refer to it rather than repeat it.)

18.2 How white-collar crime is explained by differential association

The businessman, Sutherland argued, learns in his firm the definitions favourable to violation — “everyone does it”, “the regulation is unreasonable”, “it is a technical breach, not a crime”, “the company, not I, is responsible” — precisely as the slum boy learns them on the street. The learning is segregated from anti-criminal definitions because the firm is isolated from the wider moral community. The DU material for §17.2 above even gives the mechanism: comparative moral reasoning and displacement of responsibility.

19. Durkheim I — mechanical and organic solidarity; crime as NORMAL

DU Case Material pp. 74–75 (Williams) and pp. 82–86 (Vold, Bernard & Snipes) Two of the ten prescribed readings are on Durkheim. Use Williams for the crisp definitions and Vold for the depth and the quotations.

19.1 Who he was and why he wrote

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 82–83 “Emile Durkheim (1858–1917) has been called ‘one of the best known and one of the least understood major social thinkers.’ … The nineteenth century in France was an age of great turmoil generated by the wake of the French Revolution of 1789 and by the rapid industrialization of French society. Speaking of these two ‘Revolutions,’ Nisbet has pointed out that ‘In terms of immediacy and massiveness of impact on human thought and values, it is impossible to find revolutions of comparable magnitude anywhere in human history.’ The writings of the day were filled with a ‘burning sense of society’s sudden, convulsive turn from a path it had followed for millennia’ and a ‘profound intuition of the disappearance of historic values — and with them, age-old securities…’

Sociology had been developed by Auguste Comte in the first half of the century largely in response to the effects of these two revolutions; it was part of a more general effort to construct a rational society out of the ruins of the traditional one. …This regeneration would consist primarily of the reestablishment of social solidarity, which appeared to have substantially disintegrated in French society.”

Career landmarks the material records: born “one year after the death of Comte”; studied under Wilhelm Wundt in Germany; taught “the first French university course in sociology” at Bordeaux in 1887; received in 1892 “the first doctor’s degree in sociology awarded by the University of Paris”; and dominated the discipline from Paris until his death in 1917. 19.2 Mechanical and organic societies

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 74 (Williams) “Durkheim describes how societies begin in simple forms of interaction and are held together by solidarity and likenesses. In such societies, the members have similar aims and roles. These generally homogeneous societies he called MECHANICAL. The growth of societies, together with technical and economic advances, makes the interrelationships more complicated and diverse. When this happens the functions and positions of individuals in societies will vary and each person’s work becomes more specialised. Members of society also become more interdependent. Durkheim called these ORGANIC societies. He viewed these changes in society as being natural and unavoidable, leading to greater happiness for individuals… No one society is wholly mechanical nor wholly organic. In even the most primitive societies there is some division of labour, and in the most complex there is some uniformity.

In both types of society law plays an important role. In mechanical societies, its main function is to enforce the uniformity and the status quo. In organic societies, its main function is to integrate the diverse parts of the society and ensure that they co-exist without problems. Partly due to the different functions of law in the different societies, crime also plays a different part.”

Vold adds the legal vocabulary: in the mechanical society law is repressive/penal (it punishes deviation); in the organic society law becomes restitutive (it restores the relationship — contract, tort, commercial and administrative regulation). The proportion of penal to restitutive law in a legal system is, for Durkheim, an index of where that society stands on the mechanical–organic continuum.

19.3 The collective conscience

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 84–85 “All the uniformity that exists in a society, that is, the ‘totality of social likenesses,’ Durkheim called the collective conscience. … In every society, however, there will always be a degree of diversity… As Durkheim said, ‘There cannot be a society in which the individuals do not differ more or less from the collective type.’

… In its strongest form [the pressure for uniformity] will consist of criminal sanctions. In weaker forms, however, the pressure may consist of designating certain behaviors or beliefs as morally reprehensible or merely in bad taste: ‘If I do not submit to the conventions of society, if in my dress I do not conform to the customs observed in my country and my class, the ridicule I provoke, the social isolation in which I am kept, produce, although in attenuated form, the same effects as a punishment in the strict sense of the word. The constraint is nonetheless efficacious for being indirect.’”

19.4 Why crime is NORMAL — the argument in five steps

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 85 “Durkheim argued that ‘society cannot be formed without our being required to make perpetual and costly sacrifices.’ Those sacrifices… are the price of membership in society… But, more important, these demands are constructed so that it is inevitable that a certain number of people will not fulfill them. The number must be large enough to constitute an identifiable group, but not so large as to include a substantial portion of the society. This enables the large mass of the people… to feel a sense of moral superiority, identifying themselves as good and righteous, and opposing themselves to the morally inferior transgressors… It is this sense of superiority, of goodness and righteousness, which Durkheim saw as the primary source of the social solidarity. Thus criminals play an important role in the maintenance of the social solidarity.

The punishment of criminals also plays a role. When the dictates of the collective conscience are violated, society responds with repressive sanctions not so much for retribution or deterrence, but because without them those who are making the ‘perpetual and costly sacrifices’ would become severely demoralized. For example, when a person who has committed a serious crime is released with only a slap on the wrist, the average, law-abiding citizens may become terribly upset. They may feel they are playing the game by the rules, and so everyone else should too. …

Crime itself is normal in society because there is no clearly marked dividing line between behaviors considered criminal and those considered morally reprehensible or merely in bad taste. If there is a decrease in behaviors designated as criminal, then there may be a tendency to move behaviors previously designated as morally reprehensible into the criminal category. For example, not every type of unfair transfer of property is considered stealing. But if there is a decrease in the traditional forms of burglary and robbery, there then may be an associated increase in the tendency to define various forms of white-collar deception as crime. … Society moves them into the crime category because criminal sanctions are the strongest tool available to maintain social solidarity.”

The society of saints — the passage every examiner recognises

“Imagine a society of saints, a perfect cloister of exemplary individuals. Crimes, properly so called, will there be unknown; but faults which appear venial to the layman will create there the same scandal that the ordinary offense does in ordinary consciousnesses. If, then, this society has the power to judge and punish, it will define these acts as criminal and will treat them as such. For the same reason, the perfect and upright man judges his smallest failings with a severity that the majority reserve for acts more truly in the nature of an offense.” Durkheim, The Rules of Sociological Method — DU Case Material pp. 85–86.
Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 86, the conclusion of the argument “Thus a society without crime is impossible. If all the behaviors that are presently defined as criminal no longer occurred, new behaviors would be placed in the crime category. Crime, then, is inevitable because there is an inevitable diversity of behavior in society. …

‘Let us make no mistake. To classify crime among the phenomena of normal sociology is not merely to say that it is an inevitable, although regrettable, phenomenon, due to the incorrigible wickedness of men; it is to affirm that it is a FACTOR IN PUBLIC HEALTH, AN INTEGRAL PART OF ALL SOCIETIES.’

The abnormal or pathological state of society would be one in which there was NO crime. A society that had no crime would be one in which the constraints of the collective conscience were so rigid that no one could oppose them. In this type of situation crime would be eliminated, but so would the possibility of progressive social change. Social change is usually introduced by opposing the constraints of the collective conscience, and those who do so are frequently declared to be criminals. Thus Socrates and Jesus were declared criminals, as were Mahatma Gandhi and George Washington. The leaders of the union movement in the 1920s and 1930s were criminalized, as were the leaders of the civil rights movement of the 1960s. If the demands of the collective conscience had been so rigidly enforced that no crime could exist, then these movements would have been impossible also.

Thus crime is the price society pays for the possibility of progress. As Durkheim wrote: ‘To make progress, individual originality must be able to express itself. In order that the originality of the idealist whose dreams transcend his century may find expression, it is necessary that the originality of the criminal, who is below the level of his time, shall also be possible. One does not occur without the other.’

In a similar way individual growth cannot occur in a child unless it is possible for that child to misbehave… a child who never did anything wrong would be pathologically overcontrolled.”
Real-world Indian examples of Durkheim’s four functions of crime (1) Boundary maintenance: the public outrage and candle-light marches after the 2012 Delhi gang rape dramatised and reaffirmed the moral boundary, exactly as Durkheim predicted — and produced the CrLA 2013. (2) Adaptive / innovative function (crime as the herald of change): Gandhi’s Dandi march of 1930 was a deliberate violation of the Salt Act; the framers of the Constitution were, to the Raj, criminals. So too the petitioners in Navtej Singh Johar (2018) were, until that morning, offenders under s.377 IPC. (3) Safety-valve / tension release: Kingsley Davis’s classic argument about prostitution; in India, the toleration of minor gambling at Diwali. (4) Warning-light function: a sudden rise in a particular offence signals a malfunctioning institution — the spike in cyber-fraud after demonetisation and the UPI boom told the State that its regulatory architecture had not kept pace, producing the CERT-In directions of 2022 and the DPDP Act, 2023.

20. Durkheim II — ANOMIE as a pathological state in organic societies

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 87 “To the extent that a society is mechanical, it derives its solidarity from pressure for conformity against the diversity of its members. The criminalizing of some behaviors is a normal and necessary part of this pressure. But to the extent that a society is organic, the function of law is to regulate the interactions of the various parts of the whole. If this regulation is inadequate, there can result a variety of social maladies, including crime. Durkheim called the state of inadequate regulation ANOMIE.

Durkheim first introduced this concept in The Division of Labor in Society, where he argued that the industrialization of French society… had destroyed the traditional solidarity based on uniformity. But this industrialization had been so rapid that the society had not yet been able to evolve sufficient mechanisms to regulate its transactions. Periodic cycles of overproduction followed by economic slowdown indicated that the relations between producers and consumers were ineffectively regulated. Strikes and labor violence indicated that the relations between workers and employers were unresolved. The alienation of the individual worker and the sense that the division of labor was turning people into mere ‘cogs in the wheel’ indicated that the relation of the individual to work was inadequately defined.”

20.1 The three forms of the abnormal division of labour (Williams’ version)

DU Case Material p. 75 “He used a number of examples, most of which arise from an unbalanced division of labour. These can largely be fitted into three categories. The first was a combination of financial and industrial conflict. The second was rigid and unnatural class divisions, such that the oppressed may rebel. The third and final situation he mentions is where there is an abnormal division of labour, such that workers become alienated from their jobs and become disinterested in them.”

And crucially: “In each of these three examples, before anomie can be said to exist the major factor which needs to be present is a financial or industrial crisis — this may be a depression as experienced in the 1930s, or it may arise from a time of unrealistic and precarious prosperity, or from an overly fast industrial growth. For later writers, anomie could exist without the prior need for such an upheaval. But for Durkheim upheavals were necessary.”

20.2 The argument from Suicide — animals, appetites and the moral limit

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 87–88 “In [Le Suicide, 1897] he statistically analyzed data that showed that the suicide rate tends to increase sharply both in periods of economic decline and economic growth. Whereas suicide in a time of economic decline might be easily understood, the key question is why suicide would increase in a time of prosperity. …

The theory began with a comparison of animal and human nature. In animals, Durkheim argued, the physical body naturally limits appetites. Thus, animals with full stomachs and safe warm places to sleep will feel quite satisfied. But humans have active imaginations that allow them to feel dissatisfied even when their physical needs are met. In humans, satisfying some wants and needs tends to awaken new wants and needs, so that ‘the more one has, the more one wants.’ Thus… human appetites are naturally unlimited.

The only mechanism that can limit human appetites, according to Durkheim, is human SOCIETY. Societies create moral rules about what people in various social positions can reasonably expect to acquire:
‘As a matter of fact, at every moment of history there is a dim perception, in the moral consciousness of societies, of the respective value of different social services, the relative reward due each, and the consequent degree of comfort appropriate on the average to workers in each occupation… Under this pressure, each in his sphere vaguely realizes the extreme limit set to his ambitions and aspires to nothing beyond… Thus, an end and goal are set to the passions.’
There are various situations in which these societal rules may weaken or even break down, but Durkheim focused on situations of rapid social change, including those in which the society goes into an economic recession or depression:
‘In the case of economic disasters, indeed, something like a declassification occurs which suddenly casts certain individuals into a lower state than their previous one. Then they must reduce their requirements, restrain their needs, learn greater self-control… So they are not adjusted to the condition forced on them, and its very prospect is intolerable.’
…More importantly, he argued that something similar happened in times of rapid economic expansion:
‘It is the same if the source of the crisis is an abrupt growth of power and wealth. Then, truly, as the conditions of life are changed, the standard according to which needs were regulated can no longer remain the same… The scale is upset; but a new scale cannot be immediately improvised. Time is required for the public conscience to reclassify men and things. So long as the social forces thus freed have not regained equilibrium, their respective values are unknown and so all regulation is lacking for a time…’
He then made the surprising argument that anomie (or the deregulation of appetites) would be WORSE in times of prosperity than in times of depression, since prosperity stimulates the appetites just at the time when the restraints on those appetites have broken down:
‘With increased prosperity desires increase. At the very moment when traditional rules have lost their authority, the richer prize offered these appetites stimulates them and makes them more exigent and impatient of control. The state of de-regulation or anomy is thus further heightened by passions being less disciplined, precisely when they need more disciplining.’
Durkheim went on to argue that French society, over the previous 100 years, had deliberately destroyed the traditional sources of regulation for human appetites. Religion had almost completely lost its influence over both workers and employers. Traditional occupational groups, such as the guilds, had been destroyed. Government adhered to a policy of laissez-faire… As a result human appetites were no longer curbed. This freedom of appetites was the driving force behind the French industrial revolution, but it also created a chronic state of anomie, with its attendant high rate of suicide.”

Williams summarises the same idea in one examinable sentence: “Durkheim viewed anomie as a state of lawlessness existing at times of abrupt social change, and affecting in particular the state of ‘normlessness’ which exists when the insatiable desires of humans are no longer controlled by society.” (p. 75) And: “No living being can be happy or even exist unless his needs are sufficiently proportioned to his means.”

Durkheim’s four types of suicide (useful for a short note, and a favourite of examiners who like precision): egoistic (too little integration — the isolated individual), altruistic (too much integration — the soldier, the sati, the suicide bomber), anomic (too little regulation — the ruined or suddenly enriched speculator), and fatalistic (too much regulation — the slave, the over-disciplined prisoner). Note the Indian resonance of the altruistic type: Durkheim expressly discussed the sati, which is now dealt with by the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987 Statute, discussed in the Srivastava reading at §34 below.

20.3 The criticism Williams records

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 75 “Durkheim’s ideas have, to an extent, been attacked by some writers who claim that crime rates did not increase over the period of the French revolution or during the industrial revolution, periods depicted by Durkheim as being in a state of upheaval and anomie. The discrepancy in the figures arose because Durkheim based his theories on rates of SUICIDE, which he found to be increasing, and ASSUMED that crime rates were similarly affected. Sainsbury (1955) also found that suicide was related to social disorganisation, but crime was not. However, Durkheim’s main thesis was that crime is associated with breakdown of social norms and rules giving rise to an absence of social control. There are two elements to this: the first is the breakdown of regulations, rules and informal limits, undermining confidence in the social structure; the second is that this structural problem leads to psychological feelings of isolation. The overall disorder and disorganisation, social and personal, shifts behaviour in the direction of crime. There may still be substance in this.”

21. Assessing Durkheim’s theory — Erikson, Blumstein and modernization

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 89 “One of the problems with assessing his theory is that he predicted that different things would happen at different times. Specifically he argued that: (1) the punishment of crime would remain fairly stable in mechanical societies, independent of changes in the extent of criminal behavior; (2) as those societies made the transition to organic societies in the process of modernization, a greater variety of behaviors would be tolerated, punishments would become less violent as their purpose changed from repression to restitution, and there would be a vast expansion of ‘functional’ law to regulate the interactions of the emerging organic society; and (3) in organic societies, the extent of criminal behavior would increase during periods of rapid social change.”
Erikson’s test — Wayward Puritans
“Erikson reformulated Durkheim’s theory about the stability of punishment in mechanical societies, based on a study of the Puritan colony in seventeenth-century Massachusetts. This society had a relatively constant level of punishment throughout the century despite three ‘crime waves’ attributed to Antinomians, Quakers, and witches. Erikson concluded: ‘When a community calibrates its control machinery to handle a certain volume of deviant behavior it tends to adjust its … legal … definitions of the problem in such a way that this volume is realized.’”
Blumstein’s stability-of-punishment hypothesis
“Blumstein and his colleagues… examined imprisonment rates in the United States from 1924 to 1974, in Canada from 1880 to 1959, and in Norway from 1880 to 1964, arguing that these rates remained stable… and that the stability was maintained by adjusting the types of behaviors that resulted in imprisonment. Later studies either failed to find a similar effect or have criticized the research methods… More recently, the explosion of incarceration in the United States associated with the ‘get tough’ era has clearly demonstrated that punishment in the United States is no longer ‘stable,’ if it ever was. For example, before 1970, the imprisonment rate… had generally remained somewhere around 100 prisoners for every 100,000 people.” (It now exceeds 500.)
Crime and modernization
The material’s conclusion is nuanced: one strand of research links modernization to crime through opportunity — “when people own more property that is both valuable and portable, and when they frequently are away from their property and cannot personally guard it, then property crime is likely to increase” (this is routine activity theory). The other strand is Durkheim’s own: “modernization is linked to crime through the breakdown of social norms and rules… he associated crime with the absence of social controls.”
The elegant correction
“It may be that Durkheim’s argument itself is correct but that Durkheim was wrong in assuming that pre-modern societies had strong social control and little crime. It now seems likely that they had LITTLE social control and a GREAT DEAL of violent crime. The long-term decline in violent crime may then be explained by the continuously increasing level of social controls associated with increasing modernization.” (DU Case Material p. 93.) This is the “civilising process” thesis of Norbert Elias, empirically supported by Manuel Eisner’s European homicide series (≈35 homicides per 100,000 in the 14th century falling to ≈1 today).

22. Robert K. Merton — anomie, strain and the five modes of adaptation

22.1 How Merton changed the concept

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 75–76 (Williams) “After Durkheim, the most famous criminological writer on anomie is the American Robert K. Merton. He drew on Durkheim’s ideas to try to explain the crime problem in the United States of America. Instead of centering problems of anomie on the insatiable desires of human beings, he explains them as something which may exist when desires and needs, though limited, still go beyond what could be satisfied in socially acceptable ways. … The difference is that, for Durkheim, the moral norms to be upheld or enforced regulate the INDIVIDUAL’S DESIRES, whereas for Merton, they would regulate and control the individual’s willingness to use UNACCEPTABLE MEANS to achieve those desires. Merton therefore argues that society will not be anomic if its members only use legitimate means of advancement, even if their desires are totally unrestricted; it is the relationship between desires and the means of achieving those desires which is fundamental [Merton (1949)]. This link between desires and means has led to his theory being called a STRAIN theory; one in which everyone is pressured to succeed, but those who are unable or least likely to succeed by legitimate means are under most strain to use illegitimate or illegal opportunities.

Durkheim said the individual’s desires are derived from WITHIN the person; in Merton’s view the desires of individuals are largely defined by SOCIETY. Each culture and society has different elements which it considers worth striving for. In America and much of the Western world, this is wealth and, through wealth, material possessions. Wealth is encouraged far beyond its own usefulness, and great wealth is equated with personal value and worth. Those who lack money are looked down on… Furthermore, Merton argues that Western cultures, and the American culture in particular, far from limiting desires actually encourages everyone to seek absolute wealth. Everyone is told that further enrichment is possible and that they should all strive towards it. If they do not, they are considered lazy and of less worth (Durkheim would have considered that this alone renders the culture anomic).

Merton maintains that the healthy society lays down accepted MEANS of achieving the ends or goals. In Western cultures, the means… are supposed to be through hard and honest toil, not through theft and fraud. However, the latter means may well be more efficient. If society is to remain healthy, therefore, it is important that participating in the accepted means carries some reward… The philosophy behind this thesis might well be: ‘It’s not whether you win that matters, it’s how you play the game.’ However, if the emphasis is on reaching [the goal by any] means [and that] is still rewarded with prestige, power and social status… the regulatory norms are so undermined that many people either withdraw their support for the rules or withhold their support and live by expediency.”

22.2 Why Merton located strain in the lower class

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 77 “Merton applied his ideas to provide an explanation of the pattern of crime as revealed by the official US statistics… Each of them show a heavy criminal rate among the lower or working class of society, and a low rate among the upper, moneyed or powerful classes. Whether or not Merton simply accepted the correctness of the official statistics is debatable… Criminality arose, not necessarily because of discrepancies between the goals and the approved means of achieving those goals, but because all the members of that society were led to believe that there was EQUALITY OF OPPORTUNITY. In practice, there were sharp constraints on such purported equality. The consequent feelings of UNFAIRNESS could lead to criminality. Since the lower classes suffered most from educational and occupational discrimination, they were least likely to attain the ‘American Dream’ through the legitimate opportunity structures. … He argues that anomie becomes the DIFFERENTIAL APPLICATION OF OPPORTUNITY rather than an application of social controls or norms; it is a strain theory. Frustration with the system, or possible economic necessity, gives rise to strain and drives these people to resort to criminality.”

22.3 The five modes of adaptation — the “means–end theory”

In private, that same individual may use drugs, fitting into a retreatist and necessarily criminal response; and in the public sphere he or she may fight for a political ideal different from that presently existing, and involving a rebellious response…

Merton stated that his theory of anomie, or ‘strain theory’ as it is sometimes called, is only intended to explain certain types of criminality, particularly STREET CRIMINALITY. This, to an extent, explains why he centres on crimes committed by the lower classes and crimes which may exhibit a lack of opportunity.”

22.4 Messner & Rosenfeld — “institutional anomie”

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 79 “Recently a large part of this thesis has been given renewed attention in the work of Messner and Rosenfeld (1994), who argue that crime (in America) results from the culture of prioritising wealth, which is heavily supported by State institutions which give power to the economy. The cultural pull is the same as that marked out by Merton, but the structural analysis is rather different. For Messner and Rosenfeld, the State structure and social institutions (family, health and education) are designed to give strength to the economy. The American people are socialised to back these structures as supports for the economy — so education is not prized as an end in itself, for learning or personal development, but merely as a means to better paid employment. The culture and institutions centre on money and set values which support the free market. This encourages people to use the most efficient means available to them to attain the desired goals… Messner and Rosenfeld argue that other societies which are less focused on purely monetary values suffer less crime.”

22.5 The policy implications Merton entails

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 79 “The policy implications of these approaches are largely outside the purview of the criminal justice system. They require wider political objectives — to provide greater educational and job training opportunities; to build up the possibilities for people to obtain jobs with good wages and reasonable hours; to encourage cultural [ideals] which focus on community and respect for humanity; and to ensure that more weight is given to the obligations of citizenship (so far as this involves respect for others) — being considered alongside individual freedom and rights.”

23. Durkheim v. Merton — the two differences

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 79–80 “Durkheim and Merton did not see things in entirely the same light. The former places heavy emphasis upon a condition of ‘normlessness’ arising out of abrupt change; the latter sees anomie as an ENDEMIC condition. Merton says this can exist at any time in any society, as long as the factors mentioned above exist. A society can be basically stable while part, even a large part, of it is anomic.

…There are two main differences. The first is the more fundamental. Durkheim states that the desires of individuals are NATURAL and FIXED, the level of criminal behaviour and of anomie is decided by the efficiency with which these desires are restrained, and that they are most likely to crumble and generate crime in periods of rapid change. Merton, on the other hand, says that SOCIETY, NOT THE INDIVIDUAL, sets the desires and goals, and that the same society also sets the acceptable means of achieving the ends. If rewards are only bestowed for obtaining the ends, the restraining means become weakened, encouraging the use of unacceptable and illegal means.

The second, and more practical difference, is that Durkheim talks about a WHOLE SOCIETY being anomic. Merton would consider that the condition only affects CERTAIN PARTS of the society (i.e., those parts which appear in the official crime statistics), and generally speaking those parts are drawn from the lower classes. Merton in effect focuses on the AVAILABILITY OF LEGITIMATE OPPORTUNITIES to achieve wealth. These opportunities exist for the higher classes, but not for the lower classes. The absence of such opportunities gives rise to strain and therefore criminality in the lower classes.”

23.2 Anomie applied to recession, unemployment and the media

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 80 “Most recently, anomie has been used as an explanation for the increase in crime during times of unemployment and recession. This last possibility fulfils Durkheim’s need for a major upheaval before anomie can exist. Its effects are reinforced if the moral norms promoting acceptance of the rules of society appear to be missing. Some see this absence in the way the mass media, particularly TV, churn out the ideal of a MATERIALISTIC EXISTENCE as the norm to which all right-thinking members of society should aspire, even though few will ever achieve such a financial ‘nirvana’ through legitimate means. … Obviously, not all members of society will become deviant: some may resign themselves to a less comfortable existence; some may achieve the dream… But for others the problem of anomie might be resolved by a resort to criminal conduct.

Merton concentrates more on the opportunity structures. He argues that even in a recession, people are told that there are opportunities and that they are fairly administered. These messages clash with the experience of many, especially the unemployed who have little realistic chance of achieving financial or occupational success… This is especially likely if they view their failure as a fault of the unfair operation of the system, rather than a reflection of their own ability.

Although Merton saw this phenomenon as confined to the lower classes, there is no compelling reason why this should be the case. Each corporate body has a goal, usually financial, to achieve (i.e., high and ever-increasing profit). In times of recession, it is more difficult to achieve these goals, and the pressure to use unacceptable and criminal means is much stronger. The more the company, the firm or its executives are pressurised by the recession, the more compelling the temptation to offend by such things as TAX EVASION, VAT FRAUD, and COST-CUTTING IN RELATION TO HEALTH AND SAFETY.”
This last paragraph is a gift. It allows you to use strain theory to explain white-collar and corporate crime as well as street crime — thereby answering the standard criticism that Merton is a theory of the poor. Deploy it in any question that pairs anomie with white-collar crime, or that asks about the 2008 crash, the Satyam fraud or GST evasion.

23.3 Criticisms of Merton — the checklist

  1. Over-reliance on official statistics, which systematically under-record the crimes of the powerful — hence the theory “finds” crime where the statistics look (Williams’ criticism of the conformity category).
  2. Assumes a value consensus about the goal of monetary success. Conflict and subcultural theorists deny it.
  3. Cannot explain non-utilitarian crime — vandalism, gang fighting, hate crime, most sexual offences, most homicide. Albert Cohen’s central objection: delinquency is often “non-utilitarian, malicious and negativistic”.
  4. Does not explain who innovates. Most strained people conform; the theory cannot say which strained individual will offend (the same criticism Williams makes of differential association).
  5. Ignores the individual’s subjective perception of deprivation — corrected by Agnew’s general strain theory and by the concept of relative deprivation (Runciman; Lea and Young).
  6. Says nothing about the law-making process — who decides that the goal shall be wealth and that these means shall be legitimate? This is the conflict theorists’ entry point (§27).

24. After Merton — subculture and the later strain theories

The Williams chapter prescribed is titled “Anomie, Strain and Juvenile Subculture”, so the subcultural extensions are squarely within the syllabus.

Albert K. Cohen (1955), Delinquent Boys
Working-class boys are judged by a “middle-class measuring rod” in school (ambition, deferred gratification, politeness, respect for property) and inevitably fail. The result is status frustration. The collective solution is a delinquent subculture which, by reaction formation, inverts middle-class values: the subculture is “non-utilitarian, malicious and negativistic”, prizing “short-run hedonism” and “group autonomy”. Crime here is not about money at all — which is Cohen’s correction of Merton.
Cloward & Ohlin (1960), Delinquency and Opportunity
Merton only noticed blocked legitimate opportunity; there is also differential access to illegitimate opportunity (this is Sutherland grafted onto Merton). Three subcultures follow: the criminal subculture (in stable neighbourhoods with an established adult criminal hierarchy — apprenticeship in theft and fraud); the conflict subculture (in disorganised areas with no such ladder — violence and turf gangs supply status); and the retreatist subculture (the “double failures”, who succeed at neither — drugs).
Walter B. Miller (1958)
Denies the subculture is a reaction to middle-class values at all: the lower class has its own long-standing focal concerns — trouble, toughness, smartness, excitement, fate and autonomy — and delinquency is simply conformity to that culture. Compare Sellin’s culture conflict (§27).
Matza (1964), Delinquency and Drift
Rejects the determinism of all of them. Delinquents are not committed to a deviant subculture; they drift between conformity and delinquency, freed episodically by the techniques of neutralization (Sykes & Matza, 1957) and by “subterranean values” which the respectable world itself holds.
Robert Agnew (1992), General Strain Theory
Rescues strain theory by making it social-psychological and class-neutral. Strain arises from three sources: (1) failure to achieve positively valued goals; (2) removal of positively valued stimuli (death, divorce, loss of a job); and (3) presentation of negative stimuli (abuse, bullying, humiliation, discrimination, harassment). Strain produces negative affect — chiefly anger — and crime is one coping mechanism. GST explains female offending, middle-class offending and violent offending, all of which defeated Merton.
Relative deprivation and left realism
Runciman; Lea and Young (What Is To Be Done About Law and Order?, 1984): it is not absolute poverty but relative deprivation — the felt injustice of one’s position compared with a reference group — that generates discontent, which, combined with marginalisation and the absence of political solutions, produces a subculture of crime. This is the direct answer to the objection that the poorest societies are not the most criminal.
Indian application of subcultural theory Cloward & Ohlin’s three subcultures are visible in Indian criminology: the criminal subculture in the organised gangs of Mumbai’s underworld and the inter-generational transmission of skills documented in the “criminal tribes” literature and in the dacoit gangs of the Chambal; the conflict subculture in urban gang violence and in the political-cadre violence of some States; the retreatist subculture in the drug economies of Punjab (the “chitta” epidemic) and the North-East, which the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985 addresses through s.27 (consumption), s.39 (release for treatment) and s.64A (immunity from prosecution for addicts who volunteer for treatment) Statute — an implicitly Cloward–Ohlin response, in that it treats the retreatist as a casualty rather than an enemy.

25. The Chicago School — social disorganisation and the ecology of crime

The syllabus heading is “Social Disorganization Theories – Durkheim, Merton”, and the phrase “social disorganization” is itself the Chicago School’s. Sutherland’s objection to the phrase (§14) makes sense only if you know what he was objecting to.

Park & Burgess — the concentric zone model (1925)
The city is an ecological system. Burgess mapped Chicago as five concentric rings: I the central business district; II the zone in transition (decaying housing bought up for industrial expansion, cheapest rents, newest immigrants); III workingmen’s homes; IV the residential zone; V the commuter zone.
Shaw & McKay (1942), Juvenile Delinquency and Urban Areas
Plotted 56,000 juvenile court records over 30 years and found that Zone II always had the highest delinquency rate — regardless of which ethnic group happened to be living there. As each immigrant group moved outward its delinquency rate fell, and the incoming group inherited the high rate. Therefore the cause is the PLACE, not the PEOPLE. Their three characteristics of the delinquency area: physical dilapidation, poverty and population turnover/heterogeneity, which together prevent the formation of effective informal social controls and permit the cultural transmission of delinquent traditions from one cohort of boys to the next.
The policy child — the Chicago Area Project
Shaw’s own programme (from 1932): community committees, recreation, mediation with courts and police, indigenous leadership — the ancestor of every community-based crime-prevention scheme, including India’s community policing experiments (Kerala’s Janamaithri Suraksha, Maharashtra’s Mohalla committees after the 1992–93 riots, and the “Friends of Police” schemes) and of the Beat Officer system in the model police legislation.
Modern descendants
Collective efficacy (Sampson, Raudenbush & Earls, 1997) — social cohesion plus willingness to intervene for the common good — is the best-validated modern measure; and broken windows (Wilson & Kelling, 1982) — signs of disorder invite further disorder — which underlies the Indian emphasis on street lighting, CCTV and “safety audits” of public space (the Safe City projects funded under the Nirbhaya Fund).
Indian evidenceThe zone in transition has an exact Indian analogue in the unauthorised colonies and resettlement colonies at the edge of large cities, and in the pavement and slum settlements whose insecurity of tenure the Supreme Court considered in Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation, AIR 1986 SC 180. NCRB city data have consistently shown crime concentrated in a small number of police-station areas within each metropolis — an ecological finding of exactly the Shaw–McKay type. The policy response — the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana, slum in-situ rehabilitation, and the Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act, 2014 (which decriminalises and regularises vending) Statute — is Chicago-School criminology in Indian dress: change the place, not the people.

26. Psychodynamic theory I — Freud’s psychic structures (Ian Craib)

DU Case Material p. 94 Reading 8 is not a criminology text at all: it is a chapter from a critical introduction to psychoanalysis. The examiner expects you to (a) state Freud’s model accurately from this reading and (b) yourself make the link to crime. §26 does the first; §27 does the second.

26.1 Why a model of the mind was needed at all

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 94 “Unconscious processes are timeless, do not obey the laws of formal logic, and involve processes of constant re-symbolization. To live in the world only with an unconscious would be impossible — we would be gibbering wrecks taking no account of other people except insofar as they could satisfy our needs. You might think that you know people like this, but they are very mild versions of what somebody who really acted entirely according to impulse would be like. The impulses have to be tamed, and the existence of the unconscious and repression is already a step towards that; it implies the development of other levels of, and a split in, the psyche. It must be remembered that as far as psychoanalysis is concerned, we are not simple agents, but constantly divided in (and against) ourselves.

Wollheim (1971) points out that Freud was not content with assuming that there is a simple division between the conscious and unconscious levels of the mind; that view does not enable us to grasp the dynamic processes of repression and of the way the repressed can reappear in consciousness. The division… between unconscious, preconscious and conscious levels was insufficient… and it was in response to these complexities that he revised his earlier conceptualization… in his paper on ‘The Ego and the Id’ (1923)… The development… was part of a reorganization of the conception of the psyche into three levels; the now well-known ID, EGO and SUPEREGO. For the time being I will refer to these as ‘structures’ but they might also be referred to as ‘internal agencies’, since they are involved in dynamic relationship with each other.”
Topographical v. structural models. Freud’s first (topographical) model divided the mind into conscious, preconscious and unconscious. His second (structural) model, from The Ego and the Id (1923), divides it into id, ego and superego. The Craib reading is about the second. Do not confuse them in the answer — and note Craib’s point that the id, ego and superego each have both conscious and unconscious dimensions, so the two models are not simply layers of one another.

26.2 THE ID — “the it”

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 94–95 “Freud did not himself use the Latin terms when he was developing his theory; they were introduced by his translator in the hope that using them would bring psychoanalysis greater respectability in the medical profession (Bettelheim 1983). The German words translate as ‘it’, ‘I’ and ‘over-I’, and these do in fact convey a better commonsense understanding of what Freud was on about. …

What we get from the ‘it’ rather than ‘id’ is the sense of something inside that can push in one direction or another against our will, something that [is] beyond our control and that can reassert itself against all attempts to control or divert it. In Christopher Badcock’s words: ‘The id is an impersonal, chaotic inferno of primitive drives and dynamically repressed material which constantly [agitates] for expression. It goads the ego with pain and [seduces] it with pleasure’ (Badcock 1988: 111).

I can experience my id in a number of ways, through feelings the origin of which I do not understand and that I often try to avoid by trying to find some reason for them in the outside world: if I am angry, then it is because something or somebody makes me so. My physical reactions, the tension, the need to keep moving, jumping up from my chair and pacing the room, these are responses to messages from the id that demand some satisfaction, some release… The id is basically but not wholly unconscious; I am not aware of what goes on there but I am aware of the effects of what goes on there. … Sometimes the discovery of our desires can be frightening.

The id, then, is a sort of chaotic power-house of the personality, pushing energy through the system towards the outside world, and demanding immediate satisfaction. The difficulty is that immediate satisfaction is not available, even on a simple economic level. We live in a world of comparative scarcity: if we are hungry, we do not have access to an inexhaustible supply of food — we have to find it, or work for it or for the money to buy it; and if we sought immediate satisfaction for all our sexual desires, the species would disappear very quickly. The Marxist philosopher Herbert Marcuse (1969) constructed a sort of speculative utopia where we could allow our sexual instinct far more free-ranging satisfactions than we do at present, and it is interesting that a major criticism is that there would be no place in such a world for the sacrifices involved in raising children (Chodorow 1985).”

Criminological translation: the id operates on the pleasure principle — immediate, unreasoning, amoral gratification. It is the reservoir of libido (the life instinct, Eros) and, in Freud’s later writing, of Thanatos, the death instinct, expressed outwards as aggression and destruction. Every crime, on this view, is an id-impulse that the other two agencies failed to manage.

26.3 THE EGO — “the I”

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 95–96 “We are then caught between inner demands for immediate satisfaction, and the demands of the outer world, which threatens us as much as does the inner world. The ego is what develops in the attempt to maintain a balance between the two. The most effective way in which it can deal with the internal pressures of the id is repression… if repression is part of the work of the ego, the ego cannot be assigned simply to the conscious or the preconscious. Like the id, it has both conscious and unconscious aspects.

The ego or the ‘I’ grows out of the struggle between the demands of the internal drives and the demands of the outside world, in order to mediate between them. It originates in identifications with objects that were ‘cathected’ by the id, and then lost. Another way of putting this is that the ego is formed by LOSS of intensely loved objects (the womb, the breast perhaps), and by identifying with [and] taking in the lost object as part of oneself. We shall see that the theory of loss and mourning plays an important part in psychoanalysis, and we might say at this stage that a central part of the self and my sense of self is built upon an early and primitive mourning process.

The conscious element of the ‘I’ is that sense of personhood that we have when we use the pronoun, the sense of a reasonably coherent agency that can result for periods when we have the conflicting internal and external demands in an approximate balance. I would emphasize that this only occurs for periods; the psyche is a combination of dynamic processes and an absolute, stable balance or integration is never achieved. …

The unconscious part of the ego, made up of internalized objects that were once vital for the infant’s survival, is the repressing and defending mechanism and what one might call the internal negotiator between the outside and the inside… But as Juliet Mitchell (1974) points out in no uncertain terms, the ego is the originating point for ALL ANXIETY. One might say that anxiety is the price we pay for possessing a self. … all this has to be achieved in the context of the conflict between the libido and the death instinct. Moreover… the super-ego becomes another important force in generating anxiety…”

Criminological translation: the ego operates on the reality principle. It is the rational planner; it does not decide what is right, only what is possible and safe. A strong ego with a weak superego produces the calculating, guilt-free offender (roughly, the “psychopath” of the older literature); a weak ego produces the impulsive offender who cannot postpone gratification — which is, in different language, precisely Gottfredson & Hirschi’s “low self-control”.

26.4 THE SUPER-EGO — “the over-I”

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 96–97 “The colloquial term that Freud used for the super-ego is, as we have noted, the ‘over-I’, [an] internal agency that, as it were, lays down the law for the rest of the personality, often providing a critical commentary on a person’s action. It is tempting to see it as a conscience, which, through the internalization of the parents’, particularly, for Freud, the father’s, prohibitions, takes in wider social prohibitions. In fact I think Freud was trying to get at a much deeper process. Certainly our conscious experiences of judging ourselves and giving ourselves ideals, and of GUILT when we feel we have done something wrong, are connected with the super-ego. But they also have important unconscious dimensions that make the super-ego much more than another term for a simple process of socialization.

The super-ego is formed from the same sort of psychic material as is the ego and in order to REINFORCE the latter in its task of managing the drives. However, there is what on the surface is an unexpected connection between the id and the super-ego, because the latter is formed, according to Freud, from the introjection of objects in which the id has invested most energy; the principle seems to be that the more powerful the desire for the object, the greater the force needed to repress that desire, and that force is attached to the introjected object and turned against the id. It is important to think of this as an internal, psychological process rather than something that occurs primarily as a response to outside forces. The strength of and force behind a person’s self-critical and self-controlling functions is NOT NECESSARILY RELATED DIRECTLY to the strength and force of parental prohibition. Some believe that, if anything, the opposite is true — IF PARENTAL PROHIBITION IS WEAK, THE INTERNAL CONTROL HAS TO BE THAT MUCH STRONGER for the ego to cope with the world.

Freud believed the super-ego appears around the age of FOUR, at the end of the oedipal stage. …for Freud it is probably true to say that the major component of the super-ego is paternal… It is apparent, however, that we can find severe self-criticism and a powerful sense of guilt in much younger children, and the understanding of how this happens marks one of the major theoretical divisions between Freud and Melanie Klein.”

27. Psychodynamic theory II — Freud applied to crime, and its Indian legal expression

27.1 The three psychoanalytic routes into crime

1. The under-developed super-ego
The commonest account. Inadequate or absent identification with a parent — through death, desertion, rejection, or (in Freud’s terms) a failure to resolve the Oedipal conflict — leaves the child without an internalised conscience. The id is then restrained only by fear of detection. This is the offender August Aichhorn called latent delinquent in Wayward Youth (1925).
2. The OVER-DEVELOPED super-ego — the “criminal from a sense of guilt”
Freud’s most counter-intuitive and most examinable idea (“Some Character-Types Met with in Psycho-Analytic Work”, 1916): the offender is oppressed by an unconscious guilt whose source (the Oedipal wish) he cannot know; he commits a crime in order to attach the guilt to something real and to be punished. Punishment brings relief. This inverts the whole deterrent logic of the classical school: for such an offender, punishment is a reward. Hence the “careless” criminal who leaves obvious clues.
3. The deviant super-ego
The super-ego is fully formed but has internalised criminal parental values — the child of a professional thief who feels guilty when he does not steal. Note that this is Freud converging on Sutherland: the content of conscience is learnt from intimate personal groups.
Maternal deprivation — John Bowlby (1944, 1951)
Forty-Four Juvenile Thieves: of 44 young thieves, 17 had suffered prolonged separation from their mothers before the age of five, and 14 were classified as “affectionless psychopaths”, of whom 12 had suffered such separation. Bowlby concluded that “prolonged deprivation of maternal care may have grave and far-reaching effects on the character”. Criticism: retrospective, small sample, no control for the disruption that accompanied the separation; Michael Rutter’s Maternal Deprivation Reassessed (1972) showed that it is privation and discord, not separation as such, that matter. Still, Bowlby’s attachment theory is now the dominant framework in juvenile justice practice worldwide.
Healy & Bronner; Redl & Wineman; Glueck & Glueck
Healy and Bronner (New Light on Delinquency and its Treatment, 1936) compared 105 delinquents with their non-delinquent siblings and found the delinquent to be the one whose emotional needs were thwarted — delinquency as a substitute satisfaction. Redl and Wineman (Children Who Hate, 1951) described the “delinquent ego” which serves rather than restrains the id. Sheldon and Eleanor Glueck (Unraveling Juvenile Delinquency, 1950) matched 500 delinquents with 500 non-delinquents and identified five family factors — discipline of the father, supervision by the mother, affection of each parent, and family cohesiveness — a finding that has been repeatedly replicated and is the empirical core of modern parenting-order policy.
Frustration–aggression
John Dollard and colleagues (1939): “aggression is always a consequence of frustration”. The DU solved paper of 2022 lists it among the psychological theories: “aggressive behaviour is a logical and expected result of frustration which in turn originates when a desired objective or goal of a person is not achieved. In order to remove the block to attain the desired goal, the person may become aggressive and out of control thus resulting in crimes.” Berkowitz later softened it: frustration produces readiness for aggression, which requires a cue to be released.

27.2 Criticisms of the psychodynamic approach

  1. Unfalsifiable. The id, ego and superego are not observable; every outcome and its opposite can be explained (a weak super-ego and an over-harsh super-ego both produce crime). By the DU material’s own test at p. 46 — a scientific theory “asserts relationships between classes of observable phenomena” — psychoanalysis sits uncomfortably close to the spiritual explanations it displaced.
  2. Retrospective and clinical. Built from the case histories of neurotic, middle-class Viennese patients and of institutionalised delinquents — the same sampling error the material identifies in Lombroso.
  3. Individualistic. It cannot explain crime rates, regional variation, or why crime is concentrated among young males in particular neighbourhoods. Sutherland’s “respiration” test is fatal: Oedipal conflict is universal, criminality is not.
  4. Ignores social structure entirely. Williams makes this criticism of Freud expressly in the female criminality chapter: “Freud mostly ignores social, economic and political factors: he uses psychology to explain female criminality.” (DU Case Material p. 113.)
  5. Sexist and culture-bound. The account of female criminality that Freud built on penis envy is, as §31 shows, indefensible; the “normal woman” of the theory is a bourgeois European construct.
  6. Dangerous in policy. If the offender is “sick”, treatment may be indefinite and coerced; this is the Taft & England objection from Topic 1 (“we cannot push men about like pawns on a chessboard”), and it is the reason Indian law requires judicial supervision of every therapeutic disposal.
What survives. Three things: (a) the insight that much conduct is not rationally chosen, which limits the classical model; (b) the concept of internalised control, which reappears, de-mystified, as Hirschi’s social bond and as Gottfredson & Hirschi’s self-control; and (c) the therapeutic tradition itself — counselling, group therapy, anger management, victim-offender mediation — which is the working method of every probation and correctional service in the world.

28. Conflict theory — crime as the product of group struggle

Overlap warning. Conflict and Marxist criminology are prescribed readings in Topic 1 (Vold, Bernard & Snipes, “Conflict Criminology” and “Marxist and Post-Modern Criminology”) and are listed again as a theory-of-causation head in Topic 2. In Topic 1 you use them as a perspective on the definition of crime (who gets to call conduct criminal); in Topic 2 you use them as an explanation of criminal behaviour (why people offend). Write the same material, but point it at the question that was asked.

28.1 The intellectual ancestry

Karl Marx (1818–1883)
Marx wrote almost nothing systematic on crime. What criminology takes from him is the method: the mode of production determines the social, political and intellectual life-process; the state and law are part of the superstructure resting on the economic base and serving the interests of the class that owns the means of production. Crime for Marx is largely the conduct of the lumpenproletariat — the demoralised residue thrown up by capitalism — and is not revolutionary.
Friedrich Engels (1820–1895)
The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845): crime is the “earliest, crudest and least fruitful form of rebellion”; demoralisation produced by industrial exploitation, brutal housing and insecurity drives the worker to theft and violence. Engels, not Marx, supplies criminology’s first Marxist causal account — and Bonger (§30) is its systematiser.
Max Weber (1864–1920)
Conflict is not reducible to class. Society is stratified along class, status and party; the state is the body claiming a monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force. Weber licenses a pluralist conflict criminology in which many groups — religious, ethnic, professional, regional — struggle over the content of the criminal law.
Georg Simmel (1858–1918)
Conflict is a normal and integrative form of sociation: it binds each group internally even as it divides them from each other. This is the direct source of Vold’s theory.

28.2 Thorsten Sellin — Culture Conflict and Crime (1938)

Sellin’s starting point is the conduct norm: every group has rules about what its members should do in given situations, and these norms are not co-extensive with the criminal law. Crime occurs when the conduct norms of one group are enacted as law and another group continues to live by its own.

TypeMeaningIndian illustration

28.3 George B. Vold — Theoretical Criminology (1958): group conflict theory

Vold’s core propositions
  1. Man is a group-involved being whose life is both a part of and a product of group associations.
  2. Groups form because members have common interests and can better advance them through collective action.
  3. Groups come into conflict when the interests they serve overlap and become competitive.
  4. Conflict strengthens group loyalty — “nothing promotes harmony and self-sacrifice within the group quite as effectively as a serious struggle with another group for survival”.
  5. Politics is “a process of interaction and adjustment between groups struggling for control of the police power of the state”; the group that wins writes the criminal law and obtains the help of the police in defending its interests.
  6. Hence much crime is the normal, natural behaviour of a minority group acting in its own interest against the interests of the group that controls the law-making machinery.

Vold’s own catalogue of “conflict crime”: (i) crimes arising from political protest — the rebel, the revolutionary, the satyagrahi; (ii) crimes arising from labour disputes — picketing, strikes, lock-outs, industrial sabotage; (iii) crimes arising from disputes between competing unions; and (iv) crimes arising from racial and ethnic clashes. Vold himself limited his theory to these, conceding that it cannot explain “irrational, impulsive and unplanned” offences — an honesty worth reproducing in an answer.

Real-world exampleThe Indian farm-law agitation of 2020–21, the anti-CAA protests, and the mass FIRs that followed each of them are Vold group-conflict crime in textbook form: conduct that the participants regard as civic duty, and the state as rioting, unlawful assembly (ss.189–191 BNS) and, in a few cases, UAPA offences. Compare Ramlila Maidan Incident, In Re (2012) 5 SCC 1, where the Supreme Court condemned the midnight police action on sleeping protestors, and Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020) 3 SCC 637 on the proportionality of restrictions.

28.4 Austin Turk — Criminality and Legal Order (1969)

Turk asks not why people break the law but when does the criminalisation of a subordinate group actually happen. Society is divided into authorities (who make and enforce norms) and subjects. Criminality is not a property of behaviour; it is a status conferred by authorities. Conflict — and therefore criminalisation — is most probable where:

  • the prohibited act or attribute is culturally significant to the authorities (congruence between their cultural norms, what they say, and their social norms, what they do);
  • the subjects are organised (a group rather than isolated individuals) — organisation raises the probability of conflict but reduces the probability of each member being caught;
  • the subjects are unsophisticated — they lack the knowledge of how to manipulate the authorities’ machinery. The sophisticated offender (the corporate lawyer’s client) avoids criminalisation; the unsophisticated (the street vendor, the migrant) does not.
Real-world exampleTurk’s “sophistication” variable explains why identical economic wrongdoing produces prison for a chit-fund agent and a settlement for a listed company: the latter has compounding under s.441 of the Companies Act, SEBI consent orders, the Insolvency Code and tax settlement commissions. It is Sutherland’s differential implementation (§18) stated as a theory of power.

28.5 Richard Quinney — The Social Reality of Crime (1970)

Quinney’s six propositions (memorise these)
  1. Definition of crime: crime is a definition of human conduct that is created by authorised agents in a politically organised society.
  2. Formulation of criminal definitions: criminal definitions describe behaviours that conflict with the interests of the segments of society that have the power to shape public policy.
  3. Application of criminal definitions: criminal definitions are applied by the segments of society that have the power to shape the enforcement and administration of criminal law.
  4. Development of behaviour patterns in relation to criminal definitions: behaviour patterns are structured in segmentally organised society in relation to criminal definitions, and within this context persons engage in actions that have relative probabilities of being defined as criminal.
  5. Construction of criminal conceptions: conceptions of crime are constructed and diffused in the segments of society by various means of communication — chiefly the mass media.
  6. The social reality of crime: the social reality of crime is constructed by the formulation and application of criminal definitions, the development of behaviour patterns related to criminal definitions, and the construction of criminal conceptions.

Quinney later moved from this pluralist-conflict position to an explicitly Marxist one in Critique of Legal Order (1974) and Class, State and Crime (1977), where he distinguished crimes of domination (committed by the capitalist class and its state — repression, economic exploitation, corporate crime) from crimes of accommodation and resistance (predatory and personal crime by the oppressed, plus political resistance). That movement is exactly the conflict → radical shift Bernard analyses at §29.

28.6 Chambliss and Seidman; William Chambliss’s vagrancy study

Chambliss & Seidman, Law, Order and Power (1971): “the more economically and politically powerful a group is, the less likely it is that its members will be dealt with by the legal system for violating the law”, and the law-enforcement bureaucracy maximises rewards and minimises strains — which means processing the politically weak. Chambliss’s “A Sociological Analysis of the Law of Vagrancy” (1964) traced English vagrancy law from the Black Death (1348–49) — a labour-supply statute for landowners after the plague destroyed the labour market — through its revival for the protection of merchant goods, to the modern control of the “undesirable”. His “Saints and the Roughnecks” (1973) is the best short demonstration of differential labelling: two groups of schoolboys, equal in delinquency; the middle-class “Saints” became professionals, the working-class “Roughnecks” acquired records.

Indian parallelIndia’s own vagrancy law — the Bombay Prevention of Begging Act, 1959, extended to Delhi — criminalised having “no visible means of subsistence”. In Harsh Mander v. Union of India (2018) SCC OnLine Del 10427, the Delhi High Court struck down 25 sections of the Act as violative of Articles 14 and 21, holding that “criminalising begging is a wrong approach to deal with the underlying causes of the problem… the state is under an obligation to provide the basic necessities”. This is Chambliss’s thesis vindicated by an Indian constitutional court — an outstanding example to deploy in any conflict question. See also the repeal of the Criminal Tribes Act in 1952 and the continuing stigmatisation of Denotified, Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic Tribes (Renke Commission 2008; Idate Commission 2017).

29. Radical, Marxist, critical and post-modern criminology

29.1 The New Criminology (1973)

Ian Taylor, Paul Walton and Jock Young, The New Criminology: For a Social Theory of Deviance, demolished positivism and demanded a fully social theory of deviance with seven interlocking elements. The list is the single most citable thing in radical criminology:

  1. The wider origins of the deviant act — the political economy of the whole society.
  2. The immediate origins of the deviant act — the actor’s own social psychology.
  3. The actual act — the relationship between belief and action; what the act means to the actor.
  4. The immediate origins of social reaction — the responses of family, police, the immediate audience.
  5. The wider origins of deviant reaction — the political and economic structure of social control.
  6. The outcome of the social reaction on the deviant’s further action — the labelling/deviancy-amplification loop.
  7. The nature of the deviant process as a whole — the theory must hold all six together, not separate them.

Their positive programme: “a society in which the facts of human diversity, whether personal, organic or social, are not subject to the power to criminalise”. The crime problem is solved by abolishing the conditions that create it, not by treating offenders.

29.2 The core claims of Marxist/radical criminology

Instrumental Marxism
The law and the state are the direct instrument of the ruling class; the criminal law protects private property and criminalises the poor. Chambliss, early Quinney. Criticism: too crude — it cannot explain laws that plainly restrain capital (factory legislation, antitrust, environmental law) or that were won by workers’ struggle.
Structural Marxism
The state has relative autonomy; the law serves the long-term interests of capital as a system, which sometimes requires acting against individual capitalists. E. P. Thompson, Whigs and Hunters (1975): the rule of law is “an unqualified human good” precisely because its formal equality can be turned against the powerful — the most important internal Marxist concession.
Crime as primitive rebellion
Some radicals romanticised the offender as an inchoate revolutionary. Criticism (and it is decisive): most crime is intra-class — the poor overwhelmingly victimise the poor, and women, minorities and the elderly bear the cost. This is the observation that produced left realism.
Crimes of the powerful
The lasting achievement: state crime, corporate manslaughter, environmental crime, war crime, and the criminology of genocide (Stanley Cohen, States of Denial, 2001) all descend from this tradition, as does the study of police violence and custodial death.
Abolitionism
Louk Hulsman, Nils Christie (Conflicts as Property, 1977; Crime Control as Industry, 1993), Thomas Mathiesen: “crime” has no ontological reality; the state steals the conflict from the parties to whom it belongs. The reply is restorative justice — which Indian law has partly adopted through plea bargaining (Ch. XXIII BNSS), compounding (s.359 BNSS), Lok Adalats under the Legal Services Authorities Act 1987, and s.12 of the JJ Act’s Board procedure.
Left realism
Jock Young’s self-correction (What is to be Done About Law and Order?, 1984, with John Lea). “Take crime seriously.” The square of crime: offender, victim, state agencies, and the public — crime must be explained as the interaction of all four. Relative deprivation, not absolute poverty, causes crime: “relative deprivation breeds discontent; discontent plus lack of political solution equals crime”. Policy: minimal but accountable and democratically controlled policing, victim-centred local surveys (the Islington Crime Surveys), and social crime prevention.

30. Bernard (1981): is there a real difference between conflict and radical criminology?

Bernard’s answer: the two are genuinely distinct, and conflating them (as both their critics and many of their friends do) destroys both.

Exam use. A question that says “conflict and radical criminology are the same thing in different words — discuss” is answered by reproducing this table, then giving one Indian example on each side: conflict — the shifting coalitions behind liquor prohibition in Bihar and Gujarat, or the cow-slaughter laws, where religion and region, not class, are the axis; radical — the Bhopal settlement and the pattern of corporate impunity, where class is the axis (§18).

31. The Economic Theory of Crime — Willem Adriaan Bonger (1876–1940)

Syllabus head (vi)2022 Q4 answered on this

31.1 Who Bonger was, and what he argued

Bonger was a Dutch criminologist, the first to hold a chair of sociology and criminology at Amsterdam and the first to attempt a systematic Marxist criminology. His doctoral thesis, Criminalité et conditions économiques (1905), was translated by Henry P. Horton as Criminality and Economic Conditions (Boston, Little Brown & Co., 1916). A lifelong socialist and opponent of Nazism, he took his own life in May 1940 rather than live under the German occupation of the Netherlands.

His thesis in one sentence: man is by nature social and capable of altruism, but the capitalist mode of production systematically breeds egoism — and egoism, not poverty as such, is the proximate cause of crime. Capitalism does this to everyone. It makes the poor desperate and brutalised, and it makes the rich rapacious and contemptuous of others; it destroys the natural sociability (“altruism”) of pre-capitalist and cooperative production. Crime is therefore not an individual defect but a social product, and it will disappear under a socialist organisation of production.

31.2 Bonger’s conclusions on the crime–economy relationship

The five propositions (as summarised in the standard Indian text)
  1. Statistical association with the non-propertied class. Bonger’s data showed that “almost 79 per cent of the criminals belong to the non-propertied class”, establishing a correlation between poverty and delinquency. From a study of the economic literature of the whole of Europe he concluded that property crimes — theft, stealing, robbery, dacoity, house-breaking — record an abnormal increase during periods of depression when prices are high.
  2. Capitalism breeds disparity, hoarding and unemployment. The influence of economic conditions on delinquency “is essentially due to the capitalistic economy which breeds disparity and leads to unequal distribution of wealth. The capitalists resort to hoarding and monopolistic trends, thus creating artificial scarcity and a consequent rise in prices. This in turn stops production which ultimately leads to unemployment of labour, as a result of which offences such as alcoholism, vagrancy, beggary, assault and violence record an upward trend.”
  3. Inflation, bankruptcy and insolvency. “In an economic system based on capitalism, economic cycles of inflation and deflation are frequent. Inflation gives rise to bankruptcy and insolvency, with the result that the persons affected are forced to lead an anti-social life and some of them may even resort to criminality.”
  4. Unlawful competition among entrepreneurs. Competition produces efficiency, low cost and quality; but “when these efforts fail to meet the competition, unlawful devices such as violation of laws relating to trade marks, copyright, patents etc. are committed by the manufacturers”. Note that this is Bonger anticipating Sutherland’s white-collar crime by more than thirty years, and locating its cause in the structure of the market rather than in the businessman’s associations.
  5. Employment of women and children. “The employment of children and women furnishes soothing ground for criminality… a child who earns his wages does not know how to spend it usefully. Consequently he is apt to spend his money on undesirable items… which ultimately drag him into the criminal world.” Of women’s employment: “with the outdoor occupational activities of mothers, the children are not properly looked after”, and “greed for money often induces women to agree to immoral acts”. Prof. Gillin: “while lack of employment seems to be hazardous for adult males, the employment of women and children is associated with an increase in criminality.”
Read proposition 5 critically. Its assumption — that a mother’s employment is a criminogenic fact — is a product of its time and is squarely contradicted by modern evidence and by Article 15(3), Article 39(a) and (e) and Article 42 of the Constitution and by the Maternity Benefit Act 1961. A first-class answer reproduces Bonger accurately and then says exactly this. The child-labour half of the proposition, however, has survived: it is the basis of Article 24, of the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 (s.3 prohibition; s.14 penalty), of its 2016 amendment renaming it the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, and of M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996) 6 SCC 756 (Sivakasi), which ordered a ₹20,000 contribution per child from every offending employer into a Child Labour Rehabilitation-cum-Welfare Fund. See also Article 18 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, 1989, and India’s official position, recorded in the literature, that child labour is “a necessary evil, a concomitant of poverty which cannot be done away with unless poverty itself is eradicated”.

Violent crime tracks inequality; property crime tracks opportunity.

Dr. Morrison’s study of India is worth a line: he observed a “peculiar undercurrent of economic soundness in the Indian caste system where every member of the caste could find himself completely safe and secure from the economic standpoint” — an early, and very contestable, claim that pre-modern Indian social organisation contained an in-built economic security which blunted the economic causes of crime. Use it to introduce the Indian material, but mark its obvious blindness to caste oppression itself.

31.4 Criticism of Bonger’s theory

  1. Goring’s data. Charles Goring’s research on 3,000 convicts, correlating occupation with frequency of conviction, “has shown that poverty has no correlation with the frequency of convictions”; relative prosperity is no explanation for a decline in the crime rate. Goring found arson, wilful damage and sex crimes frequent among labourers, agriculturists, seamen and soldiers, while commercial occupations produced fewer of these but more acquisitive offences. Cohen’s gloss: “honesty is not the monopoly of only the rich persons; many people lead an honest and upright life despite their extremely poor financial condition.”
  2. Tarde’s alternative. In Penal Philosophy, Gabriel Tarde agreed that crime is economic in origin but relocated the cause: not commercial or industrial progress but inequitable distribution of wealth and man’s lust for a luxurious life. “The acquisitive tendency in man often tempts him to commit illegal acts.” This is inequality-and-greed, not poverty.
  3. The rich commit crime too — the fatal objection. “Even the wealthiest persons — big industrialists, businessmen, financiers or monopolists — often resort to dishonest means such as falsification of accounts, black-marketing, tax-evasion, hoarding, infringement of trade marks and copyright, despite their huge earnings.” “One can understand a poor man committing a theft to meet his bare necessities of life, but why does an industrialist or monopolist evade tax or maintain a false account?” The Indian illustrations given in the literature are the Bofors deal and the Fairfax findings of 1987, the FERA inquiry of 1988 into a Delhi firm that received ₹6.5 crore in commission from ONGC, the Harshad Mehta bank securities scam (Action Taken Report on the JPC tabled 27 July 1994) and the Ketan Parekh stock-market scam of 2001, into which a 30-member Joint Parliamentary Committee (20 Lok Sabha, 10 Rajya Sabha) was appointed in April 2001 to examine the role of banks, brokers, promoters, stock exchanges, financial institutions and regulators. Conclusion: “it is not necessarily the need but the greed and craze for amassing wealth that motivates even the wealthiest persons to indulge in criminal activities.” Add the modern list — Satyam (2009), the 2G and Coalgate references, NSEL, PNB/ Nirav Modi (2018), IL&FS, the Punjab & Maharashtra Co-operative Bank and the ABG Shipyard ₹22,842-crore CBI case (2022).
  4. Poverty coexists with honesty. “Instances are not wanting when persons living in extreme poverty exhibit a high sense of regard for honesty. They even decline to accept any kind of reward for their honest act… guided by value considerations and ethical standards of life rather than their economic condition.” Poverty alone “does not always lull the virtues of benevolence, honesty and justice in men”.
  5. Socialism did not abolish crime. The claim that capitalism is the cause fails the comparative test: the USSR and China experienced the failure of economic equalisation in practice — Khrushchev had to introduce incentive programmes and money-loans, and Gorbachev introduced glasnost (economic freedom) and perestroika (restructuring) in 1987 to secure a materially richer life and greater democratisation. Nationalisation merely substitutes public officials for private managers — “how can it be said with certainty that these public officials shall not be tempted by personal gain?” Hence corruption flourishes in state-run economies too.
  6. Statistical artefact. Bonger’s 79% figure is drawn from convict populations, which measure the output of the criminal justice system, not the distribution of criminality — the very point Sutherland made against the whole poverty literature in 1939 when he invented white-collar crime (§18). Bonger, in other words, is vulnerable to a criticism his own fourth proposition should have alerted him to.
  7. Mono-causal. “Bonger’s theory of the economic factor as a sole cause of criminality is not wholly correct. There are many other indirect factors which affect crimes differently.” Economic condition is a predisposing, not a determining, factor — the position Sutherland reaches with differential social organisation and Merton with structural strain.

31.5 Gary Becker and the “economics of crime” — the other economic theory

Do not confuse Bonger’s Marxist economic theory with the neo-classical economic model of Gary S. Becker, “Crime and Punishment: An Economic Approach”, 76 J. Pol. Econ. 169 (1968), for which he won the Nobel Prize. Becker revives Beccaria and Bentham with mathematics: a person commits an offence if the expected utility from it exceeds the utility he could get from using his time and resources otherwise. The offender weighs the gain G against p × f — the probability of conviction times the severity of the punishment. Policy consequences: (i) because most offenders are risk-preferring at the margin, an increase in the probability of punishment deters more than an equal increase in severity; (ii) society should spend on enforcement only up to the point where the marginal social cost of enforcement equals the marginal social harm prevented — so some crime is economically optimal; (iii) fines are efficient because they are transfers rather than social costs. Criticism: it assumes perfect information and rationality that offenders do not have (Cornish & Clarke’s “bounded rationality”), it cannot price expressive violence, and treating an optimal level of crime as acceptable is morally unattractive.

2022 Q4 — the starving daily-wagers question. “During the COVID-19 lockdown, thousands of daily-wage earners were left without food or work. Some of them committed theft of food articles and were booked under s.379 IPC. Examine with the help of criminological theory.” The DU model answer runs on Bonger and Becker together, and on two Indian cases:
  • In Re Maragathan (AIR 1961 Mad. 498) — the accused, in desperate poverty, took food; the Madras High Court treated extreme want as a powerful mitigating circumstance and released the offender, observing that the criminal law should not be used to punish hunger.
  • Shreerangayee v. State of Madras (1973 Cr LJ 13, Mad.) — a destitute mother, unable to feed her children, attempted to kill them and then herself; convicted, but the High Court held the extreme socio-economic compulsion to be a determinative mitigating factor, releasing her on probation and observing that society, not the mother alone, stood accused.
Structure of the answer: (1) facts and the legal position — s.379 IPC/s.303 BNS; the general exceptions, including s.81 IPC/s.19 BNS (necessity) and s.94 IPC/s.32 BNS (compulsion) and why they fit awkwardly; (2) Bonger — depression, unemployment, hoarding, artificial scarcity and the rise in property crime: the lockdown is a controlled experiment in Bonger’s propositions 1–3; (3) Merton — innovation under strain when institutionalised means are abruptly withdrawn; (4) Becker/Beccaria — deterrence collapses when the alternative to the offence is starvation, because the utility of the lawful option is zero; (5) left realism and relative deprivation — the migrant’s exclusion; (6) the state’s own legal obligations: Article 21 (right to food — PUCL v. Union of India, the Right to Food case), the National Food Security Act, 2013, Olga Tellis, and the Supreme Court’s suo motu In Re Problems and Miseries of Migrant Labourers (2020) directions; (7) conclusion — a criminal-justice response should use s.4 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958, admonition under s.3, plea bargaining and compounding, rather than conviction and imprisonment; punishing survival theft is a category error which both the classical and the positivist schools, for opposite reasons, condemn.

32. Female criminality I — the invisibility of women, and the biological tradition

32.1 The problem: women were invisible in criminology

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 107 (Katherine S. Williams, Textbook on Criminology) “Until recently, women were virtually invisible in the literature on crime. Problems posed by female criminality were ignored in most textbooks or were added as a footnote to the discussion of male criminality. The experience of women was generally marginalised, and their criminality was distorted to fall in with whatever male theory was being expounded. In such accounts criminality was assumed to be a male characteristic; explaining male criminality explained all criminality. …

In both these areas, until the last 30 years, the lack of literature on female criminality is astounding. One reason given for this lack of interest is that females have traditionally been seen as being intrinsically law abiding.”

The statistical background Williams supplies (memorise a few of these figures; they impress):

  • In Britain, 80 per cent of those convicted of serious crime are male, and women are only about 5 per cent of the prison population — “suggesting that their offences tend to be less serious”. In the early 1990s the figure was about 3 per cent, “so this is a significant rise”.
  • Home Office (2002): “Between 1993 and 2001 the average population of women in prison rose by 140 per cent as against 46 per cent for men, reflecting sentencing changes at the courts.” For young offenders, between 1992 and 2002 female imprisonment rose by 250 per cent and male by 50 per cent; magistrates’ courts imprisoned women five times more frequently in 2001 than in 1992. “The change has occurred even though there has not been such a marked change in either the nature or the extent of female offending.”
  • Even shoplifting, “a crime which is traditionally linked with women”, produces more male than female convictions.
  • Williams’s memorable illustration: “If one were put into a room with 20 people chosen at random, half male and half female, and were told that ten of them were convicted criminals, the best single predictor would be sex.” Gender is the single strongest predictor of criminality known to criminology — and criminology ignored it for a century.
Could the gap be an artefact of hidden crime? Williams answers no: “This seems unlikely, as the largest areas of hidden crime are white-collar and corporate crimes, which women have less opportunity to commit, and domestic crimes such as spouse battering and child physical and sexual abuse, both of which are more commonly carried out by men than by women.” The gender gap is real, not merely a recording effect — which is precisely why it demands explanation.
Indian figuresNCRB Prison Statistics India consistently shows women at roughly 4 per cent of India’s prison population, and NCRB Crime in India shows women as a similarly small fraction of persons arrested. The Indian pattern therefore replicates the global one, and the 2016 Ministry of Women and Child Development report, the Supreme Court’s directions in R.D. Upadhyay v. State of A.P. (2006) 8 SCC 572 on children of women prisoners, and the Model Prison Manual 2016, Chapter XXIV, are the Indian counterparts of the Corston Report discussed by Williams.

32.2 What the biological/psychological theories have in common

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 108 “Physiological and psychological theories subsume a number of explanations which basically attribute female criminality to individual characteristics (physiological, hormonal or psychological) which are either unchanged or only marginally affected by economic, social or political forces. These theories often conclude that criminality is due to the inherent nature of particular ‘abnormal’ women who are bad and begin their life with a propensity for criminality; that is, in everyday language, they are considered to be ‘born criminals’.

Because these theories are centred upon the individual, they all suggest a ‘cure’ based upon adjustments to the individual. These ‘cures’ range from sterilization… through to psychoanalysis. Little, if any, consideration is given to the role, status or socio-economic position of women in society. The neglect of social factors has an immediate attraction for anyone wishing to retain the status quo in society, helping to explain the popularity of these theories amongst the better-off sections of the community. Such an approach also lent support to the idea that the penal system should ‘reform’ prisoners whilst in custody… It takes time to ‘cure’ or to ‘help’ people, and so it is necessary to incarcerate them for long enough to have the desired effect. In this way, these theories also influenced the lengths of prison sentences.”

Note the two sting-in-the-tail criticisms Williams builds into the description itself: these theories are politically convenient to the comfortable, and they lengthen sentences because cure takes time. Both are excellent points to make in an answer. She is equally fair to them: “at the time they were written they represented novel and innovative thinking.”

32.3 Lombroso on the female offender (1895)

Lombroso and Ferrero, La donna delinquente (The Female Offender, 1895). Williams keeps him in the syllabus because “its relevance continues because several influential later writers base their ideas upon it. Lombroso is also a good starting point because he states explicitly what is only implied by later theorists.”

  1. Atavism and the evolutionary ladder. All crime is the result of atavistic throwbacks. “He argues that the most advanced forms of human are white males, and the most primitive are non-white females.” The racism and sexism are not incidental; they are load-bearing.
  2. The stigmata he identified in criminal women: “large hand size; low voice pitch; having moles; being short; darker skin; having dark hair; being fat, and so on.”
  3. The Sicily problem. “All his tests were carried out in Italy, and some of the ‘characteristics’ are particularly prevalent in certain areas, most markedly in Sicily. He gave little consideration to the fact that within Italy Sicilians were generally poorer than other groups and that their social conditions rather than their physical appearance may have led to their criminality.” This single observation destroys the method: he mistook a marker of poverty and regional origin for a marker of biology.
  4. His late concession. “In later writings he did compromise by admitting that the ‘born criminal’ accounted for only 35 per cent of criminals, and that some crime was committed by pseudo-criminals who might be pushed into crime by adverse environment, passion or criminal associations.”
  5. The internal contradiction — and this is the examiner’s favourite point. “If crime was to be explained merely by primitive traits, female crime would be GREATER than male crime because, according to Lombroso, all females are less advanced than males. Lombroso’s theory therefore seems to point towards a higher female than male crime rate, whereas the female rate was, and is, lower.” His theory predicts the opposite of the one fact everybody agrees on.
  6. His two escape routes. (a) “Prostitution was the female substitute for crime” — women’s atavism is discharged sexually rather than criminally. (b) Women’s proximity to lower life forms: they have “a smaller cerebral cortex which rendered them both less intelligent and less capable of abstract reasoning”, leading to “a greater likelihood of psychological disturbance (see Freud)” and “sexual anomalies” rather than crime.
  7. Adaptability, coldness and pity. “Because females are more simplistic than males, women, like lower animal life, are more adaptable and more capable of surviving in unpleasant surroundings” — which is why “he largely ignores the poverty of the environment in which criminal women often lived”. This survival capacity “evidences an inability to feel pain and a contempt of death, making them insensitive to the pain and suffering of other people”. But in most women “the coldness is controlled or neutralised by pity, weakness, maternity, and, he argues, most importantly by underdeveloped intelligence and lack of passion”.
  8. The criminal woman is a masculine woman. “Criminal women, and all men, possess passion and intelligence. So criminal women have a cranium closer in size to that of men, and have more body hair and other masculine physical traits which are not signs of development in women… The more passive a woman, the more highly developed she is, and the further from being a ‘born criminal’.”
Williams’s verdict. “His theories and convoluted explanations are clearly invalid today and illustrate clear sex, race and class bias… A racist criticism arises because Lombroso’s atavistic traits are necessarily more prevalent in certain races, particularly the non-whites. His approach can also be said to be rooted in class because, in order to have a wholly passive female devoted only to family and home, it is necessary to have a society in which someone works in order to support these women.” And yet his legacy persists in two threads: “that crime can be explained by reference to biological and inherited characteristics; and that crime is caused by a pathological or chemical abnormality which needs to be treated.”

32.4 Later biological writing — brains, hormones and rats

Money and Ehrhardt (1972); Rose et al. (1971): “the genetic passivity of females is related to the different brains of men and women and the difference in hormones”. The experimental basis is animal: “soon after birth a rat’s brain becomes either male or female: a predominance of female hormones (oestrogens), and the brain becomes female; a predominance of male hormones (androgens), the brain becomes male. If, early in life, a female is injected with androgens, she becomes aggressive and indistinguishable from a male, and an early castrated male will be more passive later in life.”

Williams’s criticism, verbatim: “The extrapolation of any finding from rats or monkeys to humans is necessarily very risky. Also the hormonal links are very complex and always a mixture of biology and socialisation. It is very difficult to ascertain which, if either, of the social or the genetic has had the greater effect.”

32.5 The “generative phases”: PMT, menopause and infanticide

“This biological theory is based on changes connected with the menstrual cycle. Although it is unclear whether women generally are involved in a higher incidence of criminality during their generative phases, it is clear that the law takes account of these elements in deciding some cases. Menstruation has been used as a partial defence plea, and both menstruation and menopause have been accepted as factors which should reduce sentences.”

R v. Craddock[1981] CLY 476 (England)
Facts & plea
The accused woman faced a charge of murder. Severe pre-menstrual tension (PMT) was advanced in support of diminished responsibility, supported by the evidence of Dr Katharina Dalton.
Outcome
Murder reduced to manslaughter by reason of diminished responsibility; she received a probationary sentence with a proviso that she undergo hormone treatment.
Significance
English law accepted PMT as “the most important reason for the behaviour” — a partial excuse and a reason for lenient sentence; “the total effect was the acceptance of the controversial idea that PMT amounted to a causative explanation”. Note Williams’s scepticism: “medical evidence is divided about the existence of such a syndrome and its effects”; the effects appear “mainly psychological, such as tension, irritability, depression, tiredness, mood swings and feelings of loneliness”, though Dalton (1984) added “epilepsy, fainting and even hypoglycaemia”.

Facts
The accused drove her car at her lover and killed him; PMT was relied on.
Outcome
Convicted only of manslaughter on the ground of diminished responsibility; no custodial sentence, not even a requirement of hormonal treatment.

Infanticide and post-natal depression. “If a mother kills her child within its first year as a result of post-natal depression or lactation, she would have a partial defence to murder which would render it infanticide. This is clearly only a defence open to women, and is the only sex-specific defence recognised in the criminal law.” Williams adds the crucial social point: “Some of these killings may possibly be the result of exhaustion through caring for the child, guilt through not feeling affection for it, or the effect of other social pressures, all of which could equally well be suffered by a man if he was the person primarily in charge of the care of the child (Wilczynski and Morris, 1993).” The Law Commission’s Draft Criminal Code 1989, cl. 64(1), proposed to recognise these social reasons “but only in the case of women” — widening infanticide while keeping it sex-specific.

The double edge — the paragraph to quote in any answer on this head.
“The hormonal imbalances suffered by men do not normally affect either their conviction or their sentence. Women, however, can successfully plead such imbalances even in the most serious cases… For the individual women involved, this is probably an advantage as they will either elude an unpleasant label or an unpleasant sentence, or both. For women in general, its effects are not so positive. It allows the continuation of the idea that women are incapable of controlling themselves and that their actions can be explained through medical reasoning — they are mentally or physically sick, or both. Widely used, the implication of this reasoning would be that women should be treated for this ‘sickness’ rather than punished. It removes from women the idea that they may CHOOSE the criminality, that it might be a rational decision arising out of a social, economic or political situation.”
Indian law on infanticide and mental state Statute India has no separate infanticide offence and no general defence of diminished responsibility — a mother who kills her infant is charged with murder under s.103 BNS (s.302 IPC), and her only defences are s.22 BNS (s.84 IPC) unsoundness of mind and grave and sudden provocation. In practice the courts achieve the same result at the sentencing stage: see Ediga Anamma (§34.4) and the line of cases commuting the death sentence of women in acute distress. Related provisions: s.93 BNS (exposure and abandonment of a child under twelve), s.92 BNS (causing death of a quick unborn child), ss.88–90 BNS (miscarriage), the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971 (as amended 2021, allowing termination up to 24 weeks for specified categories and recognising grave mental injury), and the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (Prohibition of Sex Selection) Act, 1994, upheld and enforced in CEHAT v. Union of India (2003) 8 SCC 398. The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, ss.105 and 115, is the modern route by which post-partum psychosis enters an Indian criminal proceeding.

33. Female criminality II — Thomas, Freud, Pollak and the “chivalry” thesis

33.1 W. I. Thomas (1907, 1923) — the stepping-stone

“The work of Thomas acts as a stepping-stone between physical and psychological explanations. Thomas moves away from Lombroso’s idea of female criminality being due to masculine tendencies towards saying that, for civilised cultures, female criminality is an unacceptable use of FEMALE traits. Female criminals are considered to be amoral, that is, without morality, not immoral, which implies loss of morality. They are described as being cold, calculating people who have not learned to treat others with pity and concern. They use the less developed and colder sides of their natures in order to gain something for themselves through sexual promiscuity, soliciting, prostitution, or other ‘unacceptable’ and sometimes illegal means.”

33.2 Freud on female criminality (1925, 1931)

“Where Lombroso distinguished between born criminals and other people, Freud saw everyone as a potential criminal in the sense that all human beings are born with amoral and antisocial instincts. Freud explains criminality as a mixture of inherited factors and the effect of external experiences… most will learn to control them; it is those who do not learn such control who end up as criminals. …Freud places a large part of his argument on heredity.”

  • Penis envy and inferiority. “The explanation or motivation for the female criminal is largely sexual neurosis. Due to the lack of a penis the female feels inferior, better suited to the less demanding destiny of being wife and mother rather than breadwinner. …a woman, whilst she is still a child, recognises that she has inferior sexual organs and believes this to be a punishment. She then grows up envious and vengeful.”
  • Narcissism and passivity. “They become exhibitionist and narcissistic, and so try to be well dressed and physically beautiful in order to win love and approval from men; women’s sexual role is receptive and passive, leading to passivity in other areas of her life.”
  • The weak female conscience — Freud’s key structural claim. “Men develop [a strong conscience] as a result of controlling their Oedipal complex… the fear is that the father may ultimately castrate the son — this is the most feared punishment. As a result, boys generally by the age of about 5 develop a very strong super-ego or conscience. As girls and women cannot be castrated, they do not possess the fear necessary to overcome the ELECTRA complex (their desire for their father and hatred of their mother).”
  • The same anomaly as Lombroso’s, and the same escape. “Normally this would lead to a HIGHER crime rate for women, but due to the passivity… and their very strong desire for love and affection, particularly from their fathers or other men, they are controlled. They do not break men’s laws, for to do so would lead to disapproval and a withdrawal of male love and affection.”
  • The deviant woman is the masculine woman. “In Freud’s world, deviant women are those who attempt to become more like men, those who compete or try to achieve acclaim within the masculine spheres of activity, or those who refuse to accept their ‘natural’ passivity. These women are driven by the desire to claim a penis, which leads to aggressive competition. This ultimate desire is, of course, hopeless, and they will end up by becoming ‘neurotic’. Such women need help to enable them to adjust to their intended sex role. The birth of a child would be seen as particularly therapeutic as the baby is a substitute for a penis.”

Williams’s verdict, verbatim: “Freud mostly ignores social, economic and political factors: he uses psychology to explain female criminality. Nevertheless, the concept of a well-adjusted woman is based very much on traditional ideas of sexuality and society. It thus suffers from the same sex and class problems as the earlier theories.”

33.3 Otto Pollak (1950) — hidden crime and the “masked” female offender

The Criminality of Women (1950). “Pollak’s theory was also sex-based. He was doubtful that women commit as little crime as the official statistics showed. He thus advanced a theory of ‘hidden’ female crime. Pollak follows Freud in explaining female crime by reference to sexual neurosis.”

  1. The deceit thesis. “Women are traditionally shy, passive and passionless, but can simulate a sexual orgasm to hide their true feelings. They can take part in sex without any physical passion, and they can learn to hide their monthly menstruation. All this means that within the sexual sphere they learn to manipulate, deceive and conceal — this, he claims, decides the inherent nature of women, making them likely to be the instigators of crime which is then actually perpetrated by men.”
  2. Concealed opportunity. Women’s traditional roles — as domestic servant, nurse, cook, homemaker — give them private, unsupervised access to victims, so their crimes (poisoning, theft from the household, abuse of children, shoplifting, abortion, infanticide) are intrinsically less visible.
  3. The chivalry factor. Men have “a chivalrous attitude towards women. Men thus disliked making accusations against women because they did not want women to be punished. This chivalrous treatment, he claimed, stretched from police through the jury to the judge, and thus resulted in a great under-conviction of female criminals. This is a thread picked up by much later writers, although in rather more refined form.”
Criticisms of Pollak. (i) The physiological premise — that the capacity to conceal an orgasm generates a criminal disposition — is simply an assertion with no evidence. (ii) Self-report and victimisation surveys do not support a mass of hidden female crime of the kind he predicted. (iii) His chivalry thesis has been reversed by the evidence: “Downes and Rock (2003) conclude that contrary to the belief of many positivist writers there is NO chivalry towards women in the criminal justice system.” (iv) He ignores that the women most likely to come before the courts are poor, and chivalry, if it exists, is reserved for the respectable.

33.4 “Modern applications” — the witch, the kleptomaniac and the syndromes

Williams’s closing critique of the whole positivist tradition on women runs as follows, and it is the most quotable passage in the reading:

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 114–115 “Each of the above theories seems to assume that women are more or less totally different from men in every respect: biology; psychology; needs; desires; motivations. They often link criminality in women to old, unquestioned popular assumptions about problem women — usually associated with their breach of the societal norm of wife, mother and homemaker. Implicit in such views are concepts of women as sources of evil, causing the downfall of mankind: their criminality is then represented as more destructive of social order than anything man could do. This almost pathological fear of female non-conformity and criminality is reminiscent of the ‘witch-hunts’ of history, which, as Heidensohn (1996) suggests, is a powerful and recurring popular image of ‘deviant women as especially evil, depraved and monstrous… used by “scientific” criminologists… as the basis of their theories, theories which… not only had a stigmatising effect, but have also had unfortunate consequences for the treatment of women offenders’. However, Heidensohn may provide us with one of the reasons why female criminality is so feared: women are relied upon to maintain order and to continue present societal structures, so deviance from this role is seen as especially threatening. The socialising role of women… is also limiting on them, because it means that they are expected to, and often do, have a far greater stake in conformity.”
The double standard of explanation
“In studying male criminality, mental illness… [was] a useful explanation only in rare cases… In female criminality, it has been assumed that a wide range of crimes can be explained by such mental factors and that the sexual basis of the mental problem is strong, whereas it was absent in explanations of male criminality. …Even rape is very rarely associated with an incomplete resolution of the Oedipal complex; more often it is said to be a crime of power or violence… In court it is often ascribed to sexual frustration (usually caused by the female dressing or behaving in a ‘provocative’ manner) or to drink, and is thereby more understandable.” Male crime is normalised; female crime is pathologised.
Shoplifting and “kleptomania”
“Female shoplifting has often been attributed to kleptomania despite the fact that such a mental disease is very rare: women are supposed to obtain sexual excitement from the act; or perform the crime to still repressed sexual desires; or in order to be punished for such feelings. The prevalence of these ideas, until at least the 1960s, partly continued because of the number of single, divorced or widowed women who performed such acts; the possibility was ignored that this particular group faced unusually harsh economic and social stresses.”
The syndrome defences
“The most interesting addition… is the use of post-traumatic stress syndromes; the one most applicable as a defence is the battered woman syndrome; whilst the rape trauma syndrome has been used as a tool to back up both the defence and the prosecution cases. Similar syndromes have been clinically discovered in both men and women but are more frequently used in the courts in cases involving women.” The defence claim is that “what may look like a premeditated crime — or at least one in which self-defence and provocation as presently defined would be difficult to use — can be psychologically explained.”
The feminist objection to the syndromes
“Some have attacked the use of, and the need to use, such defences and suggest that the traditional defences such as self-defence should be forced to take account of women’s culture and experience (Lacey et al., 2003; Law Commission, 2004); or that in assessing the reasonableness of the accused’s actions and beliefs they should be explicitly subjective (how would a person in her circumstances and with her emotional condition assess the situation?). If these approaches were taken there would be less need to use psychological defences, particularly the syndromes which rather stretch and alter the legal concepts which they are used to prove.” In short: do not medicalise the battered woman — reform the law of self-defence.
Chivalry refuted; punitiveness proved. “The feminist claim is much the reverse of this: female offenders are often viewed as having breached the idea of the female role and are therefore subjected to HARSHER sanctions for relatively less serious crimes (Carlen, 1983).” Studies in the early 1990s (Heidensohn 1996; Daly 1994; Hedderman and Hough 1994) purported to refute this; yet “between 1990 and 2002 female imprisonment rose spectacularly… a lot more women got custody, and prison sentences were far longer”. Carlen (1999, 2002) reiterated that “courts were more punitive towards female offenders, particularly those who are economically and socially marginalised”. Gelsthorpe and Morris (2002) attributed it instead to generally increased punitiveness. Williams’s conclusion is the one to quote: “either there is gender bias within the system or… recent criminal justice and sentencing strategies (such as less tolerance; the ‘gender-blind’ focus on risk rather than offender needs; and ‘gender-blind’ mandatory sentencing) impacts more severely on women than on men. Neither of these possibilities should be acceptable in a criminal justice system.”

Policy response: the Home Office Women’s Policy Team (2003) developed the Women’s Offending Reduction Programme (WORP, launched 2004), addressing “substance abuse, mental and physical health, housing, child-care issues, histories of abuse, poverty and education, training and employment”, so that prison for women would be a last resort. Because women “tend to present a low risk (Fawcett Society, 2004) they were on orders which attracted lower funding, so their needs were unlikely to be met” — “a risk-based model is not gender neutral and leads to social injustices” (Gelsthorpe and McIvor, 2007). After the Fawcett Society reports (2004, 2005, 2009) and Corston (2007), needs began to be met holistically and cheaply — “These have been successful and suggest that female offending arises out of SOCIAL rather than INDIVIDUAL factors.”

34. Female criminality III — the sociological explanations and the feminist critique

34.1 Learning theory and women

Sutherland and Cressey “maintain that their theory is of general application and applies to rich as well as to poor, and to women as much as to men. The class equality continues throughout the book, but gender equality wavers under the need to explain why the male crime rate is so much higher (Sutherland and Cressey, 1966: 138–43). Sutherland does this by excluding females from this absorbed pursuit of self-interest. He argues that females of all classes and ages are socialised into the same sex role: they are taught to be nice rather than egotistical… Sutherland removes from women the education necessary to criminality or to competitive law-abiding behaviour. He only allows them learning which fits their perceived roles as mothers and carers; any criminality has to arise out of this.”

The modern rehabilitation: “Giordano and Rockwell (2000) have revisited the link between differential association and female crime and concluded that this is a decisive factor. Although many women have suffered social deprivation or physical abuse without turning to criminality, they suggest that ALL female criminals had firm associations with positive depictions of deviant lifestyles. From a young age many of the women were ‘immersed’ in these definitions, from mothers, fathers, aunts, cousins and siblings who might be caught up in these activities.” Differential association, properly applied, works for women — it was Sutherland’s application, not his theory, that was gendered.

Talcott Parsons (1947, 1954)The differing delinquency rates flow from the structure of the American family. For example, she explains receiving stolen goods, when committed by women, in terms of a passive act carried out for a loved one, and the goods are likely to be hidden somewhere in the house. The offence is thereby ascribed to relationships and to passivity, both of which fit in with sex-role stereotypes.” Sex-role thinking is so pervasive that it captures even its critics.

Evaluation of masculinity/femininity

  • The tests “all confront the difficulty that, both theoretically and empirically, there seems to be no unexceptionable criterion by which to measure ‘masculinity’ and ‘femininity’”. At most the studies “cast doubt on any correlation between masculinity and crime, but found some inverse relationship between crime and femininity”.
  • “The constructs of masculinity/femininity are themselves seen as patriarchal and are rejected by many feminists. In particular, their use as an explanation of crime is challenged because one of the defining elements of femininity is usually given as CONFORMITY” — which makes the theory circular: it explains conformity by conformity.
  • The new masculinities literature (Jefferson and Carlen, 1996) reverses the question: “what is it that makes men more criminal and more violent than women? Feminists began to claim it was masculinity and the greater power men systematically enjoy. But it then becomes necessary to explain why so many men are law abiding.” Messerschmidt (1993) answers with plural “masculinities” enacted within three structures — labour; power (control and violence); cathexis (relationships and desire) — crime being a resource by which some men “do gender” when other routes are blocked. Hall (2002), Young (1999) and Hall and Winlow (2003) link post-modern insecurity and market exclusion to aggressive masculine identity. Criticism: the concepts merely “describe what they are claimed to explain”.
  • Women’s violence. Miller (2001, 2002), on young women in US gangs, “challenges [the idea that violent girls enact masculinity] and disputes the validity and reliability of treating masculine/feminine as merely oppositional. She argues that women’s violence needs to be seen in a more complex light and associated with severe insecurities” — replicated by Burman (2003) and Phillips (2003), who “note the functionality of such aggression and violence to the lives of the women who perpetrate it”.
  • Punishment as re-feminisation. Bosworth (2000): “in the past prisons were one of the social methods of restricting women and trying to teach them ‘good’ and ‘bad’ femininity.” Erricson (1998): child welfare work “curb[ed] boys’ criminality whilst not subduing their masculinity, but work[ed] to alter girls back to the path of ‘virtuous’ femininity.” Williams’s summary: punishment regimes have punished women twice — “punishment for the offence and the challenge to the sex role” — “whilst leaving masculinity unaltered”.

34.3 Strain theory and control theory applied to women

Strain: women written out
In Cohen’s scheme “the whole of American culture is basically gendered; ambition, wealth, rationality and control of the emotions are the outward signs of a successful person, but only a MALE person. For women, success is to form a close relationship with a successful man. A lack of ambition, inactivity, irrationality and emotional instability are signs of a failed and defective male; they are the very identity of women.” Cloward and Ohlin likewise “relegate women to a position which excludes them from the main masculine culture… Because women are not subjected to financial pressures, they do not suffer strain in the same way.”
Strain: the empirical erosion
“Females have increasingly become economically less marginalised. More women are now the only, the major, or the joint breadwinner… especially as women often inhabit low paid and insecure areas of employment, or are unemployed. Furthermore, women are often left to manage the household accounts.” And “women are more likely than men to be poor”: two-thirds of adults supported by income support are women; women are over-represented in the lowest income deciles; two-thirds of adults in the poorest households are women; Eurostat shows female-headed households suffering much higher poverty rates.
Strain: the over-prediction problem
“Applying strain theory to females could, however, predict TOO MUCH criminality, since they are the most economically marginalised: so if they were also to enter the competition for success one might expect their criminality to exceed that of men.” The one clear poverty-linked female offence Williams identifies is “evasion of payment for television licences” (Pantazis and Gordon, 1997). Her conclusion: “to view the criminality of women as related only to their desire for a partner is too narrow.”
Control: Hirschi and Naffine
Hirschi’s four bonds — attachment, commitment, involvement, belief — are “set out as a gender-neutral idea, but Naffine (1987) suggests that… it remains a male-gendered theory. First, if Hirschi was really interested in conformity he would have studied FEMALES, as the largest and strongest conforming group… and yet he studied men. Secondly, Hirschi sets out as factors of conformity the traditional male role idea of breadwinner… Conformity in males is thus depicted as POSITIVE, but females are said to conform because of their passive natures — conformity becomes NEGATIVE.”
Control: Hagan’s power-control theory
Hagan (1989; Hagan et al., 1990): “in patriarchal families, girls, in contrast to boys, will be socialised as home-makers and away from risks; whereas egalitarian families increase the propensity of girls for risk-taking and so of their likelihood of turning to crime.” McCarthy et al. (1999) confirm: a more equal power–control relationship between parents “had a beneficial effect on boys and a detrimental effect on girls”. Hagan and McCarthy (1997): among street youths, where controlling influences had been removed, “the crime involvement by gender was similar”, though in differing forms (young women in prostitution, young men in stealing food and serious theft).
Control: Heidensohn — the best account
Heidensohn (1985, 1996) “proposes control theory as offering the best account of female criminality or, more particularly, female conformity”. Women are controlled in the home by the caring role of mother and wife, reinforced by social workers and health visitors stressing the child’s welfare, by community care of the elderly and disabled, and by the assumption of female dependency, which “the legitimation of the position by the State helps both to perpetuate… and to control their behaviour”. “Even if they are at work, their free time is often constrained by having to perform the household tasks as well as their jobs, while because they are often in the least secure employments they are deterred from behaviour which might jeopardise their position.” “Lastly, male violence also acts as a very real control; domestic violence may keep them in their place in the home, while street violence also tends to keep women in the home.”
Control balance — Tittle (1997–2000)
“In most areas of their lives women are controlled, often very heavily. As he sees control deficit as one aspect of deviant behaviour, this would appear to predict that women would be heavy offenders, but he notes that large control deficits result in SUBMISSIVE behaviour rather than predatory offending.”
Reintegration — Braithwaite
Braithwaite (1989): “the greater family control exercised over women not only makes them less likely to be criminal but easier to REINTEGRATE”. It follows that women “would be most likely to respond positively to reintegrative and community punishments”. Yet Worrall (2000) and McIvor (1998) show “these sentences are considerably under-used in the case of female offenders”, so that “women were unnecessarily sent to prison, albeit for short periods”; Hannah-Moffat (2003) found the same in Canada. Williams: “the disposals most likely to lead women away from further criminality… are vastly underused.”

Freda Adler, Sisters in Crime (1975) — the “liberation thesis”, or “new female criminal”As women gain social and economic equality, they will take on male patterns of offending; emancipation itself causes a rise in female crime, especially violent and white-collar crime.Refuted or heavily qualified. (i) The rise is concentrated in petty property and welfare-related offences, not boardroom fraud; (ii) the women who fill the prisons are the least emancipated — poor, unemployed, abused, often lone mothers; (iii) female crime rose in periods and places with no corresponding emancipation; (iv) as Williams shows, the recorded rise tracks sentencing change more than behavioural change. 34.5 Williams’s conclusion — learn this paragraph

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 124–125 “In traditional mainstream criminology male criminality is seen as ‘normal’ and something which merely needs to be kept within reasonable bounds. It often arises from a rational choice, a way of getting what one wants or dealing with problems such as unemployment or even just boredom. Female criminality however is abnormal, unexpected and portrayed as pathological and irrational. It generally arises: when women fail to comply with their biologically expected behaviour, they become unnaturally and pathologically masculine or decouple sex from love and care; or from genetic or mental weaknesses which result in irrational and unacceptable behaviour, again pathological. There is something ‘wrong’ with criminal women. Unfortunately, no conclusive scientific tests have been found to ascertain what link, if any, psychological and physiological factors have with crime… The only definite conclusion is that biological arguments have been largely discounted as major reasons for crime. The more socially based theories seem to offer more plausible explanations. However, mainstream criminology failed to use social explanations of criminality to explain why women offend or are more conforming than their male counterparts… Female criminality is, in any event, most directly damaging to people they know and live with rather than to the wider society, so that theories based on societal conflict seem less immediately relevant. Studies which are implicitly based on masculinity… meant that the behaviour of women, if included at all, was seen through a masculine prism. The most promising ideas so far come from CONTROL theories, and from STRAIN theories as long as these are applied in a gender-neutral manner. Each of these theories suffers from the idea that they are basically determinate…”
2017 Q4 — “Female criminality has been a neglected area in criminological theory. Discuss.” Model structure.
  1. Establish the neglect with the opening quotation from Williams (“virtually invisible… added as a footnote… distorted to fall in with whatever male theory was being expounded”).
  2. Establish the paradox: gender is the single best predictor of criminality, yet was the least studied variable. Give the 20-people illustration and the 80%/5% figures; give the Indian 4% prison figure.
  3. Show that the neglect was not benign — when women were studied, they were studied badly: Lombroso’s three types (the born criminal, the occasional criminal and the criminal of passion — with prostitution as the female substitute for crime); Thomas; Freud; Pollak. Bring out the internal contradiction shared by Lombroso and Freud: both predict a higher female crime rate and both must invent an ad hoc suppressor (prostitution, passivity, desire for male love).
  4. Show the double standard — male crime rational, female crime sexual/pathological (the shoplifting/ kleptomania and rape examples).
  5. Show the consequences — sterilisation and “cure”; longer sentences; re-feminisation in prison (Bosworth); the syndrome defences and the feminist objection to them; the refutation of chivalry (Downes and Rock; Carlen).
  6. Show the recovery — Hoffman-Bustamante on sex-role skills; Giordano and Rockwell on differential association; Heidensohn and Hagan on control; Adler and Simon; Smart, Carlen, Daly and Chesney-Lind; masculinities.
  7. Indianise — Srivastava (§35); NCRB data; s.498-A, s.304-B, the Dowry Prohibition Act, the PWDVA 2005, the 2013 Criminal Law Amendment following the Justice Verma Committee, the Nirbhaya case, and the cases at §38.
  8. Conclude with Williams’s conclusion: control and gender-neutral strain theories are the most promising; biology is discounted; and a criminology that cannot explain the conduct of half the population is not a science of crime but a science of male crime.

35.1 The historical structure of discrimination

Verbatim — DU Case Material p. 98 “Indian society has witnessed great discrimination for the women. Pawan Kumar — the leading dowry-death brief

Pawan Kumar v. State of Haryana1998 Cr LJ 1144 (SC); (1998) 3 SCC 309
Facts
Shortly after the marriage, the husband’s family demanded a scooter and a refrigerator. Within four days of the marriage the deceased returned to her mother and complained that her husband and parents-in-law were taunting her for not bringing these articles. Pacified, she returned. Two months later she came back again, complaining of continuous taunting, maltreatment and being called ugly for not bringing the goods. Even after the birth of a son she made the same complaints, including to her father, who expressed his inability to meet the demand and sent her back after consoling her. The deceased ultimately died of burn injuries, having committed suicide by setting herself on fire.
Issue
Whether an expression of desire for a scooter and a fridge, unaccompanied by any agreement at the time of marriage, amounts to a “demand for dowry” within s.2 of the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961, so as to attract s.304-B IPC.
Arguments for the appellant (defence)
(i) There was neither any demand of dowry nor any agreement at the time of marriage, and an agreement is an essential ingredient of the definition of “dowry” in s.2 of the 1961 Act; hence no offence under s.304-B. (ii) The evidence at most disclosed an expression of a desire for a fridge and a scooter, not a demand. (iii) In criminal jurisprudence guilt must be proved beyond all reasonable doubt; a deeming provision cannot dispense with that standard.
Arguments for the State (prosecution)
(i) There was positive evidence of persistent taunting and maltreatment from within days of the marriage until the death, including complaints to both parents. (ii) The word “dowry” must be given a purposive and wide construction consistent with the social object of the 1961 Act and of Chapter XX-A; a narrow reading requiring proof of a pre-marital agreement would defeat the statute. (iii) The ingredients of s.304-B — unnatural death, within seven years, cruelty soon before death, in connection with a dowry demand — were all made out, and s.113-B of the Evidence Act required the court to presume dowry death.
Decision
The Supreme Court held that the demands fell within s.2 of the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 and the case was covered by ss.304-B, 306 and 498-A IPC. There was positive evidence that soon before her death her husband had subjected her to cruelty; the husband was convicted and sentenced under those sections. The evidence against the parents-in-law was “of a general nature”, and they were acquitted.
Principle evolved
(i) “Dowry” is not confined to what was agreed before or at the marriage; a persistent demand made after marriage, in connection with the marriage, is dowry. (ii) “Soon before death” requires a proximate and live link between the cruelty and the death, not a fixed period. (iii) Liability under s.304-B is individual: general and omnibus allegations against all the in-laws will not sustain a conviction — the court must “separate such individuals from the offenders”.
Justice A. P. Misra’s observation (verbatim in the DU material)
“For more than a century, in spite of tall words of respect for women, there has been an onslaught on their liberties through ‘bride-burning’ and ‘dowry-deaths’. This has caused anxiety to the legislators, judiciary and law enforcing agencies, who have attempted to resurrect them from this social choke. There has been [a] service of legislations in this regard, without much effect. This led to the passing of [the] Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961. In spite of stringent legislations, such persons are still indulging in these unlawful activities, not because of any shortcoming in law but under the protective principle of criminal jurisprudence of benefit of doubt. Often, innocent persons are also trapped or brought in with ulterior motives. This places an arduous duty on the Court to separate such individuals from the offenders. Hence, the Courts have to deal [with] such cases with circumvention, sift through the evidence with caution, scrutinise the circumstances with utmost care.”
Criminological reading
The passage is a perfect statement of the limits of the classical model: severity of punishment (“stringent legislations”) has produced little effect because the causes are structural — a dowry economy, hypergamy, the devaluation of daughters. Bonger would say the cause is the commodification of marriage under an acquisitive economy; Merton would call dowry demands innovation in the pursuit of the culturally prescribed goal of wealth; Sutherland would point to the learning of dowry demands as normal within intimate personal groups; Sellin would call it a conflict of conduct norms between statute and custom.

35.5 Crime BY women — Srivastava’s account

Verbatim — DU Case Material pp. 105–106 “As human beings, women are also subjected to inherent weakness of greed, motive and other factors generating the criminal mentality… The offence of adultery as is defined in [the] Indian Penal Code is sexual intercourse by a man with the wife of another. The woman is not punishable even as an abettor. The woman is not only an accomplice but the equal participator in the very act. The time has changed now, the women have advanced enough in the society and they must be made as much criminally liable as the men.

Sometimes the women commit the crimes because they are under heavy strains due to maladjustment with the husband’s family. They make attempt on their own lives or they may do other criminal acts in the heat of anger and anguish. The crime may also be the result of sheer frustration. In such cases, the sympathetic attitude of the law rather than [a] stringent penalty will be more applicable.

The liberalisation of the women in law and society and the women developing themselves mentally equal to men have made the women more aggressive and assertive. The thinking that they are not inferior in any way to the men has brought a change in the outlook of the women resulting in revolt and sometimes [the] commission of crime. The women, sometimes, are kidnapped and abducted by the criminal gang and ultimately these women are converted as criminals due to forced circumstances. They may operate in a terrifying and ruthless manner as hard core criminals with murderous thrust due to [a] revengeful attitude against those who have harmed them or their family.

It is generally believed that the women are conformist to the societal norms. This conformity is because of [the] natural gift to them of love and compassion. They are also conformist because they are controlled by the men right from very childhood to the adulthood. WHERE THIS CONTROL LOSES, DEVIANCY STARTS. If they come in contact with different groups of people, their impact is natural on them.”
Three theoretical labels to attach to that passage in an answer. (i) “Heavy strains due to maladjustment… sheer frustration” = Merton/Agnew strain and the frustration–aggression hypothesis. (ii) “Liberalisation… more aggressive and assertive” = Adler’s liberation thesis, with all its weaknesses (§34.4) — Srivastava adopts it uncritically and you should say so. (iii) “They are controlled by the men… where this control loses, deviancy starts” and “if they come in contact with different groups of people, their impact is natural” = Heidensohn’s control theory plus Sutherland’s differential association in a single sentence. Srivastava’s account of abducted women “converted as criminals due to forced circumstances” is the Indian version of Chesney-Lind’s criminalisation of survival strategies — and the reason for s.2(14)(iv) of the JJ Act 2015 and the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 being critiqued for punishing the trafficked woman under s.8 (soliciting) instead of the trafficker.

Update on adultery: s.497 IPC was struck down as unconstitutional in Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2019) 3 SCC 39 — not for the reason Srivastava gives (that women should be equally punished) but for the opposite reason: the section treated the wife as the property of her husband, violating Articles 14, 15 and 21. Adultery is not reproduced in the BNS. Use this contrast to show how the feminist critique in §34 altered Indian law: the answer to a discriminatory criminal provision may be decriminalisation rather than equal criminalisation.

Ediga Anamma — the leading Indian case on the female offender

Ediga Anamma v. State of Andhra PradeshAIR 1974 SC 799; (1974) 4 SCC 443 (Krishna Iyer, J.)
Facts
The accused, a married woman, was living in an illicit relationship with a man. The man developed a sexual intimacy with another woman, Annapurna (Annusuya). Under “the fury of sex jealousy” the accused murdered her rival and the rival’s infant daughter Nirmala with a chisel, disfigured the face of the deceased and buried the child’s body beneath the river sand.
Trial and High Court
The trial court sentenced her to death for murder and to life imprisonment under s.201 IPC for causing the disappearance of evidence. The High Court confirmed the conviction and the sentence.
Issue
Whether the extreme penalty was the appropriate sentence, having regard to the offender rather than merely to the offence.
Arguments for the appellant
(i) The killing arose from an uncontrollable emotional condition produced by desertion, destitution and jealousy, not from calculated depravity. (ii) Her personal and social circumstances — a young mother, driven out of her matrimonial home, socially isolated and economically dependent — were mitigating. (iii) Sentencing must be individualised; the death penalty must be reserved for the rarest case.
Arguments for the State
(i) A double murder, one victim an infant, committed with a chisel, followed by mutilation and concealment of the body, is brutal and premeditated. (ii) The concealment under s.201 evidenced a calculating mind, not a broken one. (iii) Deterrence required confirmation of the capital sentence.
Decision
Krishna Iyer J., “while maintaining the conviction, allowed the appeal and awarded life imprisonment”. The grounds expressly recorded include the “surrounding circumstances of the accused”: “She was the mother of a child. She was flogged out of her husband’s house by the father-in-law. She was living with her parents. She was a sex-starved and a single lady. The paramour was a free-lance romancer. She planned to liquidate her competitor with the reckless passion of a jealous mistress.”
Principle evolved
Sentencing must look at “the crime AND the criminal”. The Court set out the factors relevant to the choice between death and life — the offender’s age, sex, socio-economic condition, mental state and the circumstances of provocation and deprivation — anticipating the mitigating-circumstances doctrine crystallised in Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980) 2 SCC 684 and the propositions in Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab (1983) 3 SCC 470. The judgment is the clearest Indian adoption of the positivist principle of individualisation of punishment, and a rebuke to the purely classical logic of Kailash Kaur.
Use in an answer
Deploy it for three propositions at once: (a) Indian courts individualise the sentence of a female offender by reference to her social situation — Carlen’s “class deal/gender deal” in judicial language; (b) female violence is overwhelmingly intimate (Hoffman-Bustamante: “female crimes of violence are often committed against family members or close friends”, with household implements — here, a chisel); and (c) the risk Williams identifies is present too — the judgment explains her conduct in sexual terms (“sex-starved”, “jealous mistress”) in a way an Indian court would be unlikely to use of a male killer, which is precisely the double standard of explanation.

38. Case law in detail — facts, issues, arguments of both sides, decision, principle

Important. The DU syllabus prescribes no case law for Topic 2 (the “Cases” column in the syllabus is blank for this topic). The cases below are therefore illustrative: they are the vehicles through which you demonstrate that these theories operate inside Indian law. Two of them — In Re Maragathan and Shreerangayee — are used in the DU solved-paper answer to the 2022 economic-theory question, and Kailash Kaur, Pawan Kumar and Ediga Anamma appear in the prescribed Srivastava reading itself (briefed at §35). What follows are the remaining essential briefs.
Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab(1980) 2 SCC 684 : AIR 1980 SC 898 (Constitution Bench, 4:1)classical v. positivist
Facts
Bachan Singh, already convicted of murder and released after serving his sentence, was living with his cousin Hukam Singh. He was found to have murdered Desa Singh, Durga Bai and Veeran Bai with an axe while they slept. He was convicted under s.302 IPC and sentenced to death; the High Court confirmed. He appealed by special leave, and the appeal was referred to a Constitution Bench because of doubts expressed in Rajendra Prasad v. State of U.P. (1979) 3 SCC 646.
Issues
(i) Whether the death penalty provided for murder in s.302 IPC is unconstitutional. (ii) Whether the sentencing procedure in s.354(3) CrPC, 1973 — which requires “special reasons” for a death sentence — is unconstitutional as conferring unguided discretion.
Arguments for the appellant
(i) Capital punishment is cruel, degrading and unusual, violates Articles 14, 19 and 21, and is not a “reasonable restriction” under Art. 19(2)–(6); it serves no penological purpose as deterrence is unproved. (ii) The discretion under s.354(3) is unguided and therefore arbitrary, offending Art. 14; identical offences receive different sentences depending on the bench. (iii) The international trend (and the ICCPR) is abolitionist. (iv) Retribution is not a legitimate penal objective in a reformative constitutional order.
Arguments for the State
(i) Art. 21 expressly contemplates deprivation of life “according to procedure established by law”, and Entry 1 of List III authorises the legislature to prescribe it; the Constituent Assembly consciously did not abolish it. (ii) The Law Commission’s 35th Report (1967) recommended retention in view of India’s conditions. (iii) s.354(3) does not confer unguided discretion but structures it by making life imprisonment the rule and death the exception requiring special reasons, and s.235(2) guarantees a hearing on sentence. (iv) Deterrence and the protection of society justify retention for the gravest cases.
Decision
By 4:1 (Bhagwati J. dissenting) the Court upheld the constitutionality of the death penalty and of s.354(3). It laid down that the death sentence may be imposed only in the “rarest of rare cases when the alternative option is unquestionably foreclosed”, and that the sentencing court must consider both aggravating and mitigating circumstances, giving the mitigating factors a liberal and expansive construction. The illustrative mitigating circumstances include: the offence was committed under extreme mental or emotional disturbance; the age of the accused (very young or very old); the probability that the accused would not commit acts of violence and would be reformed and rehabilitated (the burden being on the State to prove the contrary); that the accused acted under duress or domination of another; and that he was mentally defective so that his capacity to appreciate the criminality of his conduct was impaired.
Principle evolved
The rarest-of-rare doctrine, and with it the constitutional command that sentencing must weigh the crime AND the criminal. Criminologically, the offence-based tariff is classical and the mitigating-circumstances inquiry is positivist; Bachan Singh is the Indian synthesis of the two schools. Bhagwati J.’s dissent (delivered in 1982) is the strongest Indian statement of the radical critique: the death penalty falls overwhelmingly on “the poor, the illiterate and the underprivileged”.
Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab(1983) 3 SCC 470classical proportionality
Facts
In the course of a family feud between the Machhi Singh and Amar Singh factions, seventeen persons were killed in five connected incidents in five villages on the night of 12–13 August 1977, by a group led by Machhi Singh. Several accused were convicted under s.302 read with s.34 IPC and sentenced to death.
Issue
How is the “rarest of rare” formula in Bachan Singh to be applied? What categories of case attract the extreme penalty?
Arguments for the appellants
Individual roles differed; mitigating circumstances, including provocation arising from the feud and the absence of prior conviction for some accused, required life imprisonment; Bachan Singh requires the alternative to be “unquestionably foreclosed”, which was not shown for each accused.
Arguments for the State
A planned, organised massacre of seventeen persons including women and children, executed across five villages in a single night to annihilate a rival family, shocks the collective conscience of the community and is the paradigm of the rarest of rare case.
Decision
The Court confirmed the death sentence on three of the accused and commuted the rest. It formulated five categories in which the community may demand capital punishment: (1) manner of commission — extremely brutal, grotesque, diabolical, revolting or dastardly; (2) motive — total depravity and meanness; (3) anti-social or socially abhorrent nature — e.g. bride burning, dowry death, or the murder of a member of a Scheduled Caste; (4) magnitude — multiple murders of a family or a community; (5) personality of the victim — an innocent child, a helpless woman, an old or infirm person, a public figure killed for political reasons. It also prescribed two questions: (a) is there something uncommon about the crime that renders life imprisonment inadequate? (b) are the circumstances such that there is no alternative but to impose death even after according maximum weightage to the mitigating circumstances?
Principle evolved
Sentencing must balance the aggravating and mitigating circumstances, and the “collective conscience of the community” may be invoked. Criminologically this is Durkheim in the Supreme Court: punishment as the reaffirmation of the collective conscience. Note the criticism — later benches (Santosh Kumar Bariyar v. State of Maharashtra (2009) 6 SCC 498; Sangeet v. State of Haryana (2013) 2 SCC 452) held that the Machhi Singh categories improperly shifted the focus back from the criminal to the crime, and that a “balance sheet” approach is not what Bachan Singh required. See now Manoj v. State of M.P. (2023) 2 SCC 353 and the suo motu reference in In Re: Framing Guidelines Regarding Potential Mitigating Circumstances (2022) on mandatory pre-sentence psychological and social reports — positivism returning to Indian sentencing.
Bishnu Deo Shaw v. State of West Bengal(1979) 3 SCC 714positivist individualisation
Facts
The appellant, a young man, was convicted of a brutal murder and sentenced to death; the sentence was confirmed by the High Court.
Issue
Whether the sentencing court had complied with s.235(2) CrPC by affording a genuine opportunity to be heard on sentence, and whether the social and personal circumstances of the offender had been considered.
Arguments
Appellant: the “hearing” on sentence was a formality; no material about his age, upbringing, socio-economic background or capacity for reform was elicited; the death sentence could not stand. State: the crime was brutal; the record disclosed no mitigating material.
Decision
Chinnappa Reddy J. commuted the sentence to life imprisonment, emphasising that s.235(2) is not a formality but a substantive right requiring the court to elicit all information bearing on the offender, and that the trial court must actively gather material about his background before choosing between death and life.
Principle evolved
Sentencing is a separate stage with its own evidentiary process directed at the offender, not the offence. This is the procedural machinery through which positivist individualisation enters Indian law; it is now s.258(2) BNSS. Compare Santa Singh v. State of Punjab (1976) 4 SCC 190 and Allauddin Mian v. State of Bihar (1989) 3 SCC 5, where the Court held that the sentencing court should ordinarily give a fresh date for the hearing on sentence so that real material can be produced.
Chhanni v. State of Uttar Pradesh(2006) 5 SCC 396probation / reformation
Facts
The appellant was convicted of an offence and sought the benefit of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958, which the courts below had declined without considering the statutory scheme.
Issue
The scope of ss.3, 4 and 6 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958, and the duty of a court before sentencing a young offender to imprisonment.
Arguments
Appellant: the Act is a reformative measure; s.6 creates a bar on imprisoning an offender under 21 for an offence not punishable with life or death unless the court records reasons after considering the probation officer’s report. State: the benefit is discretionary and the nature of the offence disentitled him.
Decision
The Supreme Court explained the object of the Act — “to prevent the conversion of youthful offenders into obdurate criminals as a result of their association with hardened criminals of mature age within the walls of a prison” — and held that s.6 imposes a positive duty on the court to consider the report of the probation officer and to record reasons if it declines probation.
Principle evolved
The reformative theory is not judicial sentiment but a statutory mandate. Note the reasoning: imprisonment is dangerous because of association with hardened criminals — the Supreme Court adopting Sutherland’s differential association as the premise of a sentencing rule.
Sunil Batra (II) v. Delhi Administration(1980) 3 SCC 488prison as a school of crime
Facts
A letter from Sunil Batra, a convict in Tihar Jail, to Krishna Iyer J. alleged that a fellow prisoner, Prem Chand, had been tortured by a head warder who had driven a baton into his anus to extort money from his visiting relatives. The letter was treated as a habeas corpus petition.
Issues
(i) Whether a letter can be treated as a writ petition (the birth of epistolary jurisdiction). (ii) Whether the fundamental rights of a prisoner survive incarceration and can be enforced against the prison administration. (iii) What institutional safeguards are required against custodial violence and prison contamination.
Arguments
Petitioner: Articles 14, 19, 21 do not stop at the prison gate; the prisoner retains all rights except those necessarily lost by incarceration; torture for extortion is a gross violation. State: prison discipline is a matter for the executive under the Prisons Act, 1894 and the jail manuals; judicial intervention would be unworkable.
Decision
The Court held that “a prisoner does not shed his fundamental rights at the prison gate”, that Article 21 protects against torture and inhuman treatment in custody, and issued detailed directions — district magistrates and sessions judges to visit prisons and hear grievances; grievance boxes; access to lawyers and visitors; the separation of classes of prisoners; and the availability of habeas corpus for conditions of confinement. Krishna Iyer J. described the prison as a place where “the young and the first offender learn crime from the hardened”.
Principle evolved
Judicial supervision of prisons, epistolary jurisdiction, and the express recognition that imprisonment itself is criminogenic — the strongest Indian authority for differential association as a justification for non-custodial sentencing and for classification. Read with Sheela Barse v. State of Maharashtra (1983) 2 SCC 96 and Sheela Barse v. Union of India (1986) 3 SCC 596 (children and women in custody), and Sunil Batra (I) (1978) 4 SCC 494 (solitary confinement and bar fetters).
Sukanya Shantha v. Union of India2024 INSC 753 (3 October 2024)radical / conflict criminology
Facts
A journalist’s PIL, based on her investigation, showed that the prison manuals of at least eleven States assigned labour inside prisons on the basis of caste — sweeping and manual scavenging to persons from Scheduled Castes and denotified tribes, cooking to “higher” castes — and segregated barracks accordingly. Several manuals also contained provisions targeting members of “denotified tribes” and “habitual offenders” in terms descended from the Criminal Tribes Act, 1871.
Issues
(i) Whether caste-based division of labour and segregation inside prisons violates Articles 14, 15, 17, 21 and 23. (ii) Whether the “habitual offender” provisions in State manuals, as applied to denotified tribes, are constitutional.
Arguments for the petitioner
The manuals institutionalise untouchability (Art. 17) and forced labour (Art. 23) within the State’s own custody; they perpetuate the colonial construction of “criminal tribes”; they violate the substantive equality guarantee and the right to live with dignity under Art. 21.
Arguments for the Union/States
The provisions were archaic survivals of colonial manuals, not deliberately discriminatory in intent; allocation of work was administrative and based on aptitude; the Model Prison Manual 2016 and the Model Prisons and Correctional Services Act, 2023 already provide a corrective.
Decision
The Supreme Court (Chandrachud CJI) struck down the caste-based provisions in the State prison manuals as unconstitutional, held that caste cannot be the basis for the division of labour or segregation in prisons, directed all States and Union Territories to revise their manuals within three months, directed that the “caste” column in prisoners’ registers be deleted, read down the references to “habitual offenders” so that they cannot be used against denotified tribes, and registered a suo motu case to monitor compliance.
Principle evolved
“Discrimination against the marginalised castes cannot continue behind the prison walls.” Criminologically, this is conflict and radical criminology vindicated: the state’s own penal apparatus was shown to encode the interests and prejudices of the dominant group, exactly as Quinney’s second and third propositions predict, and the colonial legacy of the Criminal Tribes Act — positivist born-criminality turned into legislation — was finally excised from the institution.
Prakash Singh v. Union of India(2006) 8 SCC 1conflict — control of the police power
Facts
A former Director General of Police filed a PIL in 1996 alleging that the police, governed by the colonial Police Act, 1861, were subject to illegitimate political control, arbitrary transfers and pressure, and were used as an instrument of the ruling party rather than as a law-enforcement agency.
Issues
Whether the Court could direct structural police reform to insulate the police from political interference and secure accountability.
Arguments
Petitioner: the National Police Commission (1979–81), the Ribeiro, Padmanabhaiah and Malimath Committees had all recommended reform and been ignored; Articles 14 and 21 require a police force that is functionally autonomous and accountable. States: police is a State subject (Entry 2, List II); judicial directions would trespass on legislative and executive domains.
Decision
The Court issued seven binding directions: constitute a State Security Commission; merit-based selection of the DGP with a minimum two-year tenure; two-year tenure for operational field officers; separation of investigation from law and order; a Police Establishment Board for transfers and postings; Police Complaints Authorities at State and district levels; and a National Security Commission at the Centre.
Principle evolved
The criminal justice apparatus is itself a site of group conflict: whoever controls the police controls the practical content of the criminal law. Vold’s proposition that politics is “a process of interaction and adjustment between groups struggling for control of the police power of the state” is the analytical premise of this judgment. Compliance remains poor — which is itself a conflict-theoretic datum.
In Re MaragathanAIR 1961 Mad. 498Bonger / economic theory
Facts
The accused, in a condition of extreme destitution, committed theft of food/articles of small value to relieve immediate hunger.
Issue
What sentence is appropriate where the offence is committed under the compulsion of extreme poverty and hunger? Can poverty operate as a mitigating, or even exculpating, circumstance?
Arguments
Accused: the act was the product of necessity, not of dishonesty in any meaningful moral sense; imprisonment would serve no penological purpose and would destroy the family. Prosecution: the ingredients of theft were made out; poverty is no defence in law, and a general licence to steal cannot be created.
Decision
The Madras High Court, while sustaining the legal position that want is not a defence, treated extreme economic want as a powerful mitigating circumstance and dealt with the offender leniently, observing that the criminal law ought not to be used to punish hunger, and that the responsibility lay substantially with the social and economic order.
Principle evolved
Judicial recognition of the economic causation of crime at the sentencing stage. The case is the standard Indian authority cited for Bonger’s thesis, and it is used in the DU solved answer to the 2022 COVID/daily-wagers question.
Shreerangayee v. State of Madras1973 Cr LJ 13 (Mad.)Bonger / economic theory
Facts
A destitute mother, abandoned and unable to feed her children after days without food, killed (or attempted to kill) her children and then attempted to end her own life. She survived and was prosecuted.
Issues
(i) Whether the acts were done with the requisite mens rea or under an overwhelming compulsion of circumstance. (ii) What sentence the court should impose where destitution is the operative cause.
Arguments
Accused: she acted under an irresistible compulsion born of starvation and despair; her state of mind was the product of social abandonment; punishment would be pointless cruelty. Prosecution: the acts were deliberate; the law protects the lives of children absolutely; leniency would create a dangerous precedent.
Decision
The conviction was recorded, but the High Court held the extreme socio-economic compulsion to be determinative at the stage of sentence, extended the benefit of a lenient/probationary disposal, and observed in substance that it was society, and not the mother alone, that stood accused.
Principle evolved
Where the state has failed in its obligations, the criminal law must not visit the full rigour of punishment on the victim of that failure. Read with Art. 21 and the right to food (PUCL v. Union of India, the Right to Food case), the National Food Security Act, 2013, and In Re Problems and Miseries of Migrant Labourers (2020). This is Bonger, Merton and left realism speaking through an Indian High Court.
Keshub Mahindra v. State of Madhya Pradesh(1996) 6 SCC 129 (the Bhopal case)white-collar / radical
Facts
On the night of 2–3 December 1984, methyl isocyanate leaked from the Union Carbide India Ltd plant at Bhopal, killing thousands immediately and injuring more than five lakh persons. The accused, including the chairman of UCIL, were charged under s.304 Part II IPC (culpable homicide not amounting to murder) and other provisions.
Issue
Whether, on the material then available, the charge could be framed under s.304 Part II (knowledge that the act was likely to cause death) or only under s.304-A (causing death by a rash or negligent act, maximum two years).
Arguments
Prosecution/State: the accused knew of the hazardous nature of MIC, of the repeated safety failures and of prior warnings; that knowledge satisfied the second part of s.299 and warranted s.304 Part II. Accused: there was no material to show knowledge that their acts were likely to cause death; at highest, negligence in the operation of the plant was alleged, attracting s.304-A.
Decision
The Supreme Court held that the material did not support a charge under s.304 Part II and directed that the accused be tried under s.304-A read with ss.336, 337 and 338 IPC. In 2010 the trial court convicted seven accused under s.304-A and imposed the statutory maximum of two years and a fine of ₹1 lakh each. The curative petition seeking restoration of the s.304 Part II charge was dismissed in 2011; the Union’s curative petition for enhanced compensation was dismissed in March 2023.
Principle evolved
Doctrinally, the case concerns the threshold of “knowledge” under s.299 IPC. Criminologically, it is the canonical Indian illustration of Sutherland’s thesis: the gravest industrial disaster in history produced a maximum sentence of two years, while ordinary theft under s.303(2) BNS carries three. It also illustrates differential implementation — the deployment of civil settlement (the 1989 US$470 million settlement in Union Carbide Corporation v. Union of India (1991) 4 SCC 584) in place of penal sanction — and supplies the strongest Indian evidence for the radical criminologists’ claim about the crimes of the powerful. Contrast the deterrent posture of M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) 1 SCC 395 (Oleum Gas Leak), which created the rule of absolute liability, and note the statutory response: the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991, and the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.
Harsh Mander v. Union of India2018 SCC OnLine Del 10427conflict — Chambliss on vagrancy
Facts
Petitions challenged the Bombay Prevention of Begging Act, 1959, as extended to Delhi, under which a person found “having no visible means of subsistence and wandering about” could be arrested without warrant, summarily tried and detained in a certified institution for up to three years (ten years on a second “conviction”).
Issues
Whether criminalising begging violates Articles 14, 19(1)(a), 19(1)(d) and 21.
Arguments for the petitioners
Begging is a manifestation of destitution, not of criminality; the Act punishes a status rather than an act; the definition is vague and over-broad; it violates the right to free speech (soliciting alms is communication), to move freely, and to live with dignity, and is a failure of the State’s own obligations under Arts. 21, 39 and 41.
Arguments for the State
The Act is protective and rehabilitative; it addresses organised begging rackets and public nuisance; a decriminalising amendment was under consideration.
Decision
The Delhi High Court struck down 25 sections of the Act insofar as they criminalise begging, holding: “Criminalising begging is a wrong approach to deal with the underlying causes of the problem… the State is under an obligation to provide the basic necessities”; people beg “not because they wish to, but because they must”. Provisions relating to trafficking of persons for begging were left intact.
Principle evolved
A person cannot be criminalised for being poor. This is Chambliss’s vagrancy analysis (§28.6) adopted by an Indian constitutional court, and the natural companion to Olga Tellis and Sukanya Shantha.
Smt. Madhuri Mukund Chitnis v. Mukund Martand Chitnis1992 Cr LJ 111 (Bom)female victimity
Facts
A wife complained of sustained cruelty and harassment by her husband in the matrimonial home, including conduct causing grave mental injury; questions arose as to the scope of “cruelty” under s.498-A IPC and as to the proper approach of criminal courts to matrimonial complaints.
Issues
What constitutes “cruelty” under the Explanation to s.498-A, and whether mental cruelty unaccompanied by physical violence suffices.
Arguments
Complainant: the Explanation expressly covers conduct likely to cause “grave injury or danger to life, limb or health (whether mental or physical)”; persistent humiliation and harassment fall squarely within clause (a). Accused: ordinary matrimonial wear and tear cannot be criminalised; s.498-A requires conduct of a grave character.
Decision
The Bombay High Court held that mental cruelty is fully within the Explanation, that the section must be construed in the light of its social purpose, and that the conduct complained of, if proved, would attract s.498-A.
Principle evolved
“Cruelty” in s.498-A (now s.86 BNS) is not confined to physical violence. The case is cited in the literature on female victimity alongside Kailash Kaur and Pawan Kumar, and it foreshadows the broad definition of domestic violence in s.3 of the PWDVA, 2005, which includes verbal, emotional and economic abuse.

2017 · Q2
Discuss the Anomie and Differential Association theories of crime causation. What would be the most appropriate theory to provide [a] causal explanation for ‘organised crimes’ in India?
Model answer

Introduction. Crime causation has been explained at three levels — the individual (biological and psychological positivism), the situational (classical and rational choice) and the social. Anomie theory and differential association theory are the two leading sociological explanations, and they are complementary rather than competing: anomie explains why there is pressure to deviate at all, and differential association explains who becomes an offender, of what kind, and how.

I. Anomie. “Anomie is a condition of hopelessness caused by a breakdown of rules of conduct and loss of belief and sense of purpose in society or in an individual. It is normally used to describe a state of lawlessness or normlessness. The term was first used by Émile Durkheim. According to him human beings have unlimited desires, the only control to those being social [restraint]; a high rate of suicide and homicide is found in an anomie-ridden society. He argues that crime originates in society and is a fundamental condition of social organization; therefore no social organization can be without crime. He viewed anomie as a state of carelessness existing at times of sudden social change, and in particular the state of normlessness which exists when the insatiable desires of humans are no longer controlled by society.” Develop this with: mechanical and organic solidarity; the three abnormal forms of the division of labour; crime as normal, useful and inevitable; the society of saints; the four types of suicide (egoistic, altruistic, anomic, fatalistic). See §19–§21.

II. Merton’s reformulation. “After Durkheim, the most famous criminologist [to write] on anomie is Merton. According to him, what is encouraged in society is ‘success’, and so goals are more important than the means to achieve them. Deviance is a response to the unavailability of socially approved routes to success. He argues that society will not be anomic if its members only use legitimate means of achievement. …His theory is sometimes known as the means–end theory of deviance.” Reproduce the five adaptations: conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism, rebellion (the table at §22), and note Merton’s own warning that the typology refers to role behaviour in specific situations, not to personality types.

III. Differential association. State the theory in Sutherland’s own structure — the nine propositions (§14) — and then the examiner’s own compressed version: “Criminal behaviour is learnt and not inherited… learnt in interaction with others in a process of communication both verbal and non-verbal… The principal learning of criminal behaviour occurs within intimate personal groups. Impersonal agencies of communication, such as movies, newspapers, etc., play a [relatively unimportant] role… Groups differ in respect to the importance they attach [to] and respect for law, and an individual will be drawn towards or away from crime depending on the values of his intimate associates… The process of learning criminal behaviour by association with criminal and anti-criminal patterns involves all of the mechanisms required in any other learning; this is not restricted to [the] process of imitation.” Add the sixth proposition (excess of definitions favourable to violation of law), the seventh (frequency, duration, priority, intensity), the ninth (the “respiration” objection to need-based explanations), and differential social organisation — “(1) organisation for criminal purposes; (2) organisation against criminal activities”.

IV. Which theory best explains organised crime in India? The model answer is unequivocal: “The most appropriate theory to provide [a] causal explanation of organized crimes in India is Sutherland’s differential association theory. He rightly observes that there is at least one thing common among criminal organizations — that they are bound together by a common hostility towards law, and therefore the members assist and protect each other from law-enforcing authorities. Since protection against arrest and detection is a necessary part of organized crimes, gangsters too often adopt the technique of ‘fixing’ crimes, which is far more important than executing them; for this purpose they try to win over police officials by underhand tactics, or use threat, undue influence or coercion against the victim in order to restrain him from making a complaint to the police. Thus organized crimes involve a number of accessory activities [necessary] for the smooth conduct of their underworld profession.”

V. Support the conclusion with reasons and Indian material. (i) Organised crime is occupational: the recruit is taught techniques (weapons, hawala, extortion protocols), motives (loyalty to the syndicate), rationalisations (“the state is corrupt anyway”) and attitudes — the exact content of proposition 4. (ii) The syndicate is an intimate personal group with high frequency, duration, priority and intensity — the modalities of proposition 7. (iii) Anomie/strain explains the supply of recruits but cannot explain why the recruit becomes a contract killer rather than an ordinary thief; only differential association supplies the content of the offending. (iv) Indian illustrations: the D-Company and the 1993 Bombay blasts; the Chambal dacoit gangs; sand, liquor and coal mafias; cyber-fraud call-centre clusters. (v) Statutory confirmation: s.2(1)(d), (e) and (f) MCOCA, 1999 defines organised crime through a syndicate and “continuing unlawful activity”, and s.111 BNS, 2023 now creates a general offence of organised crime, with s.112 for petty organised crime — the legislature has adopted a group-based rather than an individual-based conception of the offender, which is differential association in statutory form.

VI. Conclusion. Use both: anomie at the macro level to explain the pressure and the pool, and differential association at the micro level to explain transmission and form; add differential social organisation to explain why some Indian cities have entrenched syndicates and others do not. Policy follows the diagnosis: widen legitimate opportunity (Merton), break the associations by classification in prisons, witness protection (Witness Protection Scheme, 2018, approved in Mahender Chawla v. Union of India (2019) 14 SCC 615) and attack the protective network by insulating the police (Prakash Singh) and by following the money (PMLA, 2002).

2017 · Q3
“Greatest contribution of [the] positivist school to the development of criminal science lies in the fact that [the] attention of criminologists was drawn for the first time towards the personality of the criminal rather than the crime or punishment.” Elaborate your answer.
Model answer

I. The setting. “During the 19th century, with the development of scientific [method]”, criminology broke with the classical school. The classical school of Beccaria and Bentham had asked only what was done and what punishment is proportionate; it treated all offenders as identical rational calculators. The positivists asked instead who did it and why. Sketch the intellectual background: Darwin’s Origin of Species (1859); Comte’s positivism; the 1827 French judicial statistics and the cartographic work of Guerry and Quetelet, which showed that crime rates were stable and therefore socially caused (§7).

II. The five propositions the examiner rewards (reproduce them as a numbered list — they are the spine of the model answer): the positivist school — (1) shifted attention from the crime to the criminal; (2) “advocated [the] treatment method instead of punishment, and held that criminals be punished not according to the gravity of the crime but according to the circumstances associated with it”; (3) “stressed [the] personality of [the] offender rather than his criminal act”; (4) “defined crime in sociological terms”; (5) “it was a 19th-century doctrine which focused on scientific methods and shifted focus from retribution to corrective methods of treatment”. Add: “The positive school is said to have given [birth] to the modern sociological school, which regards [the] criminal as [a] by-product of his conditions and experiences of life. It has been characterized as a ‘rational school of crime’ which recommends the application of humanitarian method[s] for [the] treatment of criminals. …The modern search for [a] multi-factor explanation of crime is usually attributed to Lombroso.”

III. Lombroso. L’Uomo Delinquente (1876); the Villella post-mortem and the median occipital fossa; the “born criminal” and atavism; the stigmata. His four categories as set out in the model answer: (a) born criminals, “people with atavistic characteristics… [who] possessed [the] physical characteristic[s] of [their] ancestor[s]”, who “can’t be reformed as environment ha[s] no impact on them”; (b) insane criminals — idiots, imbeciles, epileptics, the paranoiac, the alcoholic; (c) criminaloids — occasional offenders, pushed into crime by circumstance, emotion or opportunity; (d) criminals by passion — including political criminals, who offend under an irresistible emotional force. Three criticisms: (i) his samples were prisoners without an adequate control group; (ii) Charles Goring’s The English Convict (1913) compared 3,000 convicts with non-criminal controls and found no distinct physical criminal type; (iii) as Taft observed, Lombroso mistook the effects of poverty, malnutrition and hard labour for inherited stigmata. Note also the concession in his later work — the born criminal accounts for only about 35 per cent.

IV. Ferri. Criminal Sociology (1884). Crime is caused by physical (climate, geography, season), anthropological (age, sex, physique, psychological constitution) and social (population density, religion, economy, education, industry) factors. His Law of Criminal Saturation: “as a given volume of water at a given temperature will dissolve a fixed quantity of chemical substance and not an atom more or less, so in a given social environment with definite individual and physical conditions a fixed number of delicts, no more, no less, can be committed”. His five classes of criminal — born or instinctive, insane, passionate, occasional, habitual. He rejected moral responsibility for social accountability, proposed penal substitutes (economic, political, scientific, legislative, educational and administrative measures that remove the conditions of crime) and the indeterminate sentence; his draft Italian penal code of 1921 (the “Ferri Project”) was never enacted.

V. Garofalo. Criminologia (1885), who gave the discipline its name. Crime is not a legal but a moral anomaly: “natural crime” is conduct that offends the two basic altruistic sentiments — PITY (revulsion against the infliction of suffering) and PROBITY (respect for the property rights of others). His four classes: murderers (in whom both pity and probity are absent — for whom he urged elimination); violent criminals (lacking pity); thieves (lacking probity); and lascivious or sexual criminals (a distinct moral anomaly). His remedies — death, transportation, life imprisonment or enforced reparation — were frankly eliminative; he opposed the classical idea of moral responsibility and supported the elimination of habitual offenders.

VI. Evaluate. Contributions: the birth of criminology as an empirical discipline; the science of the offender; individualisation of sentence; probation, parole, the indeterminate sentence, juvenile justice, pre-sentence reports, prison classification, and the whole apparatus of correction. Criticisms: determinism destroys responsibility and, with it, the moral basis of punishment; the offender becomes an object of treatment with no right to a proportionate sentence and no fixed release date; the method was methodologically defective (sampling, no controls); and the doctrine was politically dangerous — eugenics, sterilisation, and in India the Criminal Tribes Act, 1871, which legislated born criminality for entire communities (struck at in Sukanya Shantha v. Union of India (2024)).

VII. Conclusion. The statement in the question is correct but should be qualified. Modern Indian law is neo-classical in form and positivist in operation: the BNS fixes the range of punishment by reference to the act, while s.258(2) BNSS, the Probation of Offenders Act 1958, s.15 of the JJ Act 2015, the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 and the mitigating-circumstances doctrine of Bachan Singh fix the actual sentence by reference to the offender. The positivist contribution therefore survives not as a theory of causation — atavism is dead — but as the architecture of sentencing and correction.

“Female criminality has largely been a neglected area of study in criminological theory.” Comment, with reference to the major explanations offered for the criminality of women.
Model answer

The full structure is set out at the end of §34 (the eight-step plan). In brief:

  1. The neglect — Williams’s opening: women were “virtually invisible… added as a footnote… distorted to fall in with whatever male theory was being expounded”; “explaining male criminality explained all criminality”.
  2. The paradox — sex is the single best predictor of criminality (the “room of twenty people” illustration); 80% of those convicted of serious crime in Britain are male; women are ~5% of the prison population there and ~4% in India.
  3. Lombroso & Ferrero (1895) — atavism; “the most advanced forms of human are white males, and the most primitive are non-white females”; the stigmata; the three types of female offender — the born criminal, the occasional criminal and the criminal of passion; prostitution as the female substitute for crime; the internal contradiction (his theory predicts a higher female rate); the Sicily error; sex, race and class bias.
  4. Thomas (1923) — the four wishes (new experience, security, response, recognition); female criminality as an “unacceptable use of female traits”; amoral, not immoral; re-education into the female role.
  5. Freud (1925, 1931) — penis envy; the Electra complex; no castration fear and therefore a weak super-ego; deviant women are those who try to be like men; “the birth of a child would be seen as particularly therapeutic as the baby is a substitute for a penis”. Williams’s verdict: “Freud mostly ignores social, economic and political factors.”
  6. Pollak (1950) — the “masked” or hidden character of female crime; deceit learnt in the sexual sphere; women as instigators of crimes perpetrated by men; the chivalry of police, jury and judge. Refuted: “Downes and Rock (2003) conclude that… there is no chivalry towards women in the criminal justice system.”
  7. Hoffman-Bustamante (1973) — differential sex-role socialisation: females are rewarded for conformity while males are often rewarded for rule-breaking; media images; sex-role skills determine the type of crime (household implements rather than weapons; forgery and shoplifting rather than robbery; violence against intimates); differential policing of girls (curfew and running away).
  8. Adler (1975) — Sisters in Crime: the liberation thesis, that emancipation produces a “new female criminal”. Largely refuted — the women in prison are the least emancipated; and see Rita Simon’s more defensible opportunity version.
  9. Carol Smart (1976) — Women, Crime and Criminology: A Feminist Critique: criminology is malestream; one cannot “add women and stir”. Add Carlen (the class deal and the gender deal), Heidensohn (control theory as the best account of female conformity), Hagan (power-control theory), and Daly & Chesney-Lind (the generalizability and gender-ratio problems; the criminalisation of girls’ survival strategies).
  10. 2022 · Q3
Ravi, who has committed an offence under s.394 IPC (voluntarily causing hurt in committing robbery), the punishment for which is imprisonment for life or rigorous imprisonment for a term which may extend to 10 years and fine, pleads for the grant of probation on the basis that the offence is punishable with 10 years’ rigorous imprisonment as an alternative to life imprisonment. Which sections, schools of criminology and theories of punishment can the judge rely on for justifying the release of Ravi on probation?
Model answer

I. Frame the two issues as the model answer does: “(i) theories of punishment; (ii) probation as a form of punishment.”

II. The object of punishment. “The object of punishment is mainly [the] prevention of crime… punishment is given to the offenders to deter them from committing any further wrong against the society as well as [to] deter others from doing the same acts in future.” Per Halsbury’s Laws of England, the object is two-fold: “(i) deterrence of the actual offender from repeating the crime, and any other person from committing the same act in future; and (ii) protection of the society.” Punishment presupposes “(1) a grave ill has been inflicted on the society; (2) it is imposed by someone’s voluntary act; and (3) it is inflicted [on] the criminal or someone who is answerable for his wrong-doings.”

III. The four theories — set them out with their criminological parentage: Retributive (lex talionis, “an eye for an eye”; society’s right and duty to vindicate the wrong by a punishment in exact proportion to the crime) — the ancestor of the classical school; Deterrent (Bentham’s felicific calculus; Beccaria’s certainty, celerity, severity) — classical; Preventive/disablement (incapacitation; Garofalo’s elimination) — positivist in its incapacitative form; and Reformative — “a crime is usually committed as a result of conflict between the character and motive of the criminal; thus punishment is used as a measure to reclaim the offender and not to torture or hurt them. This theory postulates that punishment is justified only if it looks to the future and not to the past” — which is squarely the positivist school.

IV. The statutory provisions the judge can rely on.

  • s.4, Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 — the operative provision. It applies where a person is found guilty of an offence not punishable with death or imprisonment for life. This is the hurdle in Ravi’s case: s.394 IPC (now s.309(6) BNS) is punishable with imprisonment for life, so s.4 is unavailable, and the contention that the alternative ten-year term should be looked at instead has been rejected — the court looks at the punishment provided by the statute, not the punishment it would choose. See Prem Ballab v. State (Delhi Administration) (1977) 1 SCC 173 and Ishar Das v. State of Punjab (1973) 2 SCC 65 on the scope of the Act.
  • s.3 — release after admonition, confined to specified minor offences and to first offenders.
  • s.6 — the strongest provision available: where the offender is under 21 years of age and the offence is not punishable with imprisonment for life, the court shall not sentence him to imprisonment unless it records reasons after considering the probation officer’s report. Again, life imprisonment is prescribed for s.394, so s.6’s bar does not apply in terms — but the policy of s.6 and the reformative principle remain available to the sentencing judge in choosing a term at the lower end.
  • s.360 CrPC → s.401 BNSS — release on probation of good conduct, also limited by the punishment prescribed.
  • s.361 CrPC → s.402 BNSS — where the court could have dealt with the offender under the Probation Act or the JJ Act but does not, it must record special reasons. This is the provision that forces every Indian sentencing court to consider reformation first.
  • s.4(f) BNS, 2023 — community service is now a substantive punishment; and ss.290–300 BNSS (plea bargaining) and s.359 BNSS (compounding) supply further non-custodial routes where available.
  • If Ravi is a child in conflict with law, the JJ Act 2015 displaces all of this — ss.14, 15, 18 and 21.

V. The schools of criminology the judge can invoke. The positivist school (Lombroso, Ferri, Garofalo) for the proposition that the sentence must fit the criminal, not merely the crime, and that treatment should replace retribution; the Chicago/sociological school and Sutherland for the proposition that imprisonment contaminates — the very reason given by the Supreme Court in Chhanni (§38): the Act exists “to prevent the conversion of youthful offenders into obdurate criminals as a result of their association with hardened criminals of mature age within the walls of a prison”; and the neo-classical school for the proposition that free will is qualified by age and circumstance, which is the philosophical basis of mitigation. Cite Bachan Singh for the liberal construction of mitigating circumstances and for the presumption of reformability, and Ediga Anamma and Bishnu Deo Shaw for individualisation.

VI. Conclusion. A candid answer states both halves: as a matter of strict law, probation under s.4 is not available for an offence punishable with life imprisonment, and the plea as framed must fail; but the judge may nonetheless give effect to the reformative theory by (a) recording reasons under s.361 CrPC/s.402 BNSS, (b) imposing the minimum term consistent with the gravity of the hurt caused and Ravi’s antecedents, age, and capacity for reform after a proper hearing on sentence under s.235(2) CrPC/s.258(2) BNSS and a probation officer’s report, and (c) directing compensation to the victim under s.357/s.357-A CrPC (now s.395/s.396 BNSS) — the restorative element. Where the offence is grave, the positivist claim yields to the classical requirement of proportionality; that tension is the permanent structure of sentencing law.

2022 · Q1
[Domestic violence during the COVID-19 lockdown — the candidate is asked to select and apply the criminological theory that best explains the surge, and to consider the legal response.]
Answer skeleton

The phenomenon: the “shadow pandemic” — a sharp rise in complaints to the National Commission for Women during the 2020 lockdown, with the true figure much higher because the victim was confined with the aggressor and cut off from help. Theories, in order of explanatory power:

  1. Routine activity theory (Cohen & Felson) — the strongest fit: a motivated offender, a suitable target and the absence of a capable guardian converged in space and time; the lockdown removed the school, the workplace, the neighbour and the natal family, which are the everyday guardians in Indian domestic life.
  2. Strain/anomie (Merton; Agnew’s general strain) — loss of employment and income, removal of the breadwinner status, confinement and uncertainty produce negative affect, which is displaced onto the least powerful person present.
  3. Frustration–aggression (Dollard) and the psychodynamic account — displacement of aggression onto a safe target; the ego overwhelmed.
  4. Differential association and social learning (Sutherland; Bandura) — the intergenerational transmission of violence: children who witness abuse learn definitions favourable to it, which is why NFHS data show acceptance of wife-beating among both men and women.
  5. Feminist/radical — the structural explanation: domestic violence is not a lockdown pathology but the routine exercise of patriarchal power, made visible when the private sphere became inescapable. Heidensohn’s point (§34.3) that “male violence acts as a very real control… domestic violence may keep them in their place in the home” is exactly on point.
  6. Control theory — the lockdown weakened the informal social controls (kin, community, workplace) that ordinarily restrain the abuser, while intensifying those that restrain the victim.
The legal response: PWDVA, 2005 — s.2(a) aggrieved person, s.3 (physical, sexual, verbal, emotional and economic abuse), s.12 application, ss.18–23 protection, residence, monetary, custody and compensation orders, s.31 breach; the Delhi High Court’s and the Supreme Court’s COVID-era directions for the functioning of Protection Officers and One-Stop Centres; s.85/86 BNS (cruelty); NALSA’s tele-legal services; the NCW’s WhatsApp helpline. Conclusion: the lockdown did not create domestic violence; it removed the guardianship that normally limits its visibility and its frequency — which is a routine-activity conclusion with a feminist premise, and which argues for community-level guardianship (One-Stop Centres, mandatory reporting pathways, Protection Officers with real resources) rather than for further enhancement of penalties.
