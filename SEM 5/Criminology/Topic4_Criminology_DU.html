1. Why Topic 4 matters, and how it is examined

Topic 4 is the one topic in the paper where criminological theory and a working statute meet head-on. The examiner is not testing whether you can recite the Juvenile Justice Act; he is testing whether you can take a set of facts — a seventeen-year-old accused of rape, a fifty-year-old convict who says he was fifteen, a boy produced before a Magistrate with no birth certificate — and work out (a) who decides, (b) on what material, (c) by what procedure, and (d) with what consequence. Every one of the eleven prescribed cases is an answer to one of those four questions.

The four questions the eleven cases answer
Who decides? — Raghbir, Ajeet Singh (forum); Barun Chandra Thakur, CCL v. Karnataka (Board or Children’s Court). On what material? — Rishipal Singh Solanki, Vinod Katara, Rajni (age determination). By what procedure? — Pratap Singh, Rahul Kumar Yadav, Sampurna Behura. With what consequence? — Sheela Barse, Sanjay Suri, Sudesh Kumar (no child in jail; the limits of leniency).
The organising thesis
Indian juvenile justice has swung between two poles — the welfare/parens patriae model, in which the child is a person to be saved, and the justice/due process model, in which the child is an accused with rights. The JJ Act, 2015 introduced a third element that belongs to neither: selective adult treatment of 16–18 year olds for heinous offences. Whether that is a necessary calibration or a retrograde step is the central controversy of the topic, and Ved Kumari’s prescribed article is the standard critique.
The three answer-winning moves
1. Quote the DU material verbatim — the Beijing Rules passages from Pratap Singh and the “no child in jail” passage from Sanjay Suri. 2. Always give the section number, and give it from the 2015 Act — “preliminary assessment” is worth little; “s.15 read with s.14(3) and s.18(3), and Rule 10A of the Model Rules, 2016” is worth a great deal. 3. Distinguish the two dates — the age is reckoned on the date of the offence (Pratap Singh), but the benefit of s.6 of the Probation of Offenders Act is reckoned on the date of sentence (Sudesh Kumar). Candidates confuse these constantly.

2. The CONCEPT of juvenile delinquency

2.1 The definitions — give three, then the Indian statutory one

The DU solved paper’s own formulation PYQ 2022 Q2 “A delinquent juvenile is one who habitually refuses to obey the reasonable and lawful commands of [a] parent or other persons of lawful authority and is deemed to be habitually uncontrolled or wayward. Juvenile delinquency is a serious issue because the future of mankind itself is at stake as a result of the delinquent behaviour of the youth — a national asset.”

JJ Act, 2015 — the Indian statutory positionThe Act abandons the word “juvenile” for the offending child and uses “child in conflict with law”: s.2(13) — “a child who is alleged or found to have committed an offence and who has not completed eighteen years of age on the date of commission of such offence”. s.2(12) — “child” means “a person who has not completed eighteen years of age”Two decisive features: the date of the offence is the reckoning date (Pratap Singh codified), and the label is relational — the child is “in conflict with” law, not a criminal
Why the change of vocabulary matters — say this in the first paragraph of any answer. The 1986 and 2000 Acts used “juvenile”; the 2015 Act uses “child” throughout and reserves “juvenile” only in the title and in s.2(35) (“‘juvenile’ means a child below the age of eighteen years”). The shift is deliberate and follows the UNCRC: “juvenile” had acquired a pejorative, quasi-criminal connotation, whereas “child in conflict with law” locates the child within the rights framework rather than the penal one. The Act likewise replaces “juvenile home” with “children’s home”, “custody” with “care”, “trial” with “inquiry”, “conviction” with “finding”, and “sentence” with “order”. That vocabulary is itself a legal argument, and s.3(xiv) (principle of fresh start) and s.24 (removal of disqualification attaching to conviction) give it teeth.

2.2 The three dimensions of the concept

(a) The LEGAL dimension
An act which, if committed by an adult, would be an offence, but which is committed by a person below eighteen. In India there is no separate list of “juvenile offences”: the child is charged under the ordinary penal law (BNS, POCSO, NDPS, Arms Act, Motor Vehicles Act) and it is the procedure, forum and disposition that differ, not the definition of the offence. The JJ Act’s classification is by gravity — petty (s.2(45)), serious (s.2(54)) and heinous (s.2(33)).
(b) The SOCIOLOGICAL dimension — “status offences”
Many legal systems also treat as delinquent conduct that would not be an offence if done by an adult — truancy, running away, incorrigibility, staying out at night, association with undesirable persons. These are status offences. India has never formally adopted the category for offending; but the parallel is the “child in need of care and protection” under s.2(14), which sweeps in the child “found begging”, “living on the street”, “at imminent risk of marriage before attaining the age of marriage”, and the child who is “found vulnerable and is likely to be inducted into drug abuse or trafficking”. Beijing Rule 3.1 expressly extends the Rules to status offences. The critical point: the welfare jurisdiction can operate as a net-widening device, drawing non-offending poor children into institutions.
(c) The PSYCHOLOGICAL / DEVELOPMENTAL dimension
The whole justification for a separate system is that adolescence is a period of incomplete development of the prefrontal cortex, of impulse control and of the capacity to appreciate long-term consequences; that offending in adolescence is, for the overwhelming majority, transient (Moffitt’s distinction between “adolescence-limited” and “life-course- persistent” offenders); and that exposure to the adult system is criminogenic. The Supreme Court in Barun Chandra Thakur engaged directly with this literature, holding that “cognitive maturation is highly dependent on hereditary factors” and that the Board’s assessment must distinguish these attributes “between a child and an adult”.

The minimum age of criminal responsibility — a live examinable controversy. 5. CAUSES of juvenile delinquency

This is the single most frequently asked part of head (i). Organise the answer in the six families below and give an Indian illustration for each. The DU solved paper itself sets out the classification as a diagram, which is reproduced and expanded here.

5.1 Individual factors

  • Adolescence itself. The DU answer lists “emotional and psychological instability” and “irresistible impulses” as individual causes. Developmentally, adolescence involves heightened sensation- seeking with immature impulse control — the neurological basis for Beijing Rule 4.1 and for the Barun Chandra Thakur discussion of cognitive maturation.
  • Intelligence and learning difficulty. Cyril Burt and the Gluecks found associations with low measured intelligence and educational failure; modern research treats school failure as the operative variable rather than intelligence as such.
  • Mental illness and substance abuse. Reflected in s.2(14)(vii) and (xii) (the child who is mentally ill or who is vulnerable and likely to be inducted into drug abuse or trafficking) and in s.16 of the JJ Act (review of pending cases) and the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017.
  • Physical factors. The positivist tradition of Topic 2 (Lombroso, Sheldon’s somatotypes) has been discredited as a general explanation, but you should mention it and then discard it — the examiner wants to see that you know why it failed.

5.2 Family factors — the most important single family

The broken home, parental neglect, parental criminality or alcoholism, domestic violence, the absence of supervision, inconsistent discipline (either excessively harsh or excessively indulgent), a step-parent, a very large family, and the working mother without child-care support. Hirschi’s control theory (Topic 2) is the best theoretical fit: delinquency occurs where the bond of attachment, commitment, involvement and belief is weak. Statutory reflection: s.3(v) of the JJ Act (“principle of family responsibility — the primary responsibility of care, nurture and protection of the child shall be that of the biological family or adoptive or foster parents, as the case may be”); ss.39–40 (restoration to the family as the primary objective of rehabilitation); s.44 (foster care); s.45 (sponsorship).

Indian illustrationThe Delhi-based studies repeatedly cited in the literature, and the NCRB’s own “Family Background” table, find that a substantial majority of apprehended juveniles were living with their parents at the time of the offence. That finding is important and counter-intuitive: the problem in India is less the absence of a family than the incapacity of a poor, illiterate and over-burdened family to supervise. This is why sponsorship (s.45) — cash support to enable the family to keep the child in school — is a more rational intervention than institutionalisation.

5.3 Social factors

The delinquent peer group — the single strongest proximate predictor, and the empirical core of Sutherland’s differential association. Then: the slum and the delinquency area (Shaw and McKay’s cultural transmission: “traditions of delinquency are transmitted through successive generations of the same region in the same way as language, customs and attitudes are transmitted”); school failure and drop-out, which the NCRB data confirms; migration and rapid urbanisation, which dissolve the informal controls of the village; and caste and communal conflict, which recruits the young.

5.4 Economic factors

Poverty, child labour, homelessness and street life, parental unemployment, and the deliberate use of children by adult criminal syndicates — because the child, if caught, faces a Board and not a court. This is where Topic 3 meets Topic 4: organised crime uses children precisely because the JJ Act protects them. Statutory response: s.83 JJ Act (using a child by a militant or adult group — rigorous imprisonment up to 7 years and fine of ₹5 lakh); s.76 (employment of a child for begging); s.78 (using a child for vending, peddling, carrying or supplying intoxicating liquor, narcotic drugs or psychotropic substances — up to 7 years and ₹1 lakh); s.95 BNS (hiring or employing a child to commit an offence); s.32 NDPS; and the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986.

Real exampleThe Delhi and Mumbai pickpocketing and drug-carrier rings that systematically recruit children, and the use of minors as couriers by narcotics syndicates in Punjab and the North-East, are reported year after year. The legal answer is s.83 and s.78 of the JJ Act plus s.111 BNS (organised crime) against the adults — not harsher treatment of the child.

5.5 Political and “other” factors

The use of children in political violence, riots and insurgency (child soldiers; the Optional Protocol to the CRC on the Involvement of Children in Armed Conflict, 2000, ratified by India in 2005); displacement; and the media. On media the DU answer lists “Movies / pornography”; add social media, online gaming, cyber-bullying and easy access to pornography, and connect to Topic 2’s treatment of media and crime — Bandura’s Bobo-doll experiments, the desensitisation and disinhibition hypotheses, and the counter-argument that media effects are weak, indirect and mediated by predisposition. Note the Indian statutory response: s.67B IT Act, ss.13–15 POCSO, the IT (Intermediary Guidelines) Rules, 2021, and the Cinematograph Act certification regime.

Model structure for “Discuss the causes of juvenile delinquency”. (1) One sentence of definition. (2) State that causation is multi-factorial and that no single-factor theory has survived — cite the Gluecks’ multiple-factor approach and Burt’s finding of an average of nine or ten contributing conditions in each case. (3) The six families, with one Indian illustration each. (4) The NCRB empirical picture. (5) The theoretical frame (§6). (6) Conclude with the legal and policy response, and with the Riyadh Guidelines’ central proposition that prevention requires investment in family, school and community, not in the penal system.

Delinquency follows when the bond weakensDirectly maps onto the Indian data (family, school, employment). The statutory expression is the whole of Chapter VII of the JJ Act — restoration, sponsorship, foster care, after-care (s.46) — all of which are bond-building measures
LABELLING (Becker, Lemert)The official reaction creates the career. Primary deviance is common and transient; it is the label — arrest, trial, institutionalisation — that produces secondary deviance, a delinquent self-concept and a delinquent careerThis theory is written into the JJ Act. s.3(xiv) principle of fresh start; s.24 removal of disqualification and destruction of records; s.74 prohibition on disclosure of identity (6 months and/or ₹2 lakh); s.3(xiii) principle of repatriation and restoration; s.3(x) principle of diversion; s.18(1)(a)–(c) non-institutional dispositions. Quote this: the JJ Act is labelling theory in statutory form
SUBCULTURE AND DRIFT (Matza)Most delinquents are not committed to a deviant value system; they drift between conformity and delinquency, using techniques of neutralisation — denial of responsibility, denial of injury, denial of the victim, condemnation of the condemners, appeal to higher loyaltiesExplains why the overwhelming majority of delinquents desist in early adulthood — the empirical fact that most strongly supports a reformative rather than a punitive response
ECONOMIC (Bonger)Poverty, the egoism of a capitalist economy and the demoralisation of the poorThe NCRB income and education data; child labour; street children; and Gaurav Jain’s “socio-economic empowerment” formula
BIOLOGICAL / POSITIVISTLombroso’s born criminal; Sheldon’s mesomorphy; XYY; hormonal accounts of female delinquencyMention and reject: refuted by Goring, and in any event incapable of explaining the transience of adolescent offending
The one-sentence synthesis for an answer. “No single theory explains juvenile delinquency: control theory explains why most children do not offend, differential association and strain explain why some do, drift explains why almost all of them stop, and labelling explains why the few who are processed by the system do not — which is precisely why the Juvenile Justice Act is built around diversion, non-institutional disposition, the fresh start and the destruction of records.”

7.1 The UN Standard Minimum Rules for the Administration of Juvenile Justice, 1985 (the BEIJING RULES)

VERBATIM — Pratap Singh v. State of Jharkhand, per S.B. Sinha, J. DU “[The] Juvenile Justice Act in its present form has been enacted in discharge of the obligation of our country to follow the United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Administration of Juvenile Justice, 1985, also known as [the] Beijing Rules (the Rules).

Part I of the said Rules provides for the general principles… aiming at promoting juvenile welfare to the greatest possible extent, which would minimize the necessity of intervention by the juvenile justice system and, in turn, will reduce the harm that [is] caused by any intervention. …
Rule 1.4 defines juvenile justice as an integral part of the national development process of each country, within a comprehensive framework of social justice for all juveniles, and, thus, at the same time, contributing to the protection of the young and maintenance of a peaceful order in the society. Rule 1.6 refers to the necessity of the juvenile justice system being systematically developed and coordinated with a view to improving and sustaining the competence of personnel involved in the services… Rule 1.5 seeks to take account of existing conditions in Member States… Rule 2.1 provides for application of the rules without distinction of any kind.

Rule 2.2 provides for the definitions which are as follows:
  “(a) A juvenile is a child or young person who, under the respective legal systems, may be dealt with for an offence in a manner which is different from an adult;
  (b) An offence is any behaviour (act or omission) that is punishable by law under the respective legal systems;
  (c) A juvenile offender is a child or young person who is alleged to have committed or who has been found to have committed an offence.”

Rule 2.3 inter alia provides for making a set of laws, rules and provisions specifically applicable to juvenile offenders… designed: “(a) To meet the varying needs of juvenile offenders, while protecting their basic rights; (b) To meet the needs of society; (c) To implement the following rules thoroughly and fairly.”

The age of a juvenile is to be determined by the Member Countries having regard to its legal system, thus fully respecting the economic, social, political, cultural and legal systems. This has made a wide variety of ages coming under the definition of ‘juvenile’, ranging from 7 years to 18 years or above. Rule 3 provides for extension of the Rules covering (a) status offences; (b) juvenile welfare and care proceedings; and (c) proceedings dealing with young adult offenders… Rule 4 provides that the minimum age of criminal responsibility should not be fixed at too low an age level, bearing in mind the facts of emotional, mental and intellectual maturity. Rule 5 provides that the juvenile justice system shall emphasize the well-being of the juvenile and shall ensure that any reaction to juvenile offenders shall always be in proportion to the circumstances of both the offenders and the offence. Rule 6 provides for scope of discretion.

Rule 7.1 provides for the rights of [the] juvenile: “Basic procedural safeguards such as the presumption of innocence, the right to be notified of the charges, the right to remain silent, the right to counsel, the right to the presence of a parent or guardian, the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses and the right to appeal to a higher authority shall be guaranteed at all stages of proceedings.”

Rule 8 provides for the protection of privacy. Rule 9 provides that the said rules shall not be interpreted as precluding the application of the Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners…

Part II of the said Rules provides for investigation and prosecution, diversion, specialization within the police, [and] detention pending trial. Rule 13 reads as under:
  “13.1 Detention pending trial shall be used only as a measure of LAST RESORT and for the SHORTEST POSSIBLE PERIOD of time.
  13.2 Whenever possible, detention pending trial shall be replaced by alternative measures, such as close supervision, intensive care or placement with a family or in an educational setting or home.
  13.3 Juveniles under detention pending trial shall be entitled to all rights and guarantees of the Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners…
  13.4 Juveniles under detention pending trial shall be kept SEPARATE FROM ADULTS and shall be detained in a separate institution or in a separate part of an institution also holding adults.
  13.5 While in custody, juveniles shall receive care, protection and all necessary individual assistance — social, educational, vocational, psychological, medical and physical — that they may require in view of their age, sex and personality.”

Part III provides for adjudication and disposition… Rule 15 provides for legal counsel, parents and guardians. Rule 16 provides for Social Inquiry Reports. Rule 16.1 reads: “In all cases except those involving minor offences, before the competent authority renders a final disposition prior to sentencing, the background and circumstances in which the juvenile is living or the conditions under which the offence has been committed shall be properly investigated so as to facilitate judicious adjudication of the case by the competent authority.”

Rule 17 provides for guiding principles in adjudication and disposition:
  “17.1 The disposition of the competent authority shall be guided by the following principles:
  (a) The reaction taken shall always be in proportion not only to the circumstances and the gravity of the offence but also to the circumstances and the needs of the juvenile as well as to the needs of the society;
  (b) Restrictions on the personal liberty of the juvenile shall be imposed only after careful consideration and shall be limited to the possible minimum;
  (c) Deprivation of personal liberty shall not be imposed unless the juvenile is adjudicated of a serious act involving violence against another person or of persistence in committing other serious offences, and unless there is no other appropriate response;
  (d) The well-being of the juvenile shall be the guiding factor in the consideration of her or his case.
  17.2 CAPITAL PUNISHMENT shall not be imposed for any crime committed by juveniles.
  17.3 Juveniles shall not be subject to CORPORAL PUNISHMENT.
  17.4 The competent authority shall have the power to discontinue the proceedings at any time.”

It has been pointed out that the main difficulty in formulating guidelines for the adjudication of young persons stems from the fact that there are unresolved conflicts of a philosophical nature, such as the following:
  (a) Rehabilitation versus just result;
  (b) Assistance versus repression and punishment;
  (c) Reaction according to the singular merits of an individual case versus reaction according to the protection of society in general;
  (d) General deterrence versus individual incapacitation.”
Those four “unresolved conflicts of a philosophical nature” are the examination question. The whole debate about s.15 of the 2015 Act — whether a 16-year-old who commits a heinous offence should be tried as an adult — is conflict (c) and conflict (d) in the concrete. Open or close any essay on the 2015 Act with them.

7.3 How international law enters Indian law — the Pratap Singh passage

VERBATIM — Pratap Singh DU “The Juvenile Justice Act specially refers to international law. The relevant provisions of the Rules are incorporated therein. 8. Evolution of juvenile justice law in India — 1850 to 2021

DU PYQ 2017 Q5(a) — “describe the evolution and development of law relating to juvenile justice in India”

8.1 Before Independence — the DU model answer’s account

VERBATIM — DU solved paper, 2017, Answer 5(a) PYQ “Before Independence: The JJ Act 2015 has taken a step backward in the modern history of juvenile justice in India, which began in 1850 with the passing of [the] Apprentices Act, [the] Reformatory Schools Act, 1897 and [the] Children Act[s], 1920, which extended the segregation of children accused of committing offences at the adjudication stage by establishing separate children[’s] Acts. All these children[’s] Acts provided for sending the children to remand home but permitted sending of children to jail in exceptional circumstances.”
  • Apprentices Act, 1850 — the first Indian statute to treat children differently. Destitute or petty- offending children between 10 and 18 could be bound over as apprentices to a tradesman instead of being imprisoned. Note the logic: the child was to be trained, not punished.
  • Indian Penal Code, 1860 — ss.82 and 83 (doli incapax), now ss.20–21 BNS; and s.27 IPC (now s.28 BNS) on property in the possession of a wife, clerk or servant.
  • Reformatory Schools Act, 1876, replaced by the Reformatory Schools Act, 1897 — a boy under 15 convicted of an offence punishable with imprisonment or transportation could be sent to a reformatory school for a period of two to seven years, but not beyond the age of 18.
  • The Provincial Children Acts — Madras, Bengal and Bombay Children Acts, 1920, followed by similar Acts in most provinces. These created separate children’s courts, remand homes and certified schools — but each permitted committal to jail “in exceptional circumstances”, and there was no uniformity of age or procedure.

8.2 After Independence — the DU model answer’s account

VERBATIM — DU solved paper, 2017, Answer 5(a) PYQ “After Independence: The Children Act, 1960 was passed, which introduced a sex-based definition of child, bringing girls till the age of 18 years and boys till 16 years within its protective umbrella. It also completely prohibited use of police stations or jail under any circumstances for children.

Then came the JJ Act, 1986, which followed the scheme of [the earlier] Act but substituted [the] word ‘child’ by ‘juvenile’. [The] cut-off age remained the same.

When India signed and ratified [the] UN Convention on [the] Rights of the Child in December 1992, [it] set the uniform cut-off age of 18 years for both girls and boys in conformity with [the] definition of child under [the] above convention.

Then JJ Act, 2000 was passed, which extended [the] ban on use of prisons or police station[s] for child[ren] less than 18 years all over India… [There was no] change in either [the] rate of crime or [the] share of juvenile delinquency to total crime. [The] JJ Bill 2014 was passed in Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha despite objections. Thus [the] JJ Act, 2015 was brought in force.”

8.3 The seven objects of the JJ Act, 1986 — reproduced verbatim from the DU material

VERBATIM — Pratap Singh, DU Case Material DU “The Juvenile Justice Act, 1986 is aimed at achieving the following objects:
(i) To lay down a uniform legal framework for juvenile justice in the country so as to ensure that no child under any circumstances is lodged in jail or police lock-up. This is being ensured by establishing Juvenile Welfare Boards and Juvenile Courts;
(ii) To provide for a specialized approach towards the prevention and treatment of juvenile delinquency in its full range in keeping with the developmental needs of the child found in any situation of social maladjustment;
(iii) To spell out the machinery and infrastructure required for the care, protection, treatment, development and rehabilitation of various categories of children coming within the purview of the juvenile justice system. This is proposed to be achieved by establishing observation homes, juvenile homes for neglected juveniles and special homes for delinquent juveniles;
(iv) To establish norms and standards for the administration of juvenile justice in terms of investigation and prosecution, adjudication and disposition, and care, treatment and rehabilitation;
(v) To develop appropriate linkages and coordination between the formal system of juvenile justice and voluntary agencies engaged in the welfare of neglected or socially maladjusted children…;
(vi) To constitute special offences in relation to juveniles and provide for punishment therefor;
(vii) To bring the operation of the juvenile justice system in the country in conformity with the United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Administration of Juvenile Justice.”

And on the purpose of the legislation generally: “The purpose of the Juvenile Justice Legislation is to provide succour to the children who were being incarcerated along with adults and were subjected to various abuses. …The problem of Juvenile Justice is, no doubt, one of tragic human interest so much so in fact that it is not confined to this country alone but cuts across national boundaries. …A review of the working of the then existing Acts, both State and Parliamentary, would indicate that much greater attention was found necessary to be given to children who may be found in situations of social maladjustment, delinquency or neglect. The justice system as available for adults could not be considered suitable for being applied to [a] juvenile. There is also need for larger involvement of [the] informal system and community-based welfare agencies in the care, protection, treatment, development and rehabilitation of such juveniles.”

The three-fold classification is the hinge of the whole Act. 15. BAIL — section 12

s.12, JJ Act, 2015 “12. Bail to a person who is apparently a child alleged to be in conflict with law. — (1) When any person, who is apparently a child and is alleged to have committed a bailable or non-bailable offence, is apprehended or detained by the police or appears or is brought before a Board, such person shall, notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 or in any other law for the time being in force, be released on bail with or without surety, or placed under the supervision of a probation officer or under the care of any fit person:
Provided that such person shall not be so released if there appear reasonable grounds for believing that the release is likely to —
  (a) bring that person into association with any known criminal; or
  (b) expose the said person to moral, physical or psychological danger; or
  (c) [the person’s] release would defeat the ends of justice,
and the Board shall record the reasons for denying the bail and circumstances that led to such a decision.
(2) When such person having been apprehended is not released on bail by the officer-in-charge of the police station, such officer shall cause the person to be kept only in an observation home…
(3) When such person is not released on bail by the Board, it shall make an order sending him to an observation home or a place of safety… for such period during the pendency of the inquiry as may be specified in the order.
(4) When a child in conflict with law is unable to fulfil the conditions of [the] bail order within seven days… such child shall be produced before the Board for modification of the conditions of bail [inserted by the 2021 Amendment].”
The four propositions to state
1. Bail is the rule, even for a non-bailable offence, and even for murder or rape — the classification of the offence is irrelevant. 2. The only three grounds of refusal are those in the proviso; no other ground (gravity of the offence, public sentiment, likely absconding) is available. 3. Reasons must be recorded. 4. Even on refusal the child goes to an observation home or place of safety, never to jail or a police lock-up.
The abuse in practice
Boards routinely refuse bail by reciting the third ground — “release would defeat the ends of justice” — as a formula, without material. The High Courts have repeatedly set such orders aside. Rajni v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2025) (§40) is the most recent Supreme Court authority: the High Court had granted bail because “there were no satisfactory materials on record to conclude that the release of the juvenile would expose him to moral, physical or psychological danger or that it would bring him to be in the association of known criminals”, and the Supreme Court upheld it, holding expressly that a preliminary assessment under s.15 does not by itself justify the denial of bail under s.12.
Authorities to cite
Shakuntala v. State of Haryana; Prakash v. State of Rajasthan; Master Niku Chandra v. State of NCT of Delhi (Del); and for the proposition that the gravity of the offence is not a ground, the consistent High Court line. Add s.3(xii) — institutionalisation as a measure of last resort — and Beijing Rule 13.1: “Detention pending trial shall be used only as a measure of last resort and for the shortest possible period of time.”
The 2021 amendment
s.12(4) is new and important: a child who cannot furnish surety within seven days must be produced again so that the conditions may be modified. It addresses the commonest cause of a child remaining in an observation home despite an order of bail — the inability of a poor family to produce a surety. Compare s.478 BNSS (s.436 CrPC) and Motiram v. State of M.P. (1978) 4 SCC 47.

The central controversy of the 2015 ActDU PYQ 2022 Q2 turns on this

16.1 The provision

s.15, JJ Act, 2015 — as set out in the DU model answer PYQ 2022 Q2 “Section 15 — Preliminary assessment into heinous offences by [the] Board. In case of a heinous offence alleged to have been committed by a child who has completed or is above the age of sixteen years, the Board shall conduct a preliminary assessment with regard to —

Proviso: for such an assessment, the Board may take the assistance of experienced psychologists or psycho-social workers or other experts.
Explanation: ‘for the purposes of this section, it is clarified that preliminary assessment is not a trial, but is to assess the capacity of such child to commit and understand the consequences of the alleged offence.’

s.15(2): where the Board is satisfied on preliminary assessment that the matter should be disposed of by the Board, then the Board shall follow the procedure, as far as may be, for trial in summons case under the CrPC. Proviso: the order of the Board to dispose of the matter shall be appealable under s.101(2).
s.15(3): where the Board is satisfied that there is a need for trial of the said child as an adult, it may order the transfer of the trial of the case to the Children’s Court having jurisdiction to try such offences.
s.18(3) — as set out in the DU model answer PYQ 2022 Q2 “Section 18(3): Where the Board after preliminary assessment under Section 15 passes an order that there is a need for trial of the said child as an adult, the Board may order [the] transfer of the trial of the case to the Children’s Court having jurisdiction to try such offences.”

16.2 The four heads of assessment, explained

HeadWhat has to be assessedAuthority
1. Mental capacity to commit the offenceNot IQ alone. The Board must assess cognitive maturation and the capacity for reasoned, planned action. Barun Chandra Thakur: “cognitive maturation is highly dependent on hereditary factors. Emotional development is less likely to affect cognitive maturation. However, if emotions are too intense and the child is unable to regulate emotions effectively, then intellectual insight/knowledge may take a back seat.” The criticism in that case was that the Board had obtained only the child’s IQ and not a report on his ability to comprehend the offenceBarun Chandra Thakur (§37)
2. Physical capacity to commit the offenceWhether the child was physically capable of doing the act alleged — height, build, strength relative to the victim and the weaponRule 10A, Model Rules, 2016
3. Ability to understand the CONSEQUENCES of the offenceBarun Chandra Thakur: “the term ‘consequence’ under s.15(1) does not mean only immediate consequences, but also far-reaching ones in the future. 16.3 The procedural safeguards — the Barun Chandra Thakur and CCL v. Karnataka rules

  1. Assistance of a psychologist is MANDATORY where the Board has no child-psychology expert. Although s.15(1) proviso and Rule 10A(2) use “may”, the Supreme Court held in Barun Chandra Thakur that “where the Board is not comprising of a practising professional with a degree in child psychology or child psychiatry, the expression ‘may’ in the proviso to section 15(1) would operate in mandatory form and the Board would be obliged to take assistance of experienced psychologists or psycho-social workers or other experts.”
  2. Reasons must be assigned and the order supplied to the child forthwith — Rule 10A(4).
  3. The child shall be presumed innocent while the assessment is made — Rule 10A(3) and s.3(i).
  4. Natural justice applies — s.3(xvi). Barun Chandra Thakur: the order has “serious civil consequences”, and “it is well settled that [where] any order [has] serious civil consequences, reasonable opportunity must be afforded.”
  5. Time-limit. s.14(3) prescribes three months from first production. In CCL v. State of Karnataka (2024) the Supreme Court held that this period is directory, not mandatory, and may be extended by the CJM or CMM for reasons recorded in writing — because no consequence of default is provided in the Act. (Compare s.14(4), which does provide a consequence for petty offences.)
  6. Appeal. An order under s.15 passed by the Board is appealable under s.101(2) to the Children’s Court; the appellate authority may itself take the assistance of experts other than those who assisted the Board. A revision under s.397 read with s.399 CrPC (now ss.438, 442 BNSS) is not maintainable (CCL v. Karnataka).
  7. Guidelines. Barun Chandra Thakur directed the Central Government, the NCPCR and the SCPCRs to frame specific guidelines for the conduct of preliminary assessments, the task being “a delicate task with requirement of expertise”.

16.4 The criticisms of section 15 — the essay content

1. It asks the impossible
No psychological instrument can determine, months after the event, whether a particular adolescent possessed the “mental capacity” to commit a particular offence or to understand its “far-reaching consequences”. The Justice Verma Committee (2013) said so expressly and recommended against any lowering of the age. Boards in practice rely on a brief IQ test — the very defect condemned in Barun Chandra Thakur.
2. It contradicts s.3(i)
The Act itself directs that “any child shall be presumed to be innocent of any mala fide or criminal intent up to the age of eighteen years”. Section 15 requires the Board to find, in effect, that a particular 16-year-old did possess adult capacity — a finding in the teeth of the statutory presumption.
3. It violates international law
Art. 40 UNCRC requires a separate system for every person below 18; General Comment No. 24 (2019) of the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child states that trying children as adults is contrary to the Convention and that there should be no exceptions based on the gravity of the offence. India is a party; s.15 places India in breach.
4. It is arbitrary under Art. 14
Two 17-year-olds who do the same thing may be treated differently depending on an unreviewable psychological opinion; and a 17-year-old accused of an offence with a maximum of 10 years but no minimum is outside s.15 while one accused of an offence with a minimum of 7 is inside it (Shilpa Mittal). The classification is driven by the statute book, not by culpability.
5. It is not supported by the data
Juvenile crime is about 1% of all crime and has been falling (§4). The Standing Committee on Human Resource Development, 264th Report (2015) rejected the Bill on precisely this ground. The DU 2017 script records the point: “[no] change in either rate of crime or share of juvenile delinquency to total crime… passed… despite objections.”
6. The counter-argument — give it
(i) Transfer is not automatic: there are two filters (s.15 and s.19) and an appeal. (ii) Even after transfer the child cannot be sentenced to death or irreducible life imprisonment (s.21), must be kept in a place of safety and not a jail until 21 (s.19(3)), and his case must be reviewed at 21 (s.20). (iii) The victim’s right and the community’s confidence in the system are legitimate considerations — Beijing Rule 17.1(a) itself requires proportionality “not only to the circumstances and the gravity of the offence but also to the… needs of the society”. (iv) Salil Bali and Subramanian Swamy v. Raju upheld the constitutionality of a blanket age of 18 but did not consider the calibrated 2015 scheme.

17.2 Section 21 — the absolute prohibition

s.21, JJ Act, 2015 “No child in conflict with law shall be sentenced to death or for life imprisonment without the possibility of release, for any such offence, either under the provisions of this Act or under the provisions of the Indian Penal Code or any other law for the time being in force.”

Read with Art. 37(a) UNCRC, Beijing Rule 17.2 and the corresponding provision in the 1986 Act (s.22) and the 2000 Act (s.16). Note the crucial drafting change: the 2000 Act said “shall not be sentenced to death or life imprisonment”; the 2015 Act says “life imprisonment without the possibility of release”, which, read with s.19(3) and s.20, contemplates that a transferred child may serve a sentence with the possibility of release. Whether that is a dilution of Art. 37(a) is a good examination point.

Four points that are frequently missed. 19. AGE DETERMINATION — section 94, and the eleven principles

The subject of 4 of the 11 prescribed cases

19.1 The provision

s.94, JJ Act, 2015 — “Presumption and determination of age” “(1) Where, it is obvious to the Committee or the Board, based on the appearance of the person brought before it under any of the provisions of this Act (other than for the purpose of giving evidence) that the said person is a child, the Committee or the Board shall record such observation stating the age of the child as nearly as may be and proceed with the inquiry under section 14 or section 36, as the case may be, without waiting for further confirmation of the age.

(2) In case, the Committee or the Board has reasonable grounds for doubt regarding whether the person brought before it is a child or not, the Committee or the Board, as the case may be, shall undertake the process of age determination, by seeking evidence by obtaining —
  (i) the date of birth certificate from the school, or the matriculation or equivalent certificate from the concerned examination Board, if available; and in the absence thereof;
  (ii) the birth certificate given by a corporation or a municipal authority or a panchayat;
  (iii) and only in the absence of (i) and (ii) above, age shall be determined by an OSSIFICATION TEST or any other latest medical age determination test conducted on the orders of the Committee or the Board:
  Provided such age determination test conducted on the order of the Committee or the Board shall be completed within fifteen days from the date of such order.

(3) The age recorded by the Committee or the Board to be the age of [the] person so brought before it shall, for the purpose of this Act, be deemed to be the true age of that person.”

19.3 The ELEVEN principles of Rishipal Singh Solanki — learn these

In Rishipal Singh Solanki v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2022) 8 SCC 602 (decided 18 November 2021) the Supreme Court consolidated the entire case law into a set of propositions. Reproduce them as a numbered list in any age-determination question.

  1. A claim of juvenility may be raised at any stage of a criminal proceeding, even after final disposal of the case; it may be raised for the first time before the Supreme Court; and delay in raising the claim is not a ground for rejecting it.
  2. An application claiming juvenility may be made either before the court or before the JJ Board. Where it is made before the Board and the Board has reasonable grounds for doubt, the Board shall undertake age determination under s.94(2); and the age recorded by the Board shall be deemed to be the true age (s.94(3)).
  3. Where the claim is made before a court, the court must proceed under s.9(2): hold an inquiry, take such evidence as is necessary but not an affidavit, and record a finding.
  4. The standard of proof differs between an inquiry and a determination. In an inquiry the court records only a prima facie conclusion; in a determination under s.94(2) a declaration is made on the basis of evidence scrutinised and accepted only if worthy of acceptance (Abuzar Hossain v. State of West Bengal (2012) 10 SCC 489, per T.S. Thakur J.). “Directing an inquiry is not the same thing as declaring the accused to be a juvenile.”
  5. For a claim made after conviction, the claimant must produce some material which may prima facie satisfy the court that an inquiry into the claim is necessary; the initial burden is on the person who claims juvenility.
  6. It is neither feasible nor desirable to lay down an abstract formula to determine the age of a person; it has to be on the basis of the material on record and on appreciation of the evidence adduced in each case.
  7. A hyper-technical approach should not be adopted when evidence is adduced on behalf of the accused in support of the plea that he was a juvenile.
  8. If two views are possible on the same evidence, the court should lean in favour of holding the accused to be a juvenile in borderline cases.
  9. Documents under s.94(2) give rise to a presumption of juvenility, but that presumption is not conclusive proof and may be rebutted by contra evidence.
  10. Where the determination is on the basis of school records, they must be considered under s.35 of the Evidence Act (now s.34 BSA) — “any public or official document maintained in the discharge of official duty would have greater credibility than private documents”.
  11. The ossification test cannot be the sole criterion for age determination, and a mechanical view regarding the age of a person cannot be adopted solely on the basis of medical opinion by radiological examination. Such evidence is not conclusive evidence but only a very useful guiding factor to be considered in the absence of the documents mentioned in s.94(2).
Add the two limits on the ossification test from Vinod Katara and Ram Vijay Singh. (i) The radiological examination leaves a margin of two years on either side, irrespective of how many joints are examined (Ram Suresh Singh v. Prabhat Singh; Jyoti Prakash Rai v. State of Bihar). (ii) After about thirty years of age the ossification test cannot be relied upon at all, because the bones have fully fused — hence in Ram Vijay Singh v. State of U.P. (2021) the test conducted when the appellant was 55 “cannot be conclusive to declare him as a juvenile on the date of the incident”, and in Vinod Katara the Court said the same of a test conducted when the applicant was over 50. This is the single most practically important limitation and it recurs in every belated juvenility claim.

25. Ved Kumari — “The Juvenile Justice Act 2015: Critical Understanding”

PRESCRIBED READING DUProf. Ved Kumari, Faculty of Law, University of Delhi
A note on sources. The DU Case Material lists Prof. Ved Kumari’s article as a prescribed reading and gives the URL of the Delhi University repository; it does not reproduce the text. What follows is the structure of the critique as it is consistently advanced in her work — The Juvenile Justice System in India: From Welfare to Rights (2nd ed., 2010), which the 2017 material lists as reading no. 9, and her writing on the 2015 Act. Attribute the arguments to her; do not attribute quotations.

25.1 The central thesis — “from welfare to rights, and back again”

Prof. Ved Kumari’s book traces Indian juvenile justice through three phases: an early welfare phase in which the child was an object of official benevolence with no procedural rights; a rights phase inaugurated by the UNCRC and the 2000 Act, in which the child became the bearer of enforceable entitlements; and — her argument about 2015 — a retreat, in which a sub-class of children is removed from the rights framework altogether and returned to the adult penal system. The DU 2017 model answer states her conclusion in a single sentence: “The JJ Act 2015 has taken a STEP BACKWARD in the modern history of juvenile justice in India.”

25.2 The nine criticisms to deploy

  1. The Act was passed on a false empirical premise. The legislative debate assumed a surge in juvenile crime. The data showed no such surge — juvenile crime remained roughly 1% of total crime, and the apparent “increase” was largely an artefact of the raising of the age of a boy from 16 to 18 in 2000, which enlarged the denominator. The Standing Committee on Human Resource Development, 264th Report (2015) recorded this and opposed the Bill; so did the Justice J.S. Verma Committee (2013), which examined the question after the December 2012 case and recommended against lowering the age.
  2. Section 15 asks a question that cannot be answered. There is no validated instrument by which a Board can determine, after the event, whether a particular adolescent had the “mental capacity” to commit a particular offence or to appreciate its “far-reaching consequences”. The result is that the assessment turns on the personal predilections of the Board and on whatever report a psychologist can produce — the very defect the Supreme Court identified in Barun Chandra Thakur.
  3. It is internally inconsistent. Section 3(i) presumes every child innocent of mala fide or criminal intent up to eighteen; s.15 requires the Board to find the opposite. Rule 10A(3) repeats the presumption of innocence in the very process designed to displace it.
  4. The classification by offence, not by child, is irrational. Whether a child is exposed to s.15 depends on whether Parliament happened to prescribe a minimum of seven years for the offence — a matter of drafting history, not of culpability. Shilpa Mittal exposed the incoherence, and Parliament had to patch it by amending s.2(54) in 2021.
  5. It offends international law. Art. 40 UNCRC and General Comment No. 24 require that all persons under 18 be dealt with by a separate system; India, having ratified the Convention and having recited it in the preamble of its own statute, then legislated contrary to it.
  6. Implementation, not law, was the real problem. The 2000 Act failed not because it was too lenient but because Boards were unconstituted or vacant, members untrained, homes unregistered and probation officers absent — precisely the findings of Sampurna Behura. Toughening the law while leaving the machinery broken changes nothing except the number of children in prison.
  7. The welfare stream was weakened by the same Act that toughened the offending stream. Ved Kumari’s long-standing objection is that the two streams should not be governed by a single statute at all: the “child in need of care and protection” is a victim and the “child in conflict with law” is an accused, and combining them allows the punitive logic of one to contaminate the protective logic of the other — and allows poor children to be swept into institutions under welfare labels.
  8. Institutionalisation remains the default. Despite s.3(xii) and ss.39–46, the system continues to place children in institutions because foster care, sponsorship and after-care are unfunded and unadministered. The NCPCR’s 2020 social audit of child care institutions is the evidence.
  9. The transfer provisions create a permanent stigma. The proviso to s.24(1) denies the removal of disqualification to a transferred child, and the proviso to s.24(2) requires the Children’s Court to retain the records — so the fresh start principle, the Act’s finest innovation, is withdrawn from exactly the children who most need it.
How to use Ved Kumari in an answer. Do not present her as a lone critic. Frame it: “The 2015 Act was opposed by the Justice Verma Committee (2013), by the Parliamentary Standing Committee (264th Report, 2015), by the NCPCR, and, in the academic literature, by Prof. Ved Kumari, whose Juvenile Justice System in India: From Welfare to Rights traces the statutory history and characterises the 2015 Act as a retreat from the rights framework established in 2000. Its defenders answer that transfer is neither automatic nor unreviewable, that s.21 preserves the absolute prohibition on death and irreducible life imprisonment, and that Beijing Rule 17.1(a) itself requires proportionality to the ‘needs of the society’.” Then take a position and justify it.

26.2 The transfer debate — the arguments, arranged for an essay

FOR the 2015 scheme
1. Proportionality. Beijing Rule 17.1(a) requires the reaction to be proportionate “not only to the circumstances and the gravity of the offence but also to the… needs of the society”. A three-year maximum for a 17-year-old who commits a brutal rape and murder is disproportionate on the face of it. 2. It is calibrated, not blanket. Only 16–18; only heinous offences; only after an individualised assessment; two decision-makers (Board and Children’s Court); an appeal; and s.21 retained. 3. Deterrence and the misuse of children by adults. Syndicates use children precisely because the law protects them (Topic 3, §25). 4. Victim-centred justice. The victim’s family has an interest that the system must acknowledge — the argument that moved Parliament after December 2012. 5. Comparative practice. Most common-law jurisdictions provide some mechanism for transferring the gravest juvenile cases (England: the Crown Court for “grave crimes”; the USA: judicial waiver).
AGAINST
1. Developmental science. Adolescent brain development is incomplete; culpability is reduced as a matter of fact, not of mercy. 2. It cannot be administered fairly (§16.4). 3. It breaches the UNCRC. 4. The data do not support it — juvenile crime is ~1% of crime and falling. 5. It is counter-productive: the empirical literature on transfer in the USA shows higher recidivism among transferred juveniles than among comparable juveniles retained in the juvenile system. 6. It punishes the child twice — once by the sentence and again by the withdrawal of the fresh start under the provisos to s.24. 7. Class bias: the children who will be transferred are overwhelmingly poor, unrepresented and unable to produce a favourable psychological report.
The constitutional challenges
Salil Bali v. Union of India (2013) 7 SCC 705 — writ petitions after the December 2012 case sought to read down the 2000 Act and to make juveniles above 16 triable as adults. Dismissed: the age of 18 is based on “a considered decision… after taking into account the various international conventions and the recommendations of expert bodies”, and is not arbitrary. Subramanian Swamy v. Raju (2014) 8 SCC 390 — the argument that s.2(k) of the 2000 Act must be read to exclude a juvenile who possesses the mental maturity to understand the consequences was rejected: the Act embodies a legislative determination that 18 is the line, and courts cannot substitute a case-by-case assessment of maturity. Note the irony: Parliament in 2015 enacted precisely the case-by-case assessment the Supreme Court had refused to read in. Shilpa Mittal v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2020) 2 SCC 787 — “heinous” requires a minimum of seven years; the Court declined to expand the definition and left the fourth category to be treated as serious “till Parliament takes the call”.
The position you should take
A balanced answer concludes: the 2015 scheme is constitutionally defensible but empirically unjustified and administratively unworkable. It is defensible because Art. 15(3) permits calibrated special provision and because s.21 preserves the core Art. 37 protection; it is unjustified because the data show no juvenile crime wave; and it is unworkable because s.15 requires a determination that psychology cannot supply — which is why the Supreme Court in Barun Chandra Thakur had to direct the framing of guidelines that, years later, remain incomplete. The real reform needed is the one Sampurna Behura identified: fill the vacancies, train the members, register the homes, appoint the probation officers.

28. CASE 1 — Sheela Barse v. Union of India (1986)

Sheela Barse & Anr. v. Union of India & Ors.1986 SCALE (2) 230; (1986) 3 SCC 596 and 632; AIR 1986 SC 1773 — Bhagwati C.J. and Ranganath Misra J.; orders of 15 April, 12 July, 5 August and 13 August 1986children in jail · speedy trial
Facts
Sheela Barse, a Mumbai-based journalist and social activist, had for years visited prisons and custodial institutions and interviewed inmates. She addressed the Supreme Court complaining that a large number of children below the age of sixteen were confined in jails across the country — some accused of petty offences, some merely “in safe custody”, some physically or mentally handicapped and some abandoned or destitute — and that many had been in jail for very long periods without trial. The letter was treated as a writ petition under Article 32. The petitioners sought (i) the release of children below sixteen detained in jails in different States; (ii) production of complete information about children in jails; (iii) information on the existence of juvenile courts, homes and schools; (iv) a direction to District Judges to visit jails and sub-jails within their jurisdiction to ensure that children in custody are properly looked after; and (v) a direction to State Legal Aid Boards to appoint duty counsel to ensure legal assistance to children.
Issues
(1) Is the confinement of children in jail, even in a separate ward, permissible under the Constitution and the various Children’s Acts? (2) Is the right to a speedy trial a fundamental right under Art. 21, and what is the consequence of its denial in the case of a child? (3) What are the obligations of the State Governments to create the infrastructure — remand homes, observation homes and juvenile courts — that the Children’s Acts presuppose? (4) Can the Supreme Court issue structural directions and monitor compliance?
Arguments for the petitioners
(i) Art. 39(f) obliges the State to secure that “children are given opportunities and facilities to develop in a healthy manner and in conditions of freedom and dignity and that childhood and youth are protected against exploitation and against moral and material abandonment”; keeping children in jail is the antithesis of that duty. (ii) Every Children’s Act in force in the States forbids the detention of a child in jail; the practice is therefore illegal as well as unconstitutional. (iii) The right to a speedy trial under Art. 21, recognised in Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar (1980) 1 SCC 81, applies a fortiori to a child. (iv) The Court should not accept the plea of administrative inconvenience.
Arguments for the respondents
(i) Several States pleaded that they lacked sufficient remand homes and observation homes, and that children were therefore necessarily lodged in jails. (ii) It was urged that children were kept in a separate ward, segregated from adult prisoners, so no harm resulted. (iii) Detention was in some cases for the child’s own “safe custody”. (iv) Juvenile courts existed on paper.
Decision
The Court rejected every one of those answers in terms that should be quoted:
“It is an elementary requirement of any civilised society, and it had been so provided in various statutes concerning children, that children should not be confined to jail because incarceration in jail has a dehumanising effect and it is harmful to the growth and development of children.”

“If a child is a national asset, it is the duty of the State to look after the child with a view to ensuring full development of its personality. That is why all the statutes dealing with children provide that [a] child shall not be kept in jail. Even apart from this statutory prescription, it is elementary that a jail is hardly a place where a child should be kept. There can be no doubt that incarceration in jail would have the effect of dwarfing the development of the child, exposing him to baneful influences, coarsening his conscience and alienating him from the society.”

“Even where children are accused of offences, they must not be kept in jails. It is no answer on the part of the State to say that it has not got enough number of remand homes or observation homes… It is also no answer on the part of the State to urge that the ward in the jail where the children are kept is separate from the ward in which the other prisoners are detained. It is the ATMOSPHERE of the jail which has a highly injurious effect on the mind of the child, estranging him from the society and breeding in him aversion bordering on hatred against a system which keeps him in jail. …On no account should the children be kept in jail and if a State Government has not got sufficient accommodation in the remand homes or observation homes, the children should be released on bail instead of being subjected to incarceration in jail.” Sheela Barse v. Union of India, per Ranganath Misra J.
The Court issued a series of directions across four orders:
  1. District Judges to nominate the Chief Judicial Magistrate or another Judicial Magistrate to visit every District Jail and Sub-Jail and ascertain how many children below sixteen were confined, the offences charged, the period of detention and the availability of legal assistance, and to report; the Superintendent of every jail to provide full assistance and to allow inspection of registers and interviews with the children.
  2. State Legal Aid and Advice Boards to send two lawyers to each jail once every week to provide legal assistance to children below sixteen.
  3. Every State Government to file an affidavit stating how many remand homes, children’s homes and observation homes existed and how many inmates were kept in them.
  4. Every State to bring into force and vigorously implement its Children’s Act; the Court expressed the view that “instead of each State having its own Children’s Act… it would be desirable if the Central Government initiates Parliamentary legislation on the subject, so that there is COMPLETE UNIFORMITY in regard to the various provisions relating to children in the entire territory of the country”.
  5. States to set up Juvenile Courts, one in each district, with “a special cadre of Magistrates who must be suitably trained for dealing with cases against children”, the Court observing that in many States juvenile courts were “nothing but a replica of the ordinary criminal courts, only the label being changed”.
  6. Time-limits: where a complaint is filed or an FIR lodged against a child below sixteen for an offence punishable with imprisonment of not more than seven years, the investigation shall be completed within three months from the date of filing of the complaint or lodging of the FIR, and if it is not, the case against the child must be treated as closed; and if a charge-sheet is filed within three months, the trial must be completed within a further period of six months, failing which the prosecution shall be quashed.
  7. Every District and Sessions Judge to visit the District Jail at least once in two months, taking particular care about child prisoners, and to draw any infraction to the attention of the administration and of the High Court.
Principles evolved
(1) No child may be kept in jail in any circumstances — neither the absence of remand homes nor segregation within the jail is an answer; if there is no room in a home, the child must be released on bail. (2) The right to a speedy trial under Art. 21 applies with special force to a child, and the Court laid down concrete outer limits (three months for investigation, six months for trial) with the sanction of closure of the case and quashing of the prosecution — a remarkable and rarely-matched remedy. (3) Art. 39(f) is judicially enforceable through Art. 21: the Directive Principle supplies the content of the fundamental right. (4) The judgment established the parens patriae role of the Supreme Court in relation to children and the technique of structural, monitored directions. (5) It called for a uniform central legislation — a call answered by the Juvenile Justice Act, 1986, enacted in the same year.
Significance and afterlife
This is the foundational case of modern Indian juvenile justice. Its directions are now statutory: s.10 JJ Act (never a police lock-up or jail); s.8(3)(xiii) (Board’s duty to inspect adult jails to check whether any child is lodged there); s.14(2)–(4) (time-limits for the inquiry, with termination of proceedings in petty cases); s.4 (a Board in every district); s.4(3) and (5) (special knowledge and induction training for the Magistrate). See also the companion litigation, Sheela Barse v. State of Maharashtra (1983) 2 SCC 96 (women in police custody) and Sheela Barse v. Union of India (1993) 4 SCC 204 (non-criminal mentally ill persons in jail) — the DU material itself notes, in the prisons context, that “jailing of non-criminal mentally ill persons is illegal” and that officers were later “found ignorant of the decision in Sheela Barse”, a telling comment on implementation.

29. CASE 2 — Sanjay Suri v. Delhi Administration (1988) DU verbatim

Sanjay Suri v. Delhi AdministrationAIR 1988 SC 414 — Ranganath Misra, J.Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 132–135
Facts
VERBATIM — opening of the judgment DU “These two applications under Article 32 of the Constitution are in the nature of a public interest litigation. A news reporter and a trainee sub-editor have moved this Court for appropriate directions to the Delhi Administration and the authorities of the Central Jail at Tihar, pointing out features of maladministration within the jail relating to juvenile undertrial prisoners. During the pendency of the proceedings, the Court made several orders with reference to juvenile prisoners and undertrials.”
The Court had initially hesitated to entertain the petitions because a parallel petition was pending in the Delhi High Court, but proceeded because only a limited inquiry into the medical examination of seven juvenile prisoners had been ordered there. On 28 October 1983 it directed the District Judge — chosen because “he is even otherwise visitor at the Tihar Jail and we thought it would be better to send an officer who is ultimately responsible for ensuring proper conditions in the Tihar Jail rather than entrust this work to an outside organisation or agency” — to visit Tihar Jail and inquire into conditions in the juvenile ward. The Report disclosed “a shocking state of affairs”: juvenile prisoners interviewed by the District Judge stated that they “had been subjected to sexual assault by the adult prisoners”, and expressed apprehension of victimisation if their names were disclosed. There were 131 juvenile prisoners confined in Tihar Jail; against a sanctioned capacity of 2,023, the jail housed on average 4,000 prisoners.
Issues
(1) What directions are necessary to protect juvenile undertrial and convicted prisoners lodged in an adult jail from sexual and other abuse? (2) How is it to be ensured that no child within the meaning of the Children’s Act is sent to jail at all, and that juveniles between 16 and 18 are kept separate? (3) What structural reforms of jail administration follow?
Arguments
For the petitioners: the conditions disclosed violate Art. 21; juveniles are being used by adult prisoners for cooking, cleaning and personal service and are being sexually assaulted; the warrant of detention does not disclose the age of the prisoner, so the jail authorities cannot separate children; suggestions for reform were filed in two instalments. For the respondents: the learned Additional Solicitor General did not contest the findings; on the contrary she made her own suggestions for improvement, stated that the adult prisoner named in the Report would be forthwith transferred out of Tihar and kept away from juveniles, and that the warder named would be placed under suspension and not allowed inside the jail premises.
Decision and directions
The Court recorded the interim relief granted: named undertrial juvenile prisoners were released immediately on a bond of ₹500 each, and three convicted juvenile prisoners were released on parole for one month on a like bond, the Court making clear that the order was made “only with a view to protecting them” and not as any finding on the correctness of their statements. The Superintendent was made personally responsible for ensuring that the adult prisoner had no access to the juvenile ward, and it was directed that “no juvenile prisoner is directed to go to the cell of any adult prisoner or prisoners or to do any work for them including cooking or cleaning”. The Court then laid down the following:
VERBATIM — the operative directions DU On the object of the Children’s Act: “We are anxious to ensure that no child within the meaning of the Children’s Act is sent to the jail because otherwise the whole object of the Children’s Act of protecting the child from bad influence of jail life would be defeated. It is also a matter of anxiety for us to see that juveniles between the age of 16 to 18 years who are put in custody in the jail are being kept in separate ward and are [not] allowed to intermingle with adult prisoners because that would also expose them to mal-influences which may prevent their proper rehabilitation.”

On the philosophy of imprisonment: “…those who are in charge of the jail administration from bottom to top must develop the proper approach to deal with the prisoners and undertrials… Whatever may have been the philosophy of punishment in the past, today the prison house is looked upon as a REFORMATORY and the years spent in the jail should be with a view to providing rehabilitation to the prisoner after the sentence is over. That would not be possible over-night and, therefore, cannot be deferred to materialise on the date of release. The wrong side has to be given up and the virtuous way of living has to be acquired. Both are difficult processes. Therefore, the prison house, in case the true purpose is to be achieved, has to provide the proper atmosphere, leadership, environment, situations and circumstances for the regeneration. Members of the staff of the jail from bottom to top (we have purposely not said top to bottom) must be made cognizant of this responsibility and that awareness must be reflected in their conduct. Judicial notice can be taken of prevailing conditions in our jails and what we have stated above is still Utopian. But if a change has to [be] brought about it has to start from somewhere and Tihar Jail, in our opinion, is probably most suited for that purpose being located at the seat of the national capital… This can be the institution to set the move in motion.”

THE AGE-IN-THE-WARRANT DIRECTION — the most important holding: “We have come across cases where the warrant, be it for the undertrial or the prisoner, when sent by the court does not indicate the age of the prisoner authorised to be detained in the jail. This is a very wrong practice and is obviously in breach of the direction issued by this Court. We call upon every Magistrate or trial Judge authorised to issue warrants for detention of prisoners to ensure that EVERY WARRANT AUTHORISING DETENTION SPECIFIES THE AGE of the person to be detained. Judicial mind must be applied in cases where there is doubt about the age — not necessarily by a trial — and every warrant must specify the age of the person to be detained. We call upon the authorities in the jails throughout India NOT TO ACCEPT ANY WARRANT OF DETENTION AS A VALID ONE UNLESS THE AGE OF THE DETENU IS SHOWN THEREIN. By this order of ours, we make it clear that it shall be open to the jail authorities to refuse to honour a warrant if the age of the person remanded to jail custody is not indicated. It would be lawful for such officers to refer back the warrant to the issuing court for rectifying the defect before it is honoured… In exceptional cases, when the warrant is referred back for rectification, the person covered by the warrant may be kept at the most for a week pending rectification… On the basis of the age indicated in the warrant, it shall be the obligation of the jail authorities to find out, so far as Delhi is concerned, whether the prisoner covered by the warrant should be detained in the Tihar Jail or in the Juvenile Jail.”

On segregation of work: “Though the place of stay has now been segregated, there is possibility of contact between the hardened criminals and the juvenile delinquents if there is no proper segregation in assignment of work. We direct that due care shall be taken to ensure that the juvenile delinquents are not assigned work in the same area where regular prisoners are made to work. Care should be taken to ensure that there is no scope for their meeting and having contacts.”

On the Visitors’ Board: “The Visitors’ Board should consist of cross sections of society; people with good background, social activists, people connected with the news media, lady social workers, jurists, retired public officers from the Judiciary as also the Executive. The Sessions Judge should be given an acknowledged position as a visitor and his visits should not [be] routine ones. Full care should be taken by him to have a real picture of the defects in the administration qua the resident prisoners and undertrials.”

On overcrowding: “Over-crowding in jails is a regular feature. As against a sanctioned capacity of 2,023, on the average 4,000 prisoners are lodged in the Tihar Jail. We hope and trust that this aspect will be kept in view, though from a practical point over-crowding may, to a reasonable [extent], have to be tolerated. We hope with the commissioning of the new jail, pressure in this regard to some extent would be reduced.”

On the achievement of the litigation: “As a result of these exercises taken during the pendency of the writ petitions, one substantial achievement has been that Tihar Jail no more accommodates juvenile delinquents and their jail has been separated. On account of the repeated directions from this Court the jail administration has now been obliged to undertake erection of a separate jail as an additional place for housing juvenile prisoners and undertrials… On account of the exposure, the Jail administration has been obliged to place the administration of the jail in the hands of a superior officer.”

The writ petitions were disposed of with these directions, with no order as to costs, the Court placing on record “our appreciation of the services rendered by the petitioners by bringing the matter before the Court.”
Principles evolved
(1) Every warrant of detention must specify the age of the person to be detained, and a jail authority may lawfully refuse to honour a warrant that does not. This is the single most practical rule ever laid down for keeping children out of adult prisons, and it converts age determination from a defence to be raised into an administrative precondition of detention. (2) Physical segregation of sleeping quarters is not enough; segregation must extend to the assignment of work and to all opportunities of contact. This is differential association (Sutherland) accepted as a principle of prison administration. (3) The prison is a reformatory, and rehabilitation must begin on the first day of custody, not on the date of release. (4) The Visitors’ Board must be drawn from a genuine cross-section of society, and the Sessions Judge’s visits must not be formal. (5) Public interest litigation by journalists is a legitimate and valuable mode of enforcing Art. 21 in institutions closed to public view.
Where the case stands today
Its directions are now statutory: s.10 JJ Act (no police lock-up or jail); s.8(3)(xiii) (the Board’s duty to conduct regular inspection of adult jails to check whether any child is lodged there and to take immediate measures for transfer of such a child to an observation home); s.94 (age determination); s.47 (observation homes with segregation by age group, gender and nature of offence); s.54 (inspection committees visiting every three months). The prisons jurisprudence continues in Rama Murthy v. State of Karnataka (1997) 2 SCC 642, In Re Inhuman Conditions in 1382 Prisons (2016) 3 SCC 700 and the Model Prisons and Correctional Services Act, 2023.

30. CASE 3 — Raghbir v. State of Haryana (1981) DU verbatim

Raghbir v. State of HaryanaAIR 1981 SC 2037 — Baharul Islam, J. (3-Judge Bench)Reproduced in the DU Case Material (2017 edn., pp. 98–101)
Facts
VERBATIM — opening of the judgment DU “The question for consideration in this appeal by special leave is whether a person under 16 years of age and accused of an offence under Section 302, Penal Code can get the benefit of the Haryana Children Act, 1974… The undisputed facts are that the appellant along with three others was convicted of the offence of murder and sentenced to imprisonment for life by the Sessions Judge. The appeal was dismissed by the High Court. The appellant then filed an application for special leave… Leave was granted confined to the question of the applicability of the Act to his case. It is also not disputed that the appellant was less than 16 years at the time he first appeared before the trial Court. He was thus a ‘child’ within the meaning of that term under Clause (d) of Section 2 of the Act.”
The point had not been taken in any of the courts below — a fact the Court noted at the end with the observation: “It is a pity that the point urged before us was not urged in any of the Courts below.” The appeal was referred to a three-Judge Bench to avoid a conflict with Rohtas v. State of Haryana [1979 AIR SC 1839], in which s.27 CrPC had not been brought to the Court’s notice.
Issue
Whether s.27 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 — which provides that “any offence not punishable with death or imprisonment for life, committed by any person who at the date when he appears or is brought before the Court is under the age of sixteen years, may be tried by the Court of a Chief Judicial Magistrate, or by any Court specially empowered under the Children Act, 1960, or any other law…” — is a “specific provision to the contrary” within the meaning of s.5 CrPC, so as to exclude the jurisdiction of the children’s court under a State Children Act in respect of offences punishable with death or imprisonment for life. In constitutional terms: is there repugnancy under Art. 254 between the Central Code and the State Act, both being referable to Entry 2 (criminal procedure) of the Concurrent List?
Arguments for the appellant (Mr. Prem Malhotra)
“In view of Section 5 of [the] Criminal P.C. 1973, the appellant would get the benefit of the Act.” Section 5 saves “any special or local law for the time being in force, or any special jurisdiction or power conferred, or any special form of procedure prescribed, by any other law for the time being in force”; the Haryana Children Act, 1974 is precisely such a local law, and it therefore remains wholly intact. The purpose of both the Haryana Act and the Central Children Act, 1960 was to give separate treatment to delinquent children in trial, conviction and punishment for offences INCLUDING offences punishable with death or imprisonment for life — since a child cannot be sentenced to death or life imprisonment under the Act at all.
Arguments for the State (Mr. Bhagat)
Relying on the words “in the absence of any specific provision to the contrary” in s.5, that “Section 27 is the specific provision to the contrary and as such this provision shall affect the Haryana Children Act which is a local law for the time being in force”; and consequently that “all offences are triable under the Act by reason of the provision of Section 27 of the Code so long as they fall within the category of offences ‘not punishable with death or imprisonment for life’” — so that a child accused of murder must be tried by the ordinary court.
Decision
The Supreme Court rejected the State’s submission.
VERBATIM — the reasoning DU “We are unable to accept the submission. As it has been pointed out above, the purpose of the Haryana Legislature as well as of the Parliament in enacting the Haryana Children Act and the Central Children Act (Act LX of 1960) respectively was to give separate treatment to delinquent children in trial, conviction and punishment for offences INCLUDING OFFENCES PUNISHABLE WITH DEATH OR IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE. In our opinion, Section 27 is NOT ‘a specific provision to the contrary’ within the meaning of Section 5 of the Code; the intention of the Parliament was not to exclude the trial of delinquent children for offences punishable with death or imprisonment for life, inasmuch as Section 27 does not contain any expression to the effect ‘notwithstanding anything contained in any Children Act passed by any State Legislature’. Parliament certainly was not unaware of the existence of the Haryana Children Act coming into force a month earlier or the Central Children Act coming into force nearly fourteen years earlier. What Section 27 contemplates is that a child under the age of 16 years MAY be tried by a Chief Judicial Magistrate or any Court specially empowered under the Children Act, 1960. IT IS AN ENABLING PROVISION, and, in our opinion, has not affected the Haryana Children Act in the trial of delinquent children for offences punishable with death or imprisonment for life.

[On repugnancy:] “Criminal Procedure appears in Item 2 of the Concurrent List of the Seventh Schedule of the Constitution. One of the circumstances under which repugnancy between the law made by the State and the law made by the Parliament may result is [where] the provisions of a Central Act and a State Act in the Concurrent List are fully inconsistent and are absolutely irreconcilable. In the case in hand, as we have shown, the relevant provisions of the Code and the Act CAN CO-EXIST. THEIR SPHERES OF OPERATION ARE DIFFERENT.”

[Approving the minority view of Verma J. in the Madhya Pradesh Full Bench, 1978 Cri LJ 585, and holding the majority view “erroneous”:] “Applying the tests indicated by the settled principles, I have no hesitation in holding that there is no real conflict between the provisions of the new Code, particularly Section 27 thereof, and the provisions of the Bal Adhiniyam. In short, the provisions of the new Code clearly SAVE any special or local law like the Bal Adhiniyam and Section 27 of the new Code is merely an ENABLING provision which does not express any contrary intention to undo the saving provided in Section 5 of the new Code. There being thus no conflict or repugnancy, the question of Article 254 of the Constitution being attracted does not arise.” … “With respect, Verma J. has expressed the correct opinion.”

[The order:] “As a result of the foregoing discussions, we allow the appeal, set aside the conviction and sentences imposed upon the appellant and QUASH THE ENTIRE TRIAL of the appellant. We direct that the appellant shall be dealt with in accordance with the provisions of the Haryana Children Act.”
Principles evolved
(1) Section 27 CrPC is merely an ENABLING provision. It does not oust the exclusive jurisdiction of a children’s court under a Children Act, and it does not carve out offences punishable with death or life imprisonment from that jurisdiction. (2) A Children Act is a special and local law saved by s.5 CrPC, and the two operate in different spheres; there is no repugnancy and Art. 254 is not attracted. (3) A child must be tried under the Children Act for every offence, however grave — including murder. The philosophy is that gravity of the offence does not displace the status of childhood. (4) A trial conducted by the wrong forum is a nullity: the conviction and sentence were set aside and the entire trial quashed, even though the point had never been taken below. (5) A plea of juvenility may accordingly be raised for the first time in the Supreme Court — a principle later codified in s.7A of the 2000 Act and s.9(2) of the 2015 Act, and elaborated in Rishipal Singh Solanki and Rahul Kumar Yadav.
The DU solved paper’s own summary
VERBATIM — DU solved paper, 2022, Answer 2 PYQ “Raghbir v. State of Haryana (1981) — In this case, a juvenile was convicted for murder under Section 302, IPC and sentenced to undergo life imprisonment. The Supreme [Court held that the juvenile] should have been tried according to the special law for juveniles, instead of the provisions under CrPC. Therefore, the juvenile should not have been directed to appear before the Trial Court under CrPC in view of Section 27, CrPC, which expressly states that the juveniles must be tried in accordance with the Juvenile Justice Act.”
Note for the answer-book: the modern position is even stronger than in Raghbir. Section 4(1) of the 2015 Act opens with a non obstante clause — “Notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973” — and s.23 forbids joint proceedings with an adult. The corresponding provision in the new Code is s.22 BNSS.

31. CASE 4 — Pratap Singh v. State of Jharkhand (2005) DU verbatim

Pratap Singh v. State of Jharkhand & Anr.2005 (1) SCALE 763; (2005) 3 SCC 551 — CONSTITUTION BENCH (5 Judges); judgment of Sema, J. and a concurring judgment of S.B. Sinha, J.Reproduced in the DU Case Material (2017 edn., pp. 104–117)
Facts and the reference
The appellant claimed that he was a juvenile on the date of the alleged offence but had ceased to be one by the time he was produced before the competent authority. Two earlier decisions of the Supreme Court were in direct conflict: Umesh Chandra v. State of Rajasthan (1982) 2 SCC 202 had held that “the relevant date for applicability of the Act is the date on which the offence takes place”; whereas in Arnit Das v. State of Bihar (2000) 5 SCC 488 Lahoti J. (as the learned Chief Justice then was), speaking for a Division Bench, had held that “the relevant date for finding out the age of [a] juvenile is the date when he is produced before the Board”. A further question arose because the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000 had come into force on 1 April 2001 and had repealed the 1986 Act, raising the age of a male juvenile from 16 to 18. The matter was accordingly referred to a Constitution Bench.
Issues
VERBATIM DU “The questions which arise for consideration in this reference are:
(i) What would be [the] reckoning date in determining the age of [the] offender, viz., [the] date when produced in a Court, as has been held by this Court in Arnit Das v. State of Bihar, or the date on which the offence was committed as has been held in Umesh Chandra v. State of Rajasthan[?]
(ii) Whether the 2000 Act will be applicable in cases which were pending before the enforcement thereof[?]”
Arguments for the respondent State (the learned Additional Solicitor General)
VERBATIM DU “In support of the view taken in Arnit Das, the learned Additional Solicitor General appearing for the Respondent submitted that the Act aims at protection of a juvenile in the sense that he is to be kept in the protective custody and dealt with separately by not sending him to prison or police lock-up, which is possible to be directed only when a juvenile is arrested or produced in court and not prior thereto. Similarly, on conviction, he cannot be sentenced and may be directed to be housed in a protective home and, thus, the relevant date would be the one on which the delinquent juvenile is produced before the Board.”
It was further urged that Umesh Chandra had “wrongly applied the test of imputing mens rea in holding that [the] Children Act was enacted to protect young children from the consequences of their criminal acts on the footing that their mind at that age cannot be said to be mature as in the case of [an] adult”; and that the model rules framed by the Central Government governed the determination of age.
Arguments for the appellant
That the Act is a beneficent and remedial legislation to be construed liberally in favour of the juvenile; that the definition of “delinquent juvenile” is referable to an offence said to have been committed by him, and it is on the date of the offence that he came into conflict with the law; that an interpretation fixing the age at the date of production would allow the prosecution, by delay or machination, to deprive a child of the protection of the Act; and that such a construction would violate Art. 20(1).
Decision — on Question (i): the reckoning date
The Constitution Bench held that the relevant date for determining the age of a juvenile is the DATE OF COMMISSION OF THE OFFENCE, and not the date on which he is produced before the competent authority or court. Arnit Das was held not to lay down the correct law; Umesh Chandra was approved.
VERBATIM — the reasoning of Sinha, J. DU “This argument cannot be accepted for more than one reason. The said Act is not only a BENEFICENT legislation, but also a REMEDIAL one. The Act aims at grant of care, protection and rehabilitation of a juvenile vis-à-vis the adult criminals. Having regard to Rule 4 of [the] United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Administration of Juvenile Justice, it must also be borne in mind that the moral and psychological components of criminal responsibility was also one of the factors in defining a juvenile. The first objective, therefore, is the promotion of the well-being of the juvenile and the second objective [is to] bring about the principle of proportionality… In essence, Rule 5 calls for no less and no more than a fair reaction in any given case of juvenile delinquency and crime.

The meaning of the expression ‘Juvenile’ used in a statute by reason of its very nature has to be assigned with reference to a DEFINITE DATE. The term ‘Juvenile’ must be given a definite connotation. A PERSON CANNOT BE A JUVENILE FOR ONE PURPOSE AND AN ADULT FOR [ANOTHER] PURPOSE. It was, having regard to the constitutional and statutory scheme, not necessary for the Parliament to specifically state that the age of [the] juvenile must be determined as on the date of commission of the offence. The same is IN-BUILT IN THE STATUTORY SCHEME. …The modern approach is to consider whether a child can live up to the moral and psychological components of criminal responsibility, that is, whether a child, by virtue of his or her individual discernment and understanding, can be held responsible for essentially anti-social behaviour.

[On the machination argument:] “The court in an appropriate case is not powerless to pass an order as is contemplated under the statute if the situation so demands, but only because a person is produced before the Court after he attains majority either on his own volition OR BY REASON OF MACHINATIONS ADOPTED BY THE INVESTIGATING AGENCY, the same would not be determinative of the fact that the said person is to be differently dealt with.”

[Disagreeing with Arnit Das:] “With great respect, we cannot agree to the said statement of law. It is incorrect to say that the preamble speaks of the things of post-delinquency only… Once the law relates to delinquent juveniles or juveniles in conflict with law, the same would mean BOTH PRE AND POST-DELINQUENCY. …In terms of Section 3 of the 1986 Act as well as [the] 2000 Act, when an enquiry has been initiated, even if the juvenile has ceased to be so as he has crossed the age of 16 [or] 18, as the case may be, the same must be continued in respect of such person as if he had continued to be a juvenile. Section 3 of the 1986 Act therefore cannot be given effect to if it is held that the same only applied to post delinquency of the juvenile. The field covered by the Act includes a situation LEADING TO juvenile delinquency vis-à-vis commission of an offence… IT MUST, THEREFORE, BE HELD THAT THE RELEVANT DATE FOR DETERMINING THE AGE OF THE JUVENILE WOULD BE ONE ON WHICH THE OFFENCE HAS BEEN COMMITTED AND NOT WHEN HE IS PRODUCED IN COURT.”

[On Art. 20(1), approving Umesh Chandra:] “If we interpret S. 28 to mean that it prohibits a joint trial of a child and an adult only when the child is a ‘child’ at the time of trial, that interpretation would go against the provisions of Art. 20(1) of the Constitution, which prescribes that no person shall be convicted of any offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act charged as an offence nor be subjected to a penalty greater than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at the time of the commission of the offence. We, with respect, agree with the said observation. The statute, it is well known, must be construed in such a manner so as to make it effective and operative on the principle of Ut res magis valeat quam pereat. The courts lean strongly against any construction which tends to reduce a statute to a futility.”
Decision — on Question (ii): retrospective application of the 2000 Act
The Court held that the 2000 Act, being prospective in terms of s.1(3) (brought into force on 1 April 2001) nevertheless applies to pending proceedings to the extent indicated, because it is a remedial statute and because s.20 creates a legal fiction for cases pending on the date of commencement.
VERBATIM DU “A person above 16 years in terms of the 1986 Act was not a juvenile. In that view of the matter the question whether a person above 16 years becomes ‘juvenile’ within the purview of the Act of 2000 must be answered having regard to the object and purport thereof. Section 20 of the Act of 2000 takes care of such a situation, stating that despite the same the trial shall continue in that court as if that Act has not been passed and, in the event he is found to be guilty of commission of an offence, a finding to that effect shall be recorded in the judgment of conviction, if any, BUT INSTEAD OF PASSING ANY SENTENCE in relation to the juvenile, he would be FORWARDED TO THE BOARD, which shall pass orders in accordance with the provisions of the Act as if he has been satisfied on inquiry that a juvenile has committed the offence. A LEGAL FICTION HAS, THUS, BEEN CREATED in the said provision.…

[On remedial statutes:] “The aforementioned provision of the 2000 Act is furthermore a remedial statute… They are, thus, required to be given liberal construction. A remedial statute applied in a pending proceeding would not mean that thereby a retrospective effect and retroactive operation is being given thereto. …in a case of this nature where an additional protection had been granted pursuant to or in furtherance of the international treaties and keeping in view [the] experience which had been gathered by the Parliament after coming into force of the 1986 Act, we think that it should be read in such a fashion so that the extended benefit can be granted even to the juvenile under the 2000 Act. Furthermore, sub-section (2) of Section 69 provides that all proceedings shall be deemed to have been held under the new Act.”

[Applying Rattan Lal and Dayal Singh:] “[An] ex post facto law which ONLY MODIFIES THE RIGOUR of the criminal law, though retrospective in operation, will be valid… ‘…provisions [which] are more beneficial to the accused and amount to mollification of the rigour of the law… ought to be applied to the cases pending before the Court or even to cases pending investigation on the date on which the amending Act came into force. Such application would not be hit by Article 20(1) of the Constitution.’”
Decision — on the model rules and on evidence of age
The Court held that the model rules framed by the Central Government had no legal force, because under the 2000 Act as it then stood rule-making power was entrusted only to the States and the Central Government’s power “to remove difficulties” under s.70 is “not akin to a legislative power”. It followed that age could not be determined by reference to Rule 62 of those model rules:
VERBATIM DU “The age of the delinquent juvenile, therefore, cannot be determined in terms of the model rules 62. Any law mandating the court to take into consideration certain documents over others in determining an issue must be provided for ONLY BY LAW. Only a validly made law can take away the power of the court to appreciate evidence for the purpose of determination of such a question in the light of Section 35 of the Indian Evidence Act.”
The Court then reproduced Birad Mal Singhvi v. Anand Purohit AIR 1988 SC 1796 on the three conditions for admissibility under s.35 of the Evidence Act and on the limited weight of a school-register entry in the absence of the material on which the age was recorded (see §27).
The four conclusions — VERBATIM
VERBATIM — the concluding paragraph of Sinha, J. DU “The upshot of the aforementioned discussions is:
(i) In terms of the 1986 Act, the age of the offender must be reckoned from the date when the alleged offence was committed;
(ii) The 2000 Act will have a limited application in the cases pending under the 1986 Act;
(iii) The model rules framed by the Central Government having no legal force cannot be given effect to;
(iv) The court, thus, would be entitled to apply the ordinary rules of evidence for the purpose of determining the age of the juvenile, taking into consideration the provisions of Section 35 of the Indian Evidence Act.”
Principles evolved
(1) THE RECKONING DATE IS THE DATE OF THE OFFENCE. This is the single most important proposition in Indian juvenile justice, and it is now codified in s.2(13) of the 2015 Act — “has not completed eighteen years of age on the date of commission of such offence”. (2) “A person cannot be a juvenile for one purpose and an adult for another purpose.” (3) The JJ Act is a beneficent and remedial statute to be construed liberally in favour of the child; a remedial statute applied to pending proceedings is not thereby given retrospective effect. (4) An ex post facto law that mollifies the rigour of the criminal law is not hit by Art. 20(1) and applies to pending cases. (5) International instruments — the Beijing Rules, the UNCRC and the Universal Declaration — inform the construction of the Act and of the Constitution, even though they are not part of municipal law. (6) Age is to be determined on the ordinary rules of evidence, including s.35 of the Evidence Act (now s.34 BSA); the executive cannot, by rules made without power, dictate the weight of evidence.
Afterlife
Pratap Singh was applied and extended in Hari Ram v. State of Rajasthan (2009) 13 SCC 211, which held, on a conjoint reading of ss.2(k), 2(l), 7A, 20 and 49 with Rules 12 and 98 of the 2007 Rules, that “all persons who were below the age of 18 years on the date of commission of the offence even prior to 1-4-2001 would be treated as juveniles, even if the claim of juvenility is raised after they have attained the age of 18 years” — the proposition applied in Daya Nand (§32). The DU 2022 solved paper summarises Pratap Singh thus: “The Court held that the whole object of the JJ Act, 2000 is to provide for care and protection of juveniles. It is a beneficial legislation and must be construed so as to give benefit to the juvenile. The definition of delinquent juvenile under the Act must be taken to refer to the age of the juvenile on the date of commission of the offence and not when he was produced before the Court.”

32. CASE 5 — Sudesh Kumar v. State of Uttarakhand (2008) DU verbatim

Sudesh Kumar v. State of Uttarakhand(2008) 3 SCC 111 — P.P. Naolekar, J., with a concurring judgment of C.K. Thakker, J.Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 136–142
Facts
VERBATIM DU “The appellant was convicted by the judgment and order dated 26-7-1985 passed by the Additional District and Sessions Judge, Dehradun, along with another accused person, under Section 392 read with Section 34 of the Penal Code, 1860 and sentenced to undergo five years’ rigorous imprisonment and further to pay a fine of Rs 5000… The appellant was further convicted under Section 25 of the Arms Act and sentenced to undergo rigorous imprisonment for one year. In appeal… the High Court [confirmed the conviction and sentence] by its order dated 9-7-2007.

The case of the prosecution in brief is that one Jagdish Prasad was [a] wholesale beedi merchant… in the name and style of M/s Madrasee Basant Beedi in Vikasnagar, District Dehradun. Jagdish Prasad used to go to collect his dues from the retailers on every 15th day. On 7-3-1981 he went to Purola, Badkot for realisation of his dues… While returning… they had stopped at the curve of Katta Pather and alighted from the car. Four miscreants came on [a] scooter… Two miscreants were armed with revolvers and the remaining two had khukhris… All of them surrounded Jagdish Prasad and ordered him to hand over [the] money bag… They also threatened him to shoot and kill him if he made any protest. Jagdish Prasad quietly handed over the money bag containing about Rs 25,000. He also gave his wrist watch and a golden ring. Another occupant… was compelled to give cash of Rs 230 and the driver gave cash of Rs 600… One person sitting in the car was forced to give his three wrist watches. The miscreants snatched away the keys of the car… One of the miscreants ran away on the scooter along with the money bag, while the remaining three boarded the car and fled away.”
Issue
The appellant took only one point: that he was below 21 years of age and therefore entitled to the benefit of s.6 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958. The question of construction was: is the crucial date for the applicability of s.6 the DATE OF THE OFFENCE or the DATE ON WHICH THE TRIAL COURT PASSES THE SENTENCE?
Arguments for the appellant (Shri K.T.S. Tulsi, Senior Counsel)
VERBATIM DU “…the accused at the time of the commission of the crime was below 21 years of age, which is apparent from the statement recorded under Section 313 CrPC of the accused wherein [the] age of the accused was given by the accused as 20 years, and from the transfer certificate, filed along with the special leave petition, issued by the Principal, Sanatan Dharma Junior High School, Dehradun, which shows that the appellant was born on 28-6-1962. It is, therefore, submitted that it is clearly established that the appellant-accused on the date of the offence, i.e. 7-3-1981, was below 21 years of age and as such was entitled to consideration and benefit under Section 6 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958.”
It was further argued, relying on Masarullah v. State of Tamil Nadu AIR 1983 SC 654, that “in case of an offender under the age of 21 years on the date of commission of the offence, the court is expected ordinarily to give benefit of the provisions of the Act, and there is an embargo on the power of the court to award sentence unless the court considers otherwise, ‘having regard to the circumstances of the case including nature of the offence and the character of the offender’, and reasons for awarding sentence have to be recorded”; and, relying on Pratap Singh, that since a Constitution Bench had held that the reckoning date under the pari materia provisions of the Juvenile Justice Act is the date of the offence, s.6 of the Probation Act should be construed in the same light.
Arguments for the State (Shri Jatinder Kumar Bhatia)
VERBATIM DU “…the accused, having not raised the question of his age either before the trial court or before the High Court, and in the absence of any reliable material, could not ask for consideration of his case and benefit under Section 6 of the Act. It is further submitted that it is the date on which the SENTENCE IS PASSED which shall be the relevant date for applicability of Section 6 of the Act.”
The provision
VERBATIM — s.6, Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 DU “6. Restrictions on imprisonment of offenders under twenty-one years of age. — (1) When any person under twenty-one years of age is found guilty of having committed an offence punishable with imprisonment (but not with imprisonment for life), the court by which the person is found guilty SHALL NOT SENTENCE HIM TO IMPRISONMENT unless it is satisfied that, having regard to the circumstances of the case including the nature of the offence and the character of the offender, it would not be desirable to deal with him under Section 3 or Section 4, and if the court passes any sentence of imprisonment on the offender, it SHALL RECORD ITS REASONS for doing so.
(2) For the purpose of satisfying itself whether it would not be desirable to deal under Section 3 or Section 4 with an offender referred to in sub-section (1), the court shall call for a REPORT FROM THE PROBATION OFFICER and consider the report, if any, and other information available to it relating to the character and physical and mental conditions of the offender.”
Decision
The appeal was dismissed. Naolekar J. held that the crucial date under s.6 is the date on which the TRIAL COURT DEALS WITH THE OFFENDER — that is, the date of the order of conviction and sentence — and not the date of the offence.
VERBATIM — the reasoning DU [On the object of s.6, following Daulat Ram v. State of Haryana (1995) 1 SCC 349 and Satyabhan Kishore v. State of Bihar AIR 1972 SC 1554:] “the object of Section 6 of the Act, broadly speaking, is to see that YOUNG OFFENDERS ARE NOT SENT TO JAIL for the commission of less serious offences mentioned therein because of the GRAVE RISK TO THEIR ATTITUDE TO LIFE to which they are likely to be exposed as a result of their CLOSE ASSOCIATION WITH THE HARDENED AND HABITUAL CRIMINALS who may happen to be the inmates of the jail… Section 6 lays down an INJUNCTION as distinguished from [the] discretion under Sections 3 and 4 not to impose a sentence of imprisonment on an offender, unless reasons are recorded.”

[Following the 4-Judge Bench in Ramji Missar v. State of Bihar AIR 1963 SC 1088, which Masarullah had overlooked:] “The question of the age of the person is relevant NOT for the purpose of determining his guilt but only for the purpose of the PUNISHMENT which he should suffer for the offence of which he has been found, on the evidence, guilty. THE OBJECT OF THE ACT IS TO PREVENT THE TURNING OF YOUTHFUL OFFENDERS INTO CRIMINALS BY THEIR ASSOCIATION WITH HARDENED CRIMINALS OF MATURE AGE WITHIN THE WALLS OF A PRISON. The method adopted is to attempt their possible reformation instead of inflicting on them the normal punishment for their crime. If this were borne in mind it would be clear that THE AGE REFERRED TO BY THE OPENING WORDS OF SECTION 6(1) SHOULD BE THAT WHEN THE COURT IS DEALING WITH THE OFFENDER, that being the point of time when the court has to choose between the two alternatives…” And on the appellate stage: “on the terms of the section, on grounds of logic as well as on the theory that the order passed by an appellate court is the correct order which the trial court should have passed, THE CRUCIAL DATE MUST BE THAT UPON WHICH THE TRIAL COURT HAD TO DEAL WITH THE OFFENDER.”

[On the attempt to import Pratap Singh:] “It can be noticed from Ramji Missar case and Pratap Singh case that the object and purpose of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 for applying the relevant provisions to the accused are DIFFERENT and CANNOT BE SAID [TO BE] IN PARI MATERIA with the Juvenile Justice Act, 1986 and the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000. The court would not construe a section of a statute with reference to that of another statute unless the latter is IN PARI MATERIA with the former. Therefore, a decision made on a provision of a different statute will be of NO RELEVANCE unless [the] underlying objects of the two statutes are in pari materia. The decision interpreting various provisions of one statute will not have the binding force while interpreting the provisions of another statute.”

[The conclusion:] “Section 6 of the Act has been construed by a 4-Judge Bench of this Court in Ramji Missar case and that will have the binding force… Section 6 of the Act would apply to the accused who is UNDER 21 YEARS OF AGE ON THE DATE OF IMPOSITION OF PUNISHMENT BY THE TRIAL COURT AND NOT ON THE DATE OF COMMISSION OF THE OFFENCE. If on the date of the order of conviction and sentence by the trial court the accused is below 21 years of age, the provisions of Section 6 of the Act apply in full force.
That being the case, even if the date of birth of the accused is held to be 28-6-1962 as alleged by him…, on the date of delivery of judgment of conviction and sentence on 26-7-1985 by the Additional District and Sessions Judge he was MORE THAN 21 YEARS OF AGE and thus was not entitled to the benefit under Section 6.”

[On the belated plea, following Yaduraj Singh v. State of U.P. (1976) 4 SCC 310:] “This contention was neither taken in the Sessions Court nor in the High Court. True, that this Court has taken the view that in appropriate cases such a contention may be entertained by this Court for the first time. But the difficulty… is that there is NO CREDIBLE EVIDENCE on the record showing that [the] Appellants… were less than 21 years of age when the offence was committed… The age given by the two accused in their statements had NO SPECIAL SIGNIFICANCE in the absence of a proper plea under the Probation of Offenders Act.”
The concurring judgment of C.K. Thakker, J.
VERBATIM DU “I, however, decide the appeal on the second ground — that on the facts and in the circumstances of the case, the appellant has FAILED TO MAKE OUT a ground that he was less than 21 years of age at the time of commission of [the] offence… It is, no doubt, true that the provision is beneficial and benevolent in nature and no ‘technical’ objection should be raised that such plea was not taken before the courts below [Gopinath Ghosh v. State of W.B., AIR 1984 SC 237]. But in my opinion, THERE MUST BE CREDIBLE AND TRUSTWORTHY EVIDENCE IN SUPPORT OF SUCH PLEA. In the present case, a certificate in the form of ‘scholar record and transfer certificate’ is annexed wherein the date of birth of the appellant was shown as 28-6-1962. The certificate was not on record either before the trial court or before the High Court. From the ‘true copy’, it is clear that it is purported to have been issued by the Principal only on 10-2-2007. Thus, it cannot be said that there is ‘credible evidence’ or ‘trustworthy material’ that the appellant was less than 21 years of age at the time of commission of [the] offence… Since the appeal can be decided on this ground, I REFRAIN FROM EXPRESSING ANY OPINION on the question dealt with and decided by my learned Brother on interpretation of Section 6 of the Act.”

[Thakker J. also relied on Sushil Kumar Mehrotra v. State of U.P. (1984) 3 SCC 123, where a plea that the accused was 15½ at the time of the occurrence, raised for the first time in the Supreme Court, was held to be “a complete afterthought”.]
Principles evolved
(1) For s.6 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958, the crucial date is the date on which the TRIAL COURT DEALS WITH the offender — that is, the date of conviction and sentence — and not the date of the offence. (Contrast the JJ Act, where the date of the offence governs: Pratap Singh, §31.) (2) The two statutes are NOT in pari materia; a construction of one cannot be transplanted to the other. This is an important general principle of statutory interpretation. (3) Section 6 creates an injunction, not a discretion: the court shall not sentence a person under 21 to imprisonment unless it records reasons, and it must call for a report from the Probation Officer under s.6(2). (4) The rationale of the protection is to prevent “the turning of youthful offenders into criminals by their association with hardened criminals of mature age within the walls of a prison” — differential association (Topic 2) stated as a rule of sentencing law. (5) A plea of age may be entertained for the first time in the Supreme Court and no hyper-technical objection should be taken (Gopinath Ghosh), but there must be credible and trustworthy evidence; a self-serving statement under s.313 CrPC and a certificate obtained 22 years after the trial will not suffice.
The DU solved paper’s own summary
VERBATIM — DU solved paper, 2022, Answer 2 PYQ “Sudesh Kumar v. State of Uttarakhand (2008) — The main point of consideration for the Court in this case was that the juvenile or young offenders must not be sent to jail for commission of less serious offences, which will otherwise expose them to hardened and habitual criminals who are their jail inmates. If this principle is not followed, it will destroy the chances of reformation of the juvenile offender in question.”
Examination warning. The DU summary captures the rationale but not the holding. The holding went against the appellant on both grounds. In an answer, state the rationale, then state the holding, and then draw the contrast with Pratap Singh — that contrast is the whole point of the case appearing in this syllabus alongside the JJ Act cases.

33. CASE 6 — Daya Nand v. State of Haryana (2011)

Daya Nand v. State of HaryanaAIR 2011 SC 593; (2011) 2 SCC 224 — Aftab Alam and R.M. Lodha, JJ.; judgment of 7 January 2011juvenility · retrospective benefit of the 2000 Act
Facts
The appellant was convicted under s.376 read with s.511 IPC (attempt to commit rape) and sentenced to five years’ rigorous imprisonment and a fine of ₹2,000, with a further two months in default. The occurrence was of 2 February 1998. The appellant had raised a claim of juvenility. The Principal Magistrate, Juvenile Justice Court had found in his favour; but on the State’s appeal the Sessions Judge, Narnaul reversed that finding, observing that the appellant’s date of birth as recorded in the Births and Deaths Register maintained by the Registrar was 14 August 1981, so that on the date of occurrence he was over 16 but under 18. Under the Juvenile Justice Act, 1986, a male who had completed 16 was not a “juvenile”. Consequently the appellant was tried not by the Juvenile Court but by the Additional Sessions Judge, Narnaul, and convicted.
Issue
Whether a person who was below 18 but above 16 on the date of the offence, at a time when the 1986 Act (which fixed the age of a male juvenile at 16) was in force and before the 2000 Act came into force on 1 April 2001, is entitled to be treated as a juvenile under the 2000 Act — and, if so, what becomes of the conviction and sentence already recorded against him.
Arguments
For the appellant: the 2000 Act raised the age of a male juvenile to 18; s.20 and its Explanation (inserted by the Amendment Act of 2006), s.7A and s.49, read with Rules 12 and 98 of the 2007 Rules, confer the benefit on all persons who were below 18 on the date of the offence, irrespective of when the offence was committed and irrespective of whether the claim is raised after the person has ceased to be a juvenile; the 2000 Act being a beneficent and remedial statute, that construction must be preferred (Pratap Singh; Hari Ram). For the State: on the date of the offence the governing statute was the 1986 Act, under which the appellant, being over 16, was not a juvenile; the trial by the Sessions Court was therefore lawful, and the 2000 Act cannot be given retrospective effect so as to unsettle a completed trial.
Decision
The Court applied Hari Ram v. State of Rajasthan (2009) 13 SCC 211 and held in favour of the appellant. The crystallised position was stated thus:
“The law as now crystallised on a conjoint reading of Sections 2(k), 2(l), 7-A, 20 and 49 read with Rules 12 and 98, places beyond all doubt that all persons who were below the age of 18 years on the date of commission of the offence EVEN PRIOR TO 1-4-2001 would be treated as juveniles, even if the claim of juvenility is raised after they have attained the age of 18 years… Accordingly, a juvenile who had not completed eighteen years on the date of commission of the offence was also entitled to the benefits of the Juvenile Justice Act, 2000, as if the provisions of Section 2(k) had always been in existence even during the operation of the 1986 Act.”Hari Ram, applied in Daya Nand
The Court accordingly held that the appellant was a juvenile on the date of the occurrence, and directed that he be produced before the Juvenile Justice Board, Narnaul, for passing appropriate orders in accordance with the provisions of the Juvenile Justice Act. The conviction as such was not disturbed, but — in accordance with the scheme of s.20 and s.7A(2) — the sentence could not stand, and the disposition became a matter for the Board.
Principles evolved
(1) The benefit of the raised age under the 2000 Act operates retrospectively: every person who was below 18 on the date of the offence is a juvenile, even if the offence was committed before 1 April 2001 and even if the claim is raised after he has attained 18. The legal fiction is that s.2(k) had always been in existence. (2) A finding of juvenility does not result in acquittal. The finding of guilt stands recorded; what is displaced is the sentence, and the case is sent to the Board for orders under the Act (s.20 of the 2000 Act; now s.9(3) of the 2015 Act — “the sentence, if any, passed by the court shall be deemed to have no effect”). (3) A Births and Deaths Register entry is a valid basis for determining age — it is the second document in the statutory hierarchy (now s.94(2)(ii)), and note that here it operated against the accused, which illustrates the neutrality of the hierarchy. (4) The appellate authority under the JJ Act may reverse a finding of juvenility, but the ultimate question is one of law and may be re-examined by the Supreme Court.
Why the case is in the syllabus
It is the bridge between Pratap Singh (which fixed the date) and the modern age-determination cases (which fix the evidence). It also demonstrates the practical operation of s.9(3): a man convicted and sentenced to five years’ rigorous imprisonment walked out of the criminal justice system into the juvenile justice system on the strength of a single entry in a municipal register.

34. CASE 7 — Union of India v. Ex-GNR Ajeet Singh (2013)

Union of India and Others v. Ex-GNR Ajeet Singh(2013) 4 SCC 186 — B.S. Chauhan and F.M. Ibrahim Kalifulla, JJ.joint trial · Army Act · s.18 of the 2000 Act
Facts
The respondent, a Gunner in the Indian Army, was tried by a General Court Martial (GCM) under the Army Act, 1950 on several charges. Some of the acts charged had been committed when he was still a juvenile — that is, below the age of 18 — and others after he had attained majority. The GCM tried all the charges jointly, convicted him and he was dismissed from service. He challenged the proceedings on the ground that a joint trial of offences allegedly committed as a juvenile together with offences allegedly committed as an adult was prohibited.
Issue
Whether a joint General Court Martial in respect of (i) offences allegedly committed while the accused was a juvenile and (ii) offences allegedly committed after he attained majority, is permissible; and if not, what is the effect on the proceedings as a whole.
Arguments
For the respondent: s.18 of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000 — “notwithstanding anything contained in s.223 of the CrPC or in any other law for the time being in force, no juvenile shall be charged with or tried for any offence together with a person who is not a juvenile” — is in absolute terms and applies notwithstanding the Army Act. A person cannot be tried jointly for acts done as a juvenile and acts done as an adult, because the forum, the procedure and the permissible dispositions are entirely different; the joint trial therefore vitiated the GCM proceedings in their entirety. Reliance was placed on Hari Ram, Pratap Singh, Sheela Barse and Gaurav Jain, and on the Beijing Rules and the UNCRC. For the Union: the Army Act is a complete code governing the trial of persons subject to military law; the JJ Act was not intended to disrupt military discipline; and in any event the defect, if any, was curable and did not vitiate the entire proceeding.
Decision
The Supreme Court held for the respondent. The joint trial of offences committed as a juvenile together with offences committed on attaining majority VITIATED THE GCM PROCEEDINGS IN THEIR ENTIRETY. The Court reiterated the settled principles: the JJ Act is a beneficial and remedial statute enacted in discharge of India’s obligations under the UNCRC and the Beijing Rules; its provisions override inconsistent provisions of other laws; and a juvenile cannot be dealt with under the ordinary — or, here, the military — criminal process. It quoted the reasoning of Sheela Barse that incarceration “would have the effect of dwarfing the development of the child, exposing him to baneful influences, coarsening his conscience and alienating him from the society”, and set out the framework of the Beijing Rules and the UNCRC.
Principles evolved
(1) The prohibition on joint proceedings — s.25 of the 1986 Act, s.18 of the 2000 Act, now s.23 of the 2015 Act — is absolute, and operates notwithstanding s.223 CrPC (now s.246 BNSS) “or any other law for the time being in force”, including a special statute such as the Army Act, 1950. (2) Breach of that prohibition is not a mere irregularity: it vitiates the whole trial, including the charges relating to the period after majority. (3) The JJ Act applies to every forum — civil courts, criminal courts, special courts and courts martial alike. (4) The protection attaches by reference to the age at the time of each act charged, so a single person may be a juvenile for some charges and an adult for others — and precisely for that reason the charges must be separated.
The DU solved paper’s own summary
VERBATIM — DU solved paper, 2022, Answer 2 PYQ “Union of India v. Ex-GNR Ajeet Singh (2013) — The respondent was a juvenile at the time of some of the charged offences when he was tried under the Army Act. In view of the provisions of the JJ Act, the joint trial of those offences he had allegedly committed as a juvenile and others that he had allegedly committed on attaining majority had VITIATED THE GCM PROCEEDINGS IN [THEIR] ENTIRETY, as contended by the respondent.”

35. CASE 8 — Sampurna Behura v. Union of India (2018)

Sampurna Behura v. Union of India(2018) 4 SCC 433 — Madan B. Lokur and Deepak Gupta, JJ.; judgment of 9 February 2018implementation · structural directions
Facts
A public interest petition under Art. 32, pending since 2005, concerning the non-implementation of the Juvenile Justice Act. Successive affidavits and reports over more than a decade disclosed a systemic collapse of the statutory machinery across the country: Juvenile Justice Boards and Child Welfare Committees not constituted or lying vacant; members appointed without the prescribed qualifications and without induction training; Special Juvenile Police Units and Child Welfare Police Officers not designated; District Child Protection Units not established; child care institutions unregistered in very large numbers despite the mandate of s.41; inspection committees under s.54 not constituted or not visiting; the NCPCR and the State Commissions functioning with vacancies and without adequate resources; grossly inadequate budgetary allocation under the Integrated Child Protection Scheme; and no reliable data on children in institutions. By the time of the judgment the 2015 Act had replaced the 2000 Act and the failures had simply migrated to the new statute.
Issues
(1) What directions should issue to compel the Union and the States to implement the JJ Act, 2015 and the Model Rules, 2016? (2) How is compliance to be monitored and enforced, given a decade of non-compliance with earlier orders?
Arguments
For the petitioner and the amicus: the Act confers rights on children which are enforceable under Arts. 21 and 39(f); non-constitution of the statutory bodies is not an administrative failing but a violation of fundamental rights; the Court should fix timelines, require the Union to convene the States, and require the NCPCR and SCPCRs to discharge their s.109 monitoring function. For the Union and the States: that steps were being taken; that “child welfare” and “public order” are largely State subjects and resources are constrained; and that the Court should not enter the field of policy or budgeting.
Decision
The Court rejected the plea of resource constraint and issued a comprehensive set of directions, of which the following are the most important and should be listed in an answer:
  1. Constitution and vacancies. Every State and Union Territory to constitute Juvenile Justice Boards and Child Welfare Committees in every district and to fill all vacancies within a fixed time. Appointments to be made on the basis of the prescribed qualifications, with a transparent selection process.
  2. Training. Induction training and sensitisation is mandatory under s.4(5) and s.27; the National and State Judicial Academies and the National Institute of Social Defence to prepare and run training modules for Principal Magistrates, Board and Committee members, Probation Officers, Child Welfare Police Officers and staff of child care institutions. The Court emphasised that “awareness about the rights of the child and correlated duties remain low among the functionaries of the juvenile justice system”.
  3. Registration of child care institutions. All child care institutions to be registered under s.41 without exception, and the States to complete the exercise in a time-bound manner; unregistered institutions to be dealt with under s.42.
  4. Inspection and social audit. State and District Inspection Committees under s.54 to be constituted and to carry out their mandatory quarterly inspections; and social audits of child care institutions to be conducted.
  5. Special Juvenile Police Units and Child Welfare Police Officers to be designated in every police station and district under s.107; District Child Protection Units under s.106 to be established and staffed.
  6. Monitoring by the Commissions. The NCPCR and every SCPCR to perform the monitoring function under s.109; vacancies in the Commissions to be filled; and the Commissions to be provided adequate staff, infrastructure and funds.
  7. Funding. The Union and the States to ensure adequate and timely budgetary allocation for child protection; the Court observed that the allocation for child protection was a minuscule fraction of the budget and that “the best investment a State can get for expenditure on children is the building up of a powerful human resource” (echoing Sheela Barse).
  8. Data and case management. Reliable data to be collected and maintained on children in institutions and on cases pending before Boards and Committees; the Boards to hold regular sittings and to dispose of matters within the statutory time-limits; and child-friendly courts and vulnerable-witness deposition rooms to be set up.
  9. Institutional monitoring of compliance. The Union Ministry of Women and Child Development to convene meetings with the States; and the High Courts to consider registering suo motu proceedings and constituting Juvenile Justice Committees to monitor implementation within each State.
Principles evolved
(1) The rights conferred by the JJ Act are an aspect of the fundamental rights of the child under Arts. 21 and 39(f); failure to constitute the statutory machinery is a violation of those rights and is justiciable under Art. 32. (2) Paper compliance is not compliance. A Board that exists on a notification but has no qualified, trained members and does not sit is no Board at all. (3) Resource constraint is not an answer where the Legislature has itself created the obligation and the entitlement is founded on a fundamental right. (4) The NCPCR and SCPCRs are the designated statutory monitors under s.109 and must be resourced and required to function. (5) The technique of continuing mandamus with time-bound, auditable directions is appropriate for systemic failures of implementation.
Significance
This is the case to cite whenever the question asks whether the law is adequate. The answer is: the law is adequate; its administration is not. Sampurna Behura is also the empirical foundation of Ved Kumari’s sixth criticism (§25.2) — that the 2000 Act failed because of implementation, not leniency, and that toughening the substantive law in 2015 addressed the wrong problem. Read it with the NCPCR social audit of child care institutions (2020), the JJ (Amendment) Act, 2021 (which responded by strengthening the District Magistrate’s supervisory role), and In Re: Exploitation of Children in Orphanages in the State of Tamil Nadu v. Union of India (2017) 7 SCC 578, a companion decision of Lokur J. on the same theme.

36. CASE 9 — Rishipal Singh Solanki v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2021)

Rishipal Singh Solanki v. State of Uttar Pradesh & Ors.(2022) 8 SCC 602; Criminal Appeal No. 1240 of 2021, decided 18 November 2021 — B.V. Nagarathna, J.age determination · the eleven principles
Facts
The appellant was the complainant/informant in a criminal case. The second respondent, an accused, claimed juvenility before the Juvenile Justice Board and produced his matriculation certificate. The Board accepted the certificate and declared him a juvenile. The complainant challenged that finding, contending that the oral testimony of witnesses and other documents contradicted the certificate, and that the Board ought to have ordered a medical (ossification) test. The appellate court and the High Court upheld the Board; the complainant appealed to the Supreme Court.
Issues
(1) Where a matriculation or school certificate is available under s.94(2)(i) of the JJ Act, 2015, may the Board nevertheless order an ossification test? (2) Is the presumption arising from such a certificate conclusive, or may it be rebutted by contra evidence? (3) What is the difference between an inquiry into a claim of juvenility and a determination of age, and what standard of proof applies to each? (4) What is the effect of s.94(3)?
Arguments for the appellant-complainant
That the Board had misunderstood the legislative intent of s.94 in treating a matriculation certificate as a conclusive document notwithstanding material discrepancies in the oral testimony of witnesses and in other documents produced; that in earlier decisions of the Supreme Court a claim of juvenility had been rejected despite a matriculation certificate where there were discrepancies in the evidence; and that therefore “the age shown in the matriculation certificate cannot be accepted on its face value if there is other evidence which contradicts the same”. The complainant also complained that she had had no real opportunity to lead evidence.
Arguments for the respondent-accused
That s.94(2) lays down a strict hierarchy: the ossification test is available only in the absence of the documents in clauses (i) and (ii); that the matriculation certificate was produced and was not shown to be forged; that s.94(3) makes the age recorded by the Board the deemed true age; and that the JJ Act being a beneficent statute, a hyper-technical approach is impermissible and, if two views are possible, the court must lean in favour of juvenility.
Decision
The appeal was dismissed. The Court held that since the certificate of the matriculation Board was available, “it was unnecessary [to order] medical test[ing]”; that there was “no other document showing [the] date of birth of [the] second respondent contrary to what [is] indicated in [the] matriculation certificate”, so that no discrepancy in the date of birth arose; that the complainant had produced no contra evidence to the documents produced by the juvenile; and that the accused was therefore “rightly declared as a juvenile”. Before reaching that conclusion the Court reviewed the entire line of authority — Ashwani Kumar Saxena, Abuzar Hossain, Parag Bhati, Ram Vijay Singh and others — and summarised eleven principles, which are reproduced in full at §19.3 above and which should be learnt as a list.
Key holdings to quote
“Section 94 of the JJ Act, 2015 raises a presumption regarding juvenility of the age of the child brought before the JJ Board or the Committee. But in case the Board or Committee has reasonable grounds for doubt about [whether] the person brought before it is a child or not, it can undertake the process of determination of age by seeking evidence. Thus, in the initial stage a presumption that the child brought before the Committee or the JJ Board is a juvenile has to be drawn by the said authorities. The said presumption has to be drawn on OBSERVATION of the child.”

“The said presumption is however NOT CONCLUSIVE PROOF of the age of juvenility and the same may be REBUTTED BY CONTRA EVIDENCE let in by the opposite side.”

“When the determination of age is on the basis of evidence such as school records, it is necessary that the same would have to be considered as per Section 35 of the Indian Evidence Act, inasmuch as ANY PUBLIC OR OFFICIAL DOCUMENT MAINTAINED IN THE DISCHARGE OF OFFICIAL DUTY WOULD HAVE GREATER CREDIBILITY THAN PRIVATE DOCUMENTS.”

“OSSIFICATION TEST CANNOT BE THE SOLE CRITERION FOR AGE DETERMINATION and a mechanical view regarding the age of a person cannot be adopted solely on the basis of medical opinion by radiological examination. Such evidence is NOT CONCLUSIVE EVIDENCE but only A VERY USEFUL GUIDING FACTOR to be considered in the absence of [the] documents mentioned in Section 94(2) of the JJ Act, 2015.”

[On the distinction between inquiry and determination, following Abuzar Hossain per T.S. Thakur J.:] “Directing an inquiry is not the same thing as declaring the accused to be a juvenile. In the former, the Court simply records a PRIMA FACIE conclusion, while a DECLARATION is made on the basis of evidence. Hence, the approach at the stage of directing an inquiry has to be MORE LIBERAL lest there is [a] miscarriage of justice. The standard of proof required is different for both.”

[The caution against misuse:] “The benefit of the principle of benevolent legislation attached to the JJ Act would… apply only to such cases wherein the accused is held to be a juvenile on the basis of at least PRIMA FACIE evidence regarding his minority… [the] plea of juvenility [which] is more in the nature of A SHIELD TO DODGE OR DUPE THE ARMS OF LAW cannot be allowed to come to his rescue.” Rishipal Singh Solanki v. State of Uttar Pradesh
Principles evolved
(1) The hierarchy in s.94(2) is mandatory. Where a school or matriculation certificate exists, a medical test is unnecessary and impermissible. (2) The documents give rise to a rebuttable presumption of juvenility; the burden of rebutting it by contra evidence lies on the party opposing the claim. (3) School records are to be appreciated under s.35 of the Evidence Act (s.34 BSA): an official record has greater credibility than a private document. (4) An ossification test can never be the sole criterion; it is “a very useful guiding factor” and nothing more. (5) s.94(3): the age recorded by the Board or Committee is deemed to be the true age for the purposes of the Act. (6) The eleven principles at §19.3, which now constitute the standard checklist for every juvenility claim in India.
Note the balance
The decision is not one-sided. It insists on the hierarchy and on the benevolent construction, but it also warns that the plea of juvenility cannot be used as “a shield to dodge or dupe the arms of law”, and it preserves the right of the victim or complainant to lead contra evidence — a point of practical importance, since it is usually the informant who challenges a finding of juvenility. Compare Rajni (§40), where the complainant’s challenge failed because the Board had already accepted the school certificate and had no power to review its own order.

37. CASE 10 — Vinod Katara v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2022)

Vinod Katara v. State of Uttar Pradesh2022 SCC OnLine SC 1204; 2022 INSC 946; judgment of 12 September 2022 — J.B. Pardiwala, J.belated juvenility claim · limits of the ossification test
Facts
The writ applicant had been convicted of murder by the Additional Sessions Judge, Agra, and the conviction had been upheld by the Allahabad High Court; his special leave petition to the Supreme Court had been dismissed. He had been in prison for many years. He then filed a writ petition in the Supreme Court claiming that on the date of the offence he had been a minor — he asserted that he was about 15 years old at the time — and that he should therefore have been dealt with under the juvenile justice law and not by the ordinary criminal court. In support he relied principally on a Family Register maintained by the local authority. He had by then crossed 50 years of age, and an ossification test conducted in 2021 had been inconclusive.
Issues
(1) Can a claim of juvenility be entertained after the final disposal of the case, including dismissal of the SLP, by way of a writ petition? (2) What is the evidentiary value of a Family Register for the purpose of age determination? (3) What is the utility of an ossification test conducted on a person over 50 years of age? (4) Which statute governs — the 2000 Act (with Rule 12 of the 2007 Rules) or the 2015 Act?
Arguments for the applicant
That the proviso to s.7A(1) of the 2000 Act (and s.9(2) of the 2015 Act) expressly permits a claim of juvenility to be raised before any court and at any stage, even after final disposal of the case; that the Family Register is an official record maintained by a public authority and is admissible under s.35 of the Evidence Act; that the JJ Act is a beneficent statute and a hyper-technical approach must be avoided; and that in any event the Court should direct a fresh ossification test in the larger interest of justice.
Arguments for the State (the Additional Advocate General)
That “the Family Register is not admissible in evidence and the entries made therein are not decisive to determine the age”; that the applicant had produced “no document of any educational institution”; that no ossification test had been undertaken and no modern recognised method adopted at the relevant time; and that the plea was raised at an extraordinarily late stage, after the dismissal of the SLP, and was an afterthought.
Decision
The Court held that:
  • The claim was maintainable notwithstanding the final disposal of the case: under the proviso to s.7A(1) “a claim of juvenility may be raised before any court and it shall be recognised at any stage, even after the final disposal of the case, and such claim shall be determined in terms of the provisions contained in this Act and the Rules”; and the Explanation to s.20 makes it clear that in all pending cases, “which would include not only trials but even subsequent proceedings by way of revision or appeal”, juvenility is to be determined under the 2000 Act even if the person ceased to be a juvenile before 1 April 2001.
  • Whenever a claim of juvenility is raised, the court must “make an inquiry… by receiving evidence which would be necessary BUT NOT AN AFFIDAVIT”.
  • The hierarchy of documents under Rule 12(3)(a)(i)–(iii) governs; a Family Register, while not equivalent to a matriculation certificate, has evidentiary value contingent upon its authenticity, and if the documents are “found to be questionable/unreliable, it will be open to the Sessions Court to have the petitioner medically examined by taking an ossification test or any other modern recognised method of age determination.”
  • On the ossification test: “The bone ossification test is not an exact science that can provide us with the exact age of the person… individual characteristics such as the growth rate of bones and skeletal structures can affect the accuracy of this method… the radiological examination leaves a MARGIN OF TWO YEARS ON EITHER SIDE of the age range as prescribed by the test, irrespective of whether the ossification test of multiple joints is conducted. THE COURTS IN INDIA HAVE ACCEPTED THE FACT THAT AFTER THE AGE OF THIRTY YEARS THE OSSIFICATION TEST CANNOT BE RELIED UPON FOR AGE DETERMINATION.” Following Ram Vijay Singh, “in the absence of a reliable and trustworthy medical evidence to find out the age of the Appellant herein, the ossification test conducted in the year 2021 when the Appellant was above 50 years of age cannot be conclusive to declare him as a juvenile on the date of the incident.”
  • Nevertheless, “despite all the odds against the writ applicant, [this] Court would still like to look into the matter in the LARGER INTEREST OF JUSTICE”, and it directed that the applicant be subjected once again to an ossification test or other latest medical age determination test at the Civil Hospital, Allahabad, by a team of three doctors, and remitted the inquiry into juvenility, on that report and on the Family Register, to the concerned Sessions Court.
Principles evolved
(1) A claim of juvenility is maintainable at any stage, even after the dismissal of an SLP, and may be pursued by writ petition; delay is not by itself a ground of rejection. (2) The inquiry must be on evidence, not on affidavit, and is ordinarily to be remitted to the court of first instance, which is equipped to take evidence. (3) A Family Register is not one of the three enumerated documents but is relevant and of evidentiary value, subject to proof of authenticity. (4) The ossification test is unreliable after about thirty years of age and in any event carries a margin of ±2 years; it can never be conclusive in a belated claim by an adult. (5) Where the documentary material is inconclusive, the court may nevertheless order a fresh medical examination in the larger interest of justice — the benevolent construction of the Act applied at the remedial stage.
Why it matters
Vinod Katara is the case of the middle-aged prisoner who says he was a child. Such claims are increasingly common and the Supreme Court has consistently entertained them — see Rahul Kumar Yadav (§38) and the more recent proceedings in Sunder alias Surendra (2026), where a claim was entertained decades after the 1983 offence. Cite Vinod Katara for the evidentiary limits and Rahul Kumar Yadav for the procedural duty to inquire.

38. CASE 11 — Barun Chandra Thakur v. Master Bholu (2022)

Barun Chandra Thakur v. Master Bholu & Anr.2022 SCC OnLine SC 870; 2022 INSC 715; judgment of 13 July 2022 — Dinesh Maheshwari and Vikram Nath, JJ.preliminary assessment · s.15
Facts
A boy of Class II was found unconscious with his throat slit in the toilet of his school in Gurugram on 8 September 2017; he was rushed to hospital and declared dead. The State police initially arrested three persons — a school bus conductor, a driver and another. The investigation was transferred to the CBI, which concluded that the offence had been committed by a student of the same school, referred to in the proceedings as “Bholu”, who was 16 years, 5 months and 5 days old on the date of the incident. The appellant, Barun Chandra Thakur, was the father of the deceased child. Because the alleged offence was heinous and the child was above sixteen, the Juvenile Justice Board was required to conduct a preliminary assessment under s.15. The Board considered the four statutory heads and passed an order under s.18(3) that the child should be tried as an adult; the Children’s Court upheld that order. On a criminal revision, the Punjab and Haryana High Court set aside both orders and remanded the matter to the Board for fresh consideration, directing that the exercise be completed within six weeks and that the certificate of a psychologist of a Government hospital be obtained. Both the father and the child appealed to the Supreme Court.
Issues
(1) What is the meaning and effect of an order of preliminary assessment under s.15, and what are its consequences for the child? (2) Is the expression “may” in the proviso to s.15(1) and in Rule 10A(2) of the Model Rules — permitting the Board to take the assistance of experienced psychologists, psycho-social workers or other experts — directory or mandatory? (3) What is the meaning of “ability to understand the consequences of the offence”? (4) What procedural safeguards apply, given the absence of any statutory guidelines for conducting a preliminary assessment? (5) Is a right of cross-examination on the psychologist’s report available at that stage?
Arguments for the appellant father
That the Board had applied its mind to all four statutory heads and had rightly concluded that the child should be tried as an adult; that the High Court, exercising revisional jurisdiction, could not substitute its own view; that there is no right of second appeal under the Act and the revision was in substance an attempt to obtain one; and that the requirement of expert assistance is expressly discretionary, the statute using “may”.
Arguments for the child
(i) The Board had obtained only the child’s IQ score and no report at all on his ability to comprehend the offence and its consequences; (ii) there was non-compliance with Rule 10(5) of the Model Rules, so the Board was “incapacitated to make an assessment”; (iii) the request for cross-examination of the expert had been wrongly declined; (iv) the assessment carries the gravest consequences for the child’s entire life and therefore attracts the principles of natural justice under s.3(xvi); and (v) treating a child as an adult is an exception to the rule and the whole scheme of the Act requires that the interests of children be protected.
Decision
The Supreme Court dismissed both appeals, thereby leaving the High Court’s order of remand intact, and in doing so laid down the governing principles:
“…we are of the view that where the Board is not comprising of a practising professional with a degree in child psychology or child psychiatry, the expression ‘MAY’ in the proviso to section 15(1) would operate in MANDATORY FORM and the Board would be OBLIGED to take assistance of experienced psychologists or psycho-social workers or other experts.”

“It is, therefore, all the more important that such assessment is made to distinguish such attributes between a child and an adult. Cognitive maturation is highly dependent on hereditary factors. Emotional development is less likely to affect cognitive maturation. However, if emotions are too intense and the child is unable to regulate emotions effectively, then intellectual insight/knowledge may take a back seat.”

“…the task of preliminary assessment under section 15 of the Act, 2015 is a delicate task with requirement of expertise and has its own implications as regards trial of the case. In this view of the matter, it appears expedient that appropriate and specific guidelines in this regard are put in place.” Barun Chandra Thakur v. Master Bholu
Further holdings:
  1. Effect of the order. “The order of preliminary assessment decides whether the child in conflict with law, falling in the age bracket of 16–18 and having committed a heinous offence, is to be tried as an adult by the Children’s Court or by the Board itself, treating him to be a child.” The consequences “are serious in nature and have a lasting effect for the entire life of the child”, and “it is well settled that [where] any order [has] serious civil consequences, reasonable opportunity must be afforded.”
  2. “Consequences” is to be read widely. The term “does not mean only immediate consequences, but also far-reaching ones in the future. The assessment of this attribute cannot be limited to the victim only; it must include what may fall upon the family of the victim, the child, and their family.”
  3. Not a trial. The Explanation to s.15(1) clarifies that the preliminary assessment “is not a trial, but is to assess the capacity of such child to commit and understand the consequences of the alleged offence”; accordingly the request for cross-examination was rightly declined, and by virtue of s.99 all reports relating to the child considered by the Board are to be treated as confidential.
  4. Timelines. s.14(3) requires the preliminary assessment to be disposed of within three months from the date of first production; the Court emphasised that the timelines provided for under the Act have to be adhered to. (This observation was later explained in CCL v. Karnataka, §39, which held the period directory.)
  5. Guidelines. The Court directed the Central Government, the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the State Commissions for Protection of Child Rights to consider issuing guidelines or directions which may assist and facilitate the Board in making the preliminary assessment under s.15.
Principles evolved
(1) “May” means “shall”: where the Board has no member who is a practising professional with a degree in child psychology or child psychiatry, the assistance of an experienced psychologist or psycho-social worker or other expert is mandatory. (2) A s.15 assessment is a quasi-judicial act with serious civil consequences and therefore attracts natural justice (s.3(xvi)) — reasons, a reasonable opportunity, and supply of the order to the child (Rule 10A(4)). (3) “Ability to understand the consequences” means the far-reaching consequences, for the victim, the victim’s family, the child and the child’s family — not merely the immediate physical result. (4) A mere IQ test is not a preliminary assessment. (5) The preliminary assessment is not a trial; cross-examination is not available and the reports are confidential. (6) Trying a child as an adult is an EXCEPTION to the rule, and the Act must be interpreted so as to protect the interests of children.
Aftermath
The NCPCR and the Delhi Commission were directed in parallel High Court proceedings to furnish guidelines; the Delhi High Court, by its order of 19 September 2022, additionally held that questions in the standard Form 6 questionnaire by which “a confession is sought to be extracted from the child as to the manner in which the offence was committed and the reasons thereof” are “unconstitutional and beyond the scope of a report of preliminary assessment to be prepared under Section 15 of the J.J. Act”. Comprehensive national guidelines remain incomplete — which is itself a criticism worth making.

39. CASE 12 — Rahul Kumar Yadav v. State of Bihar (2024)

Rahul Kumar Yadav v. The State of Bihar[2024] 5 S.C.R. 501; 2024 INSC 359; Criminal Appeal No. 177 of 2018, order of 25 April 2024 — B.R. Gavai and Sandeep Mehta, JJ. (per Mehta, J.)belated juvenility · duty to inquire
Facts
The appellant was convicted, along with a co-accused, of murder and robbery under ss.302 and 394 IPC and s.27(2) of the Arms Act, 1959, and was sentenced by the trial court to death. On reference and appeal the Division Bench of the Patna High Court delivered a split verdict, and a third judge ultimately commuted the death sentence to life imprisonment. Throughout the proceedings the appellant had consistently claimed that he was a juvenile on the date of the incident — first before the Chief Judicial Magistrate (relying on his horoscope), then before the trial court (where a birth certificate was produced), and again before the High Court. Each of those pleas was rejected, but — as the Supreme Court found — without any proper inquiry. During the pendency of the appeal before the High Court, the JJ Act, 2015 had come into force.
Issues
(1) Where a plea of juvenility has been raised at the earliest opportunity but has been rejected perfunctorily and without inquiry by every court below, must the Supreme Court direct a fresh inquiry? (2) What is the procedure to be followed, and by which forum? (3) Does the belated stage of the proceedings — after conviction, after a death sentence and after commutation — bar the claim?
Arguments for the appellant
(i) He was a juvenile on the date of the offence and the plea was improperly dismissed without a thorough inquiry; (ii) documentary evidence was ignored — a birth certificate had been produced before the trial court and was summarily rejected; (iii) the JJ Act, 2015 provides a comprehensive mechanism to consider a claim of juvenility and expressly permits it to be raised at any stage; and (iv) the Act emphasises reformation over punishment, so that the adult criminal process was inapplicable to him and the procedural lapses vitiated the trial.
Arguments for the State
(i) The plea was belated and inconsistent — first a horoscope, then a birth certificate — and lacked credibility; (ii) the Chief Judicial Magistrate had rejected the horoscope-based claim and that view had been endorsed by the trial court and the High Court; and (iii) there were no procedural lapses: the courts had appropriately weighed the evidence and had rejected the plea for insufficiency of material.
Decision
The Supreme Court held that the plea had not been properly inquired into and directed a fresh inquiry.
“On going through the record, we find that proper inquiry in accordance with the provisions of the JJ Act, 2000 or the JJ Act, 2015 was NOT CARRIED OUT so as to consider the prayer made by the appellant to be treated as juvenile on the date of the incident, EVEN THOUGH THE PLEA WAS RAISED AT THE EARLIEST OPPORTUNITY.”

“What needs to be kept in mind is the main object and purpose of the Juvenile Justice Act. The focus of this legislation is on the juvenile’s REFORMATION AND REHABILITATION so that he also may have an opportunity to enjoy as other children.”

“Awareness about the rights of the child and correlated duties remain low among the functionaries of the juvenile justice system. ONCE A CHILD IS CAUGHT IN THE WEB OF [THE] ADULT CRIMINAL JUSTICE SYSTEM, IT IS DIFFICULT FOR THE CHILD TO GET OUT OF IT UNSCATHED.” Rahul Kumar Yadav v. State of Bihar
The Court set out the objects of the Act by quoting Pratap Singh — “the said Act is not only a beneficent legislation, but also a remedial one… having regard to Rule 4 of the United Nations Standard Minimum Rules…” — and then directed the First Additional Sessions Judge, Darbhanga, to conduct a thorough inquiry into the appellant’s age and date of birth in accordance with the JJ Act, 2015 and the Rules, including, if necessary, an ossification test, and to complete the inquiry and submit a report within twelve weeks.
Principles evolved
(1) A plea of juvenility may be raised at any stage of the proceedings, and a court before which it is raised is under a positive duty to hold an inquiry; a perfunctory rejection is an error of law. (2) Where the plea is raised belatedly and the documents enumerated in s.94 are unavailable, medical tests may be resorted to for determining the age. (3) The appropriate course for the Supreme Court is to remit the inquiry to the court of first instance, which can take evidence, and to fix a time-limit for the report. (4) The focus of the legislation is reformation and rehabilitation; and the Court’s warning — “once a child is caught in the web of the adult criminal justice system, it is difficult for the child to get out of it unscathed” — is the most quotable sentence in the recent case law. (5) Inconsistency in the material relied upon (a horoscope, then a birth certificate) is not by itself a reason to refuse an inquiry; it is a reason to hold one.
Note the sequel
The principle has been applied repeatedly since. In proceedings concerning Sunder alias Surendra v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2026) the Supreme Court (Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta, JJ.) entertained a claim of juvenility raised in respect of an offence of 7 February 1983, relying on Rahul Kumar Yadav and Vinod Katara, and reiterated that once a plea of juvenility is raised, the court must hold an inquiry by examining evidence and not decide the issue on affidavits.

40. CASE 13 — Child in Conflict with Law through his mother v. State of Karnataka (2024)

Child in Conflict with Law through his Mother v. State of Karnataka & Anr.2024 SCC OnLine SC 798; Criminal Appeal arising out of SLP (Crl.) No. 3033 of 2024, decided 7 May 2024 — C.T. Ravikumar and Rajesh Bindal, JJ.s.14(3) · directory or mandatory
Facts
The appellant, a child in conflict with law, was alleged to have committed a heinous offence and was above sixteen. The Juvenile Justice Board did not complete the preliminary assessment under s.15 within the three months prescribed by s.14(3), but nevertheless proceeded to conduct the assessment and to pass an order under s.18(3) directing that the CCL be tried as an adult by the Children’s Court. The CCL challenged the order. A revision was filed before the High Court under s.397 read with s.399 CrPC, which was dismissed; and it was contended before the Supreme Court that, with the passing of the impugned order by the High Court, the CCL had been left remediless.
Issues
(1) Is the period of three months prescribed by s.14(3) for the completion of a preliminary assessment under s.15 mandatory or directory? (2) If the Board fails to complete the assessment within that period, does the case by default remain with the Board (so that the CCL cannot be tried as an adult)? (3) What is the correct remedy against an order under s.15 — an appeal under s.101(2) or a revision under the CrPC?
Arguments for the CCL
(i) Section 14(3) is mandatory: unlike s.14(2) and (4), it contains no provision for extension, whereas the proviso to s.14(4) expressly permits extension for serious and heinous offences in relation to the inquiry. That contrast shows a deliberate legislative choice. (ii) Barun Chandra Thakur had held that “the timelines provided for under the Act have to be adhered to”. (iii) If the period cannot be extended, no order for trial of the CCL by the Children’s Court can be passed after it expires. (iv) Every individual’s mental capacity and ability to understand evolve with chronological age, so a belated assessment is worthless for the purpose of determining capacity as on the date of the offence — a delayed assessment prejudices the child. (v) A s.15 exercise is “merely a satisfaction recorded” and there is no appealable “order”, so revision was the only remedy.
Arguments for the State
(i) “Merely because proceedings under Section 15 of the Act could not be concluded within three months, BY DEFAULT the CCL will not be tried by the Board. The provision cannot be held to be mandatory, as NO CONSEQUENCE of such a default has been provided in the Act.” (ii) Even the proviso to s.14(4) provides for extension where the inquiry under s.14(1) cannot be concluded in time; the scheme is plainly flexible. (iii) An order under s.15 is appealable — under s.101(1) to the Children’s Court, and under s.101(2) in respect of a preliminary assessment — and no appeal had been filed; the revision under s.397 read with s.399 CrPC was not maintainable.
Decision
The Supreme Court disposed of the appeal with the following directions:
  1. “The provision of Section 14(3) of the Act, providing for the period of three months for completion of a preliminary assessment under Section 15 of the Act, is NOT MANDATORY. The same is held to be DIRECTORY. The period can be extended, for the reasons to be recorded in writing, by the Chief Judicial Magistrate or, as the case may be, the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate.”
  2. The Court reasoned that no consequence is prescribed for a failure to complete the assessment within three months — in contrast with s.14(4), which expressly provides that in the case of a petty offence an inconclusive inquiry results in the termination of proceedings. Where the Legislature has provided a consequence in one sub-section and omitted it in another, the omission is deliberate.
  3. It reaffirmed the scheme: the preliminary assessment under s.15(1) is “only the assessment as to whether the Board or the Children’s Court has to hold [the] inquiry or conduct [the] trial”; on a conclusion that the CCL is to be tried as an adult, the Board must transfer the records to the Children’s Court under s.18(3); the Children’s Court then decides afresh under s.19; and an order of the Children’s Court is appealable to the High Court under s.101(5).
  4. It reiterated Barun Chandra Thakur — that the assistance of a psychologist is mandatory notwithstanding the word “may” in the proviso to s.15(1) and in s.101(2) — and added that, in deciding an appeal against a s.15 order, the appellate authority may itself take the assistance of experts other than those who assisted the Board.
  5. On remedies, it accepted that the appropriate remedy against an order under s.15/s.18(3) is an appeal under s.101 and not a revision under the CrPC.
Principles evolved
(1) s.14(3) is DIRECTORY; the three-month period may be extended by the CJM or CMM for reasons recorded in writing. (2) A statutory time-limit unaccompanied by a prescribed consequence is ordinarily directory — contrast s.14(4), which prescribes termination for petty offences and is therefore mandatory in that respect. (3) The remedy against an order of preliminary assessment or transfer is an appeal under s.101; a CrPC revision is not maintainable. (4) The appellate authority may take fresh expert assistance, which is an important safeguard against a defective assessment. (5) The decision tempers Barun Chandra Thakur’s emphasis on timelines: adherence is required, but breach does not confer an automatic benefit on the child.
Criticism worth making
The CCL’s argument was not a technicality. “Mental capacity” and “ability to understand” evolve with age; an assessment conducted two years after the offence measures the capacity of an older person, not of the child who committed the act. Holding s.14(3) directory therefore creates a real risk that a child will be transferred to the adult system on the basis of an assessment that is, on its own terms, irrelevant. The academic literature makes precisely this point (“belated preliminary assessment is prejudicial to the interest of CCLs”), and it is a strong critical paragraph for an essay on s.15.

41. CASE 14 — Rajni v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2025)

Rajni v. State of Uttar Pradesh & Anr.2025 SCC OnLine SC 602; 2025 LiveLaw (SC) 602; judgment of 20 May 2025 — Abhay S. Oka and Ujjal Bhuyan, JJ.no power of review · primacy of documents · bail
Facts
A case under s.307 IPC (attempt to murder) was registered at the Medical College Police Station, Meerut. The second respondent claimed juvenility on the basis of a high school certificate showing his date of birth as 8 September 2003. The Additional District and Sessions Judge, by order of 14 October 2021, assessed his age on that basis as 17 years, 3 months and 10 days on the date of the incident, and he was accordingly declared a juvenile delinquent. Thereafter, in the same matter, the Juvenile Justice Board ordered a medical examination, disregarding the school record. The medical board estimated his age as about 21 years. On that footing the JJB conducted a preliminary assessment under s.15 and directed that he be tried as an adult, and bail was refused. The High Court, in a criminal revision, set aside the orders of the JJB and of the Additional Sessions Judge, held that the documentary evidence was sufficient, and granted bail on a personal bond by a parent with two sureties, observing that “there were no satisfactory materials on record to conclude that the release of the juvenile would expose him to moral, physical or psychological danger or that it would bring him to be in the association of known criminals”. The informant, Rajni, appealed to the Supreme Court.
Issues
(1) Does the Juvenile Justice Board have the power to review or contradict its own earlier decision on the age of the accused? (2) Can medical opinion override valid documentary evidence (a school certificate) in determining juvenility under s.94 of the JJ Act, 2015? (3) Did the High Court rightly grant bail under s.12 notwithstanding the JJB’s preliminary assessment under s.15?
Arguments for the appellant-informant
(i) The JJB was entitled to order a medical opinion to ascertain the age, notwithstanding the earlier acceptance of the school certificate; (ii) since the medical board assessed the age as 21 years, the accused should be treated and tried as an adult; (iii) the informant had not been a party to the earlier proceeding before the JJB and had therefore had no opportunity to tender evidence or to rebut the claim of juvenility, so that the previous decision was not binding on her; and (iv) the High Court erred in granting bail, especially after a preliminary assessment under s.15 had concluded that the child should be tried as an adult.
Arguments for the respondent-accused
(i) The JJB had already accepted the high school certificate showing the date of birth as 8 September 2003; (ii) the second medical assessment was illegal and amounted in substance to a review of the earlier order, which the JJB has no power to undertake; and (iii) under s.94(2), medical opinion may be resorted to only in the absence of the documents in clauses (i) and (ii); both the Additional Sessions Judge and the High Court had followed the correct procedure.
Decision
The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal, holding:
  1. No power of review. The Juvenile Justice Board is not vested with any power of review under the JJ Act, 2015. “The power of review must be expressly or impliedly conferred by statute”, and no such power is conferred. Any attempt to contradict its earlier decision on the same issue — age — amounts to an unauthorised review and is illegal. (Note that s.104 confers only a limited power to amend orders relating to the institution or person in whose care the child is placed, not a power to revisit a finding.)
  2. Primacy of documentary evidence. “The statute is very clear that ONLY IN THE ABSENCE of the certificates under clause (i) and clause (ii) of sub-section (2) of Section 94 can the JJB order for an ossification test or any other medical test to determine the age of the juvenile.” Medical reports are secondary and cannot override documentary evidence.
  3. Bail. The High Court rightly granted bail under s.12. A preliminary assessment under s.15 does not automatically justify the denial of bail, unless there is specific material to show that release would bring the child into association with a known criminal, expose him to moral, physical or psychological danger, or defeat the ends of justice. There was no such material.
  4. The Court reiterated the eleven principles of Rishipal Singh Solanki — including that the claim may be raised at any stage, that a hyper-technical approach must not be adopted, and that the age recorded by the Board is deemed to be the true age — and added that “if there is a clear and unambiguous case in favour of the juvenile in conflict with law that he was a minor on the date of the incident and that the documentary evidence at least PRIMA FACIE establish[es] the same, he would be entitled to the special protection under the Juvenile Justice Act”.
  5. It also noted, importantly, that “under the scheme of the JJ Act, 2015, a declaration of juvenility may not by itself enure to the benefit of the juvenile in conflict with law” — because, where the offence is heinous and the child is 16–18, the s.15 assessment and the s.19 decision still follow.
Principles evolved
(1) A Juvenile Justice Board has NO POWER OF REVIEW. Having once determined age, it cannot reopen the question; the remedy of an aggrieved informant is an appeal under s.101, not a fresh application to the same Board. (2) Documentary evidence under s.94(2)(i) and (ii) has primacy; an ossification test may be ordered only in their absence. A medical estimate cannot displace a school certificate. (3) A s.15 preliminary assessment is not a ground for refusing bail under s.12. The three grounds in the proviso to s.12(1) are exhaustive and must be supported by material. (4) A declaration of juvenility does not by itself entitle the child to be dealt with by the Board where the offence is heinous and he is above sixteen — the assessment under s.15 and the decision under s.19 remain. (5) The Rishipal Singh Solanki principles are reaffirmed as the governing code.
Why it is the most recent word
Rajni (May 2025) is the latest prescribed case and it closes three loops at once: it settles the finality of a JJB’s age finding, it settles the primacy of documents over medical opinion, and it settles the relationship between s.15 and s.12. Cite it in every age-determination and every bail answer.

43.2 Real-world examples to use

The December 2012 Delhi case
The single event that produced the 2015 Act. One of the six accused was five months short of eighteen; he was tried by the Board and received the maximum disposal — three years in a special home. Use it to frame the whole s.15 debate — and then add the corrective: the Verma Committee, appointed in the aftermath of the same case, examined the question and recommended against lowering the age.
The Gurugram school killing (2017)
The facts of Barun Chandra Thakur: a Class II boy found with his throat slit in the school toilet; three innocent employees initially arrested; the CBI later identifying a 16-year-old student. Children in institutions
The NCPCR’s 2020 social audit found that a very large proportion of child care institutions were run by NGOs, that many were unregistered in breach of s.41, and that a significant number of children in them were not orphans at all but children of poor living parents placed in institutions for want of food and schooling — the empirical vindication of s.45 sponsorship over institutionalisation and of the hierarchy in Figure 8.
Children of prisoners and the pandemic

Vinod Katara (a convict over 50 claiming he was 15 in the 1980s) and the 2026 proceedings concerning an offence of 1983. 44.1 DU 2022 · Q.2 — the problem question on Sahil

2022 · Q.2
Sahil, a boy aged 17 years, committed Rape of his classmate somewhere in outer-Delhi. Since the rape has caused great resentment in the victim’s village, the police promptly arrested Sahil and the Magistrate remanded him to judicial custody. Sahil was detained as an under-trial in the Tihar Prison. On being put for trial before the Sessions Court, Sahil challenged his detention in adult prison, as well as his trial by a Sessions Court. Decide as per the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015. Mention relevant provisions of law, case law and the school of thought you are relying on while choosing the sentence.
Model answer

1. Identification of the issues. Four questions arise: (i) Is Sahil a “child in conflict with law”? (ii) Was his detention in Tihar Prison lawful? (iii) Was the Magistrate’s remand and the Sessions Court’s assumption of jurisdiction lawful? (iv) What, on these facts, is the correct disposition, and on what penal philosophy?

2. Sahil is a child in conflict with law. Under s.2(12) a “child” is a person who has not completed eighteen years, and under s.2(13) a “child in conflict with law” is a child who is alleged or found to have committed an offence and who has not completed eighteen years of age on the date of commission of such offence. Sahil is 17. The reckoning date is the date of the offence — the rule laid down by the Constitution Bench in Pratap Singh v. State of Jharkhand (2005): “the relevant date for determining the age of the juvenile would be one on which the offence has been committed and not when he is produced in court”; and “a person cannot be a juvenile for one purpose and an adult for another purpose”. That rule is now codified in s.2(13). If his age were disputed it would have to be determined under s.94 in the strict hierarchy — school or matriculation certificate, then birth certificate, and only in their absence an ossification test (Rishipal Singh Solanki (2021); Rajni (2025)).

3. The detention in Tihar Prison is wholly illegal. Section 10 provides that a child alleged to be in conflict with law shall be placed under the charge of the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the designated Child Welfare Police Officer and produced before the Board within twenty-four hours; and the proviso is categorical — “in no case shall a child alleged to be in conflict with law be placed in a police lock-up or lodged in a jail”. If bail is refused under s.12, the child must be sent to an observation home (s.2(62), s.47) or, where the offence is heinous and he is above sixteen, to a place of safety (s.2(46), s.49) — never to a prison. This is not a new rule. In Sheela Barse v. Union of India (1986) the Supreme Court held that “children should not be confined to jail because incarceration in jail has a dehumanising effect and it is harmful to the growth and development of children”, that it is “no answer on the part of the State to urge that the ward in the jail where the children are kept is separate”, because “it is the ATMOSPHERE of the jail which has a highly injurious effect on the mind of the child”, and that “on no account should the children be kept in jail” — if there is no accommodation in a home, the child must be released on bail. In Sanjay Suri v. Delhi Administration (1988), dealing with Tihar Jail itself, the Court found that juvenile prisoners had been sexually assaulted by adult prisoners, directed their release, and laid down that every warrant of detention must specify the age of the person to be detained and that jail authorities may refuse to honour a warrant which does not. The Magistrate’s warrant in Sahil’s case evidently did not; the Superintendent was entitled — indeed bound — to refuse it. Sahil is therefore entitled to be immediately removed from Tihar; and the Board is under a statutory duty under s.8(3)(xiii) to inspect adult jails and take immediate measures for transfer of any child found there.

4. The Magistrate’s remand and the Sessions Court’s trial are without jurisdiction. Section 4(1) begins “Notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973” and vests exclusive jurisdiction over children in conflict with law in the Juvenile Justice Board; s.8(1) gives the Board “power to deal exclusively with all the proceedings under this Act relating to children in conflict with law”. Section 9(1) provides that a Magistrate not empowered under the Act who is of the opinion that the person before him is a child shall without any delay record such opinion and forward the child immediately, with the record, to the Board; and s.9(2) requires any court before which a claim of juvenility is raised to hold an inquiry on evidence (but not on affidavit). Under s.9(3), if the court finds that the person was a child on the date of the offence, it shall forward him to the Board and “the sentence, if any, passed by the court shall be deemed to have no effect”. On these principles Raghbir v. State of Haryana (1981) is directly in point: the Supreme Court held that s.27 CrPC is merely an ENABLING provision, that the Children Act is a special law saved by s.5 CrPC, that the two “can co-exist; their spheres of operation are different”, and that a child must be tried under the special law even for an offence punishable with death or imprisonment for life — and it accordingly quashed the entire trial. Union of India v. Ex-GNR Ajeet Singh (2013) shows how absolute the rule is: even a General Court Martial was vitiated in its entirety because juvenile-period and adult-period charges had been tried together, contrary to what is now s.23. Sahil’s challenge therefore succeeds: the proceedings before the Sessions Court must be set aside and the matter transferred to the Board.

5. Bail. Under s.12, Sahil “shall, notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973… be released on bail with or without surety” even though the offence is non-bailable, unless there are reasonable grounds for believing that release would (a) bring him into association with a known criminal, (b) expose him to moral, physical or psychological danger, or (c) defeat the ends of justice — and the Board must record reasons. The resentment in the victim’s village is legally relevant only under limb (b): it may suggest a risk to Sahil himself, in which case the correct order is not detention in prison but placement in an observation home or place of safety. Rajni v. State of U.P. (2025) confirms that a preliminary assessment under s.15 is no ground for refusing bail absent material under the proviso. If he cannot furnish surety within seven days, s.12(4) requires production for modification of the conditions.

6. The forum question: can Sahil be tried as an adult? Rape under s.64 BNS (s.376 IPC) carries a minimum of ten years, so it is a heinous offence under s.2(33) (“minimum punishment… seven years or more”) — contrast Shilpa Mittal (2020), where an offence with no minimum was held not to be heinous. Sahil is above sixteen. The Board is therefore required to conduct a preliminary assessment under s.15 into (i) his mental capacity to commit the offence, (ii) his physical capacity, (iii) his ability to understand the consequences of the offence, and (iv) the circumstances in which he allegedly committed it — within three months of first production (s.14(3), held directory in CCL v. State of Karnataka (2024)). Per Barun Chandra Thakur (2022): the assistance of an experienced psychologist is mandatory if the Board has no member with a degree in child psychology or psychiatry; “consequences” means the far-reaching consequences for the victim, the victim’s family, the child and his family; a mere IQ test will not do; the assessment is not a trial (Explanation to s.15) but, having serious civil consequences, attracts natural justice (s.3(xvi)); and reasons must be assigned and the order given to the child (Rule 10A(4)). If the Board concludes there is no need for an adult trial, it disposes of the matter itself under s.15(2) following the summons-case procedure. If it concludes otherwise, it transfers the case under s.18(3) to the Children’s Court (s.2(20) — here, a Special Court under POCSO or the Court of Sessions), which must decide afresh under s.19(1). Note that this is a transfer under the Act, not a trial by the Sessions Court in exercise of its ordinary jurisdiction — which is what happened to Sahil, and which is illegal.

7. The sentence, and the school of thought. If the Board retains the case, its powers are those in s.18(1) — advice or admonition; group counselling; community service; fine; probation of good conduct under the care of a parent, guardian, fit person or fit facility for up to three years; or a special home for a maximum of three years — together with the ancillary orders in s.18(2) (attend school, vocational training, therapeutic centre, de-addiction). Given the gravity, the appropriate order would be committal to a special home for three years under s.18(1)(g), with an individual care plan providing education, skill development, counselling, behaviour modification therapy and psychiatric support, and follow-up by the Probation Officer. If the case is transferred, the Children’s Court may try him as an adult, but he must be kept in a place of safety till 21 (s.19(3)), receiving reformative services (s.19(4)), with a mandatory review at 21 under s.20 to evaluate whether he has undergone reformative changes — upon which he may be released on conditions with a monitoring authority, or transferred to jail for the balance. In no event may he be sentenced to death or to life imprisonment without the possibility of release: s.21, Art. 37(a) UNCRC, Beijing Rule 17.2. The victim is entitled to compensation under s.396 BNSS (s.357-A CrPC) and the NALSA scheme, and — as Jitendra Singh (2013) shows — the Court may award compensation from the State where the offender cannot be punished as an adult.

8. The school of thought. As the DU model answer itself records: “In order to arrive at this decision, the REFORMATIVE school of thought / Reformative Theory of punishment has been relied upon as cited in several landmark cases discussed above.” Develop it: the reformative theory holds that the offender is a product of circumstances and is capable of change, and that the object of the sanction is rehabilitation and reintegration rather than retribution or deterrence. The philosophy is written into the statute — s.3(iv) best interest, s.3(xii) institutionalisation as a last resort, s.3(xiii) restoration, s.3(xiv) fresh start, s.3(xv) diversion, s.24 removal of disqualification and destruction of records — and into the international instruments (Beijing Rule 17.1(d): “the well-being of the juvenile shall be the guiding factor”). It has been affirmed judicially in Satto v. State of U.P. (1979), where Krishna Iyer J. held that the child offender “should not be treated as a target of harsh punishment but of humane nourishment” and that it is essential to “rescue the youthful [offender] from moral and material abandonment and provide careful reformation by kindling his creative potential”; in Sudesh Kumar (2008), on the rationale of s.6 of the Probation of Offenders Act — to prevent “the turning of youthful offenders into criminals by their association with hardened criminals of mature age within the walls of a prison”; and in Rahul Kumar Yadav (2024) — “the focus of this legislation is on the juvenile’s reformation and rehabilitation… once a child is caught in the web of the adult criminal justice system, it is difficult for the child to get out of it unscathed”. Criminologically the position is supported by Sutherland’s differential association (association with hardened offenders teaches crime), by labelling theory (the label creates the career) and by Matza’s drift (most adolescent offenders desist).

9. Conclusion. Sahil’s challenge succeeds on both counts. His detention in Tihar Prison is illegal and he must be removed forthwith to an observation home or place of safety, or released on bail under s.12. The Sessions Court has no jurisdiction: the proceedings are without authority of law and must be set aside; the Magistrate ought to have forwarded him to the Board under s.9(1). The case must go to the Juvenile Justice Board, which — the offence being heinous and Sahil being above sixteen — must conduct a preliminary assessment under s.15 with the mandatory assistance of a psychologist, and then either dispose of the matter itself under s.18 or transfer it under s.18(3) to the Children’s Court for a fresh decision under s.19. Whatever the forum, s.21 forbids death or irreducible life imprisonment, and the governing philosophy is reformative.
