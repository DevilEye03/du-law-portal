2. Role of Police DU CORE

2.1 Origin & development of the Indian police (Paranjape)

  • Ancient India: organised policing existed — Manusmriti speaks of spies and village watchmen; Kautilya's Arthashastra describes an elaborate system of gopas, sthanikas and secret agents for prevention and detection of crime.
  • Medieval/Mughal period: faujdars (district military-police chiefs), kotwals (city police), village chowkidars — policing fused with revenue and military functions.
  • British period: after the 1857 uprising the Police Commission of 1860 recommended a civil police; result — the Police Act, 1861, still the basic framework. S. 3 vests “superintendence” in the State Government; the force was designed more for order-maintenance for the rulers than service to the people.
  • 1902–03 Fraser (Police) Commission: first comprehensive review — recommended better pay, training, CID in each province, and separation of judicial and police functions; most executive-control features retained.
  • Post-independence: Gore Committee on police training (1971); National Police Commission (1977–81); state police commissions; judicial intervention culminating in Prakash Singh (2006).
⚖️ Core critique (exam line): the 1861 framework was built for a colonial ruler to control subjects; free India's police is still judged “selectively efficient, unsympathetic to the underprivileged, politicised and criminalised” (MHA Status Note) — hence reforms aim to convert a ruler's police into a people's police service.

2.2 Eight Principles of Policing (Paranjape)

  1. Prevention first: the basic mission of police is the prevention of crime, not merely its detection and repression.
  2. Public approval: the ability of police to perform depends on public approval of police actions and behaviour.
  3. Voluntary cooperation: police must secure the willing cooperation of the public in observing law — voluntary compliance reduces the need for compulsion and force.
  4. Impartial service: police must offer impartial service to law, without fear or favour, and show courtesy and good humour.
  5. Minimum force: use physical force only when persuasion, advice and warning fail, and then only the minimum necessary.
  6. Police–public unity: “the police are the public and the public are the police” — policemen are only members of the public paid to give full-time attention to duties incumbent on every citizen.
  7. No judicial usurpation: police must never usurp the powers of the judiciary — they only bring offenders before courts and assist prosecution.
  8. Test of efficiency: the test of police efficiency is the absence of crime and disorder, not visible evidence of police action.

2.3 Role of police in society & the criminal justice system

  • Gatekeeper of the CJS: police decide which complaints become FIRs, whom to arrest, what evidence to collect — the quality of prosecution and trial depends on the quality of policing.
  • Conflict-ridden role: police must simultaneously enforce law (coercion) and serve people (consent); maintain order yet protect liberty; obey political masters yet act impartially. This tension is the root of most police deviance.
  • Political interference: transfers, postings and investigations are routinely used as instruments of patronage — “the greatest single factor” weakening professional policing; insulated only by tenure security and independent complaints bodies (the logic of Prakash Singh).
  • Public image: fear, distrust and non-cooperation mark police–public relations; community policing, citizen committees and transparent complaint redress are prescribed correctives.
“The police in India suffers from an image of brutality, corruption and political subservience; unless the common man begins to see the policeman as a friend and protector, the criminal justice system cannot command legitimacy.” — substance of Paranjape's summing-up (see also Nani Palkhivala's lament on the decline of policing standards quoted in the chapter).

4. Custodial Violence & Judicial Controls DU CORE

4.1 The problem (Paranjape)

  • Custodial torture, deaths, rapes and illegal detention persist despite constitutional (Arts. 20–22) and statutory safeguards — attributed to colonial attitudes, pressure to “show results”, lack of scientific aids, and impunity.
  • Judiciary's indictment: police described as “the largest single lawless group” in the country; courts have repeatedly laid down preventive guidelines and awarded compensation.

4.3 NHRC guidelines on police–public relations (22-12-1999)

  • Set up toll-free complaint numbers; display rights of arrestees at every police station; transparency in FIR registration (receipt to complainant).
  • Monthly meetings of SHOs with community representatives; periodic human-rights training; report every custodial death/rape to NHRC within 24 hours.
  • Paranjape adds: Citizen Volunteers' Force experiments (Delhi, Mumbai), mohalla committees, and victim-assistance cells as bridges between police and public.

5. Police Reforms: Master Chronology DU CORE

1861 — Police Act, 1861 (after 1860 Police Commission): colonial control model; s. 3 — State Government's “superintendence”.
1902–03 — Fraser Police Commission: pay, training, CID; control structure retained.
1971 — Gore Committee on police training.
1977–81 — National Police Commission (Dharam Vira): 8 reports, Feb 1979–May 1981; State Security Commission, tenure security, investigation separation — mostly unimplemented.
1996 — Prakash Singh PIL filed (ex-DGPs) seeking implementation of NPC recommendations.
1998–99 — Ribeiro Committee (J.F. Ribeiro, on SC direction 25-5-1998): endorses NPC with modifications; two reports.
2000 — Padmanabhaiah Committee (report 30-8-2000): ~240 recommendations on restructuring for the new millennium.
2002–03 — Malimath Committee on CJS reforms (report April 2003): 158 recommendations incl. investigation separation, SSC, federal crimes agency.
2004–06 — MHA Review Committee shortlists 49 crucial recommendations; NIC sub-committee (29-7-2006).
2006 — Model Police Act (30-10-2006); Prakash Singh judgment 22-9-2006: 7 binding directives, compliance by 31-12-2006.
2007 — National Security Commission mechanism set up (2-1-2007); 11-1-2007: three directives declared self-executory.
2008–10 — Justice K.T. Thomas Monitoring Committee (16-5-2008); report circulated 4-10-2010.
2017 — T.P. Senkumar v. UOI (24-4-2017): first-ever reinstatement of a removed DGP; tenure protection enforced.
2018–19 — Follow-up: no “acting DGPs” (3-7-2018); minimum 6-month residual tenure + merit rule (13-3-2019).
⚖️ Why reforms stalled — the standard exam answer: (a) “Police” is a State subject (List-II, Seventh Schedule) — Centre can only persuade, not compel; (b) entrenched political interest in controlling transfers, postings and investigations; (c) resource crunch and colonial work culture. The MHA Status Note expressly admits the Centre's “constitutional limitations”.

6. National Police Commission, 1977–81 DU CORE

Constituted
15 November 1977, Chairman Dharam Vira (ex-Cabinet Secretary); comprehensive review of the police system at national level.
Tenure of work
Eight reports, February 1979 to May 1981; first report tabled in Lok Sabha 1-2-1980; remaining seven released March 1983 with Centre's directive to States to act.
Terms of reference
10 heads — incl. role of police, political interference, accountability, investigation, personnel, training, community relations, modernisation.

Landmark recommendations (examination favourites)

  1. State Security Commission (SSC) in every State — to lay down policy, evaluate performance, and insulate police from extraneous pressures; annual report to the legislature.
  2. Fixed tenures for DGP and field officers (SP and above) to prevent arbitrary transfers.
  3. Separation of investigation from law-and-order duties.
  4. Central Police Committee and an All-India Police Institute for standards, research and training.
  5. Amendments to CrPC 1973 (guidelines on arrest, victim protection) — partly carried into the CrPC (Amendment) Bill, 1994 (passed 2005).
  6. Recruitment, training and welfare reforms; revised IPS probationers' syllabus; augmentation of DCPW; regulation of private security agencies (later enacted as a separate Act).
📌 Linkage line for answers: the NPC's core ideas — SSC, tenure security, investigation separation, complaints redress — were endorsed by Ribeiro, repeated by Malimath, and finally converted into binding law by the Supreme Court in Prakash Singh under Arts. 32/142 “till appropriate legislations are framed.”

7. Ribeiro • Padmanabhaiah • Malimath • Model Police Act DU CORE

7.1 Ribeiro Committee (1998–99)

  • Constituted 25-5-1998 under J.F. Ribeiro, IPS (Retd.), on the Supreme Court's direction in the pending Prakash Singh PIL — to review action taken on NPC recommendations and suggest implementation.
  • Submitted two reports (1998, 1999) filed in the Supreme Court; endorsed NPC with modifications; on 10-2-2005 the Court sought responses of UOI, States and NHRC on Ribeiro + Vineet Narain directions.

7.2 Padmanabhaiah Committee (2000)

  • Set up January 2000 under K. Padmanabhaiah (ex-Union Home Secretary) for structural changes for the new millennium; report 30-8-2000 with ~240 recommendations.
  • 23 rejected by MHA (e.g., direct IPS charge of districts, IAS/IPS as judicial magistrates, entry age 24, federal offences list).
  • 154 implementable without structural change (recruitment, training, beat system revival, highway patrolling, police-station design, SP-level postings, public involvement in crime prevention).

7.3 Malimath Committee (2000–03)

  • Under Dr. Justice V.S. Malimath (ex-CJ, Karnataka & Kerala HCs), report April 2003: 158 recommendations for revamping the whole criminal justice system.
  • Police-relevant: separate investigation wing in police stations; more investigative posts; State Security Commission; central law-enforcement agency for federal crimes; stronger FSLs/fingerprint infrastructure; new Police Act.

7.4 MHA Review Committee (2004–06) & NIC sub-committee

  • After the PM's 2004 interaction with DGPs/IGPs, MHA (Dec 2004) shortlisted 49 crucial recommendations under five heads: (I) professional standards in urban/rural stations; (II) internal-security role; (III) recruitment, training, career, service conditions; (IV) complaints (non-registration, arrests); (V) insulating police from extraneous influences.
  • Reviewed with Chief Secretaries/DGPs (Sept 2005, Feb 2006) and by the Committee of Secretaries; NIC sub-committee (7 CMs + 3 eminent persons + Law Minister) met 29-7-2006 stressing intelligence, training and accountability.

7.5 Model Police Act, 2006 (Expert Committee, Sept 2005 → draft 30-10-2006)

  • Philosophy: a professional police “service” in a democracy — efficient, responsive, accountable to the rule of law, governed by impartiality and human-rights norms with protection for weaker sections and minorities.
  • Functional autonomy: defined “superintendence” of Government; State Police Board; merit-based DGP selection; tenure security; Establishment Committees.
  • Professionalism: dedicated investigation staff; distinct civil-police vs armed-police cadres.
  • Accountability paramount — for performance and conduct.
  • Service conditions: rational hours, weekly off/compensatory benefits, Police Welfare Bureau (health, housing, legal aid, family security), insurance, risk allowances.
  • Sent to States 31-10-2006; ~15 States then enacted new/amended Police Acts (Assam, Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Haryana, HP, Kerala, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Punjab, Rajasthan, Sikkim, Tripura, Uttarakhand + Gujarat, Karnataka amendments).

8. Case 25: Prakash Singh v. Union of India, (2006) 8 SCC 1 DU CORE

Citation
W.P. (C) No. 310 of 1996, decided 22 September 2006 — (2006) 8 SCC 1. Bench: Y.K. Sabharwal, C.J., C.K. Thakker & P.K. Balasubramanyan, JJ. Jurisdiction: Art. 32 r/w Art. 142.
Petitioners
Prakash Singh (former DGP, Assam & U.P.; ex-DG, BSF) and other retired police officers — a PIL filed in 1996.

8.1 Facts & background

  • Petitioners sought implementation of the NPC recommendations (1979–81), which had gathered dust for 15+ years despite repeated assurances.
  • During pendency the Court directed constitution of the Ribeiro Committee (1998); its two reports endorsed NPC with modifications. Pleadings also invoked the Vineet Narain v. UOI (1998) 1 SCC 226 model — where the Court had issued CBI/SVC reform directions to fill a legislative vacuum.
  • UOI, States/UTs and NHRC filed responses (per order dated 10-2-2005); Sorabjee Committee inputs were also before the Court.

8.2 Issues before the Court

  1. Whether the Supreme Court can issue binding directions to reform police organisation where the legislature/executive has failed to act for decades?
  2. What institutional framework (commissions, tenures, boards, complaints authorities) is necessary to secure professional, politically-neutral policing?

8.3 Arguments

Petitioners / supporting intervenors: NPC–Ribeiro recommendations enjoy expert consensus; police politicisation violates Arts. 14 & 21 (fair investigation is part of the right to life); Vineet Narain sanctions judge-made frameworks till legislation; directions under Art. 142 can fill the vacuum.
States / Union (resistance): “Police” is a State subject — a uniform judicial scheme offends federalism; ground conditions vary (insurgency, UTs without legislatures); financial/administrative burden; separation of powers — reform is for legislatures. (The Court recorded but overruled these objections given decades of inaction.)

8.4 Decision: the SEVEN directives (para 31) — learn verbatim structure

Directive (1) — State Security Commission (SSC)
Every State to constitute an SSC (on any of the NHRC / Ribeiro / Sorabjee models) to ensure the Government does not exercise unwanted influence on police. Headed by the CM or HM as Chairperson, DGP as ex-officio Secretary. Functions: frame policy guidelines for crime prevention & maintenance of order; evaluate State police performance and place an annual report before the legislature; ensure no extraneous pressure. Recommendations binding on Government.
Directive (2) — Selection & minimum tenure of DGP
DGP to be selected from among the three senior-most officers empanelled by UPSC for the rank; once selected, minimum tenure of 2 years irrespective of superannuation; removal only in consultation with the SSC and on grounds of: action under AIS (Discipline & Appeal) Rules, conviction in criminal/civil/corruption case, or incapacity (physical/mental).
Directive (3) — Minimum tenure of field officers
IG (Zone), DIG (Range), SP (district) and SHO to have minimum 2-year tenure; premature removal only after disciplinary proceedings or on exceptional grounds recorded in writing.
Directive (4) — Separation of investigation from law & order
Investigation police to be separated from law-and-order police for speedier, more expert investigation — beginning with towns/cities of 10 lakh+ population, gradually extended to smaller towns. (Echoes NPC, Malimath and the Model Police Act.)
Directive (5) — Police Establishment Board (PEB)
A PEB in each State (DGP + 4 senior officers) to decide all transfers, postings, promotions and service matters of officers of and below DySP rank, and to recommend transfers/postings of officers of SP rank and above; Government may depart from recommendations only in exceptional cases for reasons recorded.
Directive (6) — Police Complaints Authorities (PCA)
District-level PCA (headed by a retired District Judge) for complaints against officers of and below DySP rank; State-level PCA (headed by a retired HC/SC Judge) for complaints against officers of SP rank and above; members chosen from panels suggested by NHRC / Lok Ayukta / State PSC etc. Cognizance of serious misconduct — death, grievous hurt or rape in police custody, extortion, land-grabbing. Recommendations binding.
Directive (7) — National Security Commission (NSC)
Central Government to set up an NSC at Union level (HM as Chair; heads of CPOs + security experts as members; Union Home Secretary as Secretary) to prepare panels for selection of Chiefs of Central Police Organisations (CRPF, BSF, CISF, ITBP, SSB, NSG, CBI etc.), who get minimum 2-year tenure; plus mandate to review force effectiveness, service conditions, coordination and proper utilisation.
⏰ Compliance timetable: all mechanisms to be set up by 31 December 2006; affidavits of compliance by 3 January 2007. Directions to operate “till appropriate legislations are framed” — i.e., they fill the legislative vacuum under Art. 142.

8.5 The Bhushan coda (often missed — DU material includes it)

Counsel Prashant Bhushan suggested a specialised central investigation agency for inter-state/terror offences; the Court did not rule on it but directed NHRC, Sorabjee Committee inputs, BPR&D and the Centre to file views within 4 months, with monitoring through NHRC/Police Bureau machinery — the seed that later matured into the NIA Act, 2008 debate. Real example: the NIA was in fact created after 26/11 (Dec 2008).

8.6 Ratio & principles evolved

  1. Judicial-legislation doctrine extended: where executive apathy perpetuates violation of Arts. 14/21 (politicised, unaccountable policing), the Court may issue continuing-mandamus-style structural directions under Arts. 32/142 — Vineet Narain applied to State police.
  2. Insulation + accountability twin: functional autonomy (tenures, SSC, PEB, NSC) must pair with external accountability (PCAs) — autonomy without accountability breeds impunity and vice versa.
  3. Binding till legislation: directives operate as law until States enact their own Police Acts — several States' subsequent Acts were tested against this template.

8.7 Critical evaluation (for 15-mark answers)

  • Achievements: first uniform national template; tenure litigation (e.g., Senkumar) now anchored to it; ~15+ States enacted new Police Acts; separation of investigation begun in Delhi, Puducherry, Chandigarh.
  • Shortfalls: selective/cometic compliance — SSCs packed with officials, PCAs starved of staff or given recommendatory status, tenures circumvented via “acting” arrangements (struck down in 2018); UT difficulties (no legislature/DGP); Centre's own NSC compliance initially found unsatisfactory.
  • Standard concluding line: “Prakash Singh supplied the skeleton of police reform; the flesh — political will, resources and genuine complaints redress — is still awaited.”

9. T.P. Senkumar v. Union of India & Ors. (2017) DU CORE (starred)

Citation
Decided 24 April 2017 — AIR 2017 SC 2628. Bench: Madan B. Lokur & Deepak Gupta, JJ.
Appellant
T.P. Senkumar, IPS — State Police Chief (DGP), Kerala.

9.1 Facts

  • Senkumar became DGP/State Police Chief, Kerala; in May 2016 the newly-elected LDF Government removed him mid-tenure, citing (a) the Puttingal temple-fire tragedy (Kollam, April 2016 — 100+ deaths) and (b) alleged mishandling of the Jisha murder case investigation — invoking s. 97(2)(e), Kerala Police Act, 2011 (removal on Government's “serious dissatisfaction”).
  • He challenged removal before the Central Administrative Tribunal (lost) and the Kerala High Court (lost), then appealed by SLP to the Supreme Court.

9.2 Issues

  1. Can a State Government remove its DGP mid-tenure on subjective “dissatisfaction” without objective, verifiable material?
  2. Does Prakash Singh's two-year tenure protection bind States even where a State Police Act (Kerala, 2011) provides a removal clause?

9.3 Arguments

Senkumar (appellant): removal was political and punitive — a new Government sweeping out the police chief; no inquiry, no charge, no hearing; neither tragedy was attributable to him personally; Prakash Singh tenure + Kerala Act's own scheme protect him; police chiefs cannot be made scapegoats for every tragedy.
State of Kerala: Government lost confidence after two high-profile failures; s. 97(2)(e) expressly permits removal on dissatisfaction; policing needs a chief enjoying the Government's trust; courts cannot substitute their satisfaction for the executive's.

9.4 Decision & principle evolved

  • Appeal allowed; removal quashed; Senkumar ordered to be reinstated as DGP — the first-ever judicial reinstatement of a removed DGP in India.
  • Principle 1 — objective satisfaction: “dissatisfaction” under s. 97(2)(e) must rest on objective, verifiable material on record, not subjective or political displeasure; courts can test whether such material exists.
  • Principle 2 — tenure protection is real: Prakash Singh's minimum-tenure guarantee cannot be defeated by vague allegations or change of Government; else the directive becomes a dead letter.
  • Principle 3 — no scapegoating: police leadership cannot be removed merely because a tragedy occurred on its watch; accountability requires attribution of fault through due process.
📌 Exam linkage: cite Senkumar as the leading enforcement decision on Prakash Singh Directive (2) — proof that tenure security is judicially enforceable, and pair it with the 2018–19 follow-up orders below.

10. Follow-up Orders & Implementation Status VALUE-ADD

10.1 Monitoring timeline after 22-9-2006

  • 11-1-2007: Court holds Directives (2) DGP tenure, (3) field-officer tenure, (5) PEB to be self-executory — comply within 4 weeks; time till 31-3-2007 for (1) SSC, (4) separation, (6) PCA.
  • 2-1-2007: Centre sets up Committee on National Security & Central Police Personnel Welfare (HM-Chair; NSA, Cabinet Secy, Home Secy, IB Director) — Court initially unsatisfied; UOI's modification plea (12-2-2007) lingered.
  • 16-5-2008: Justice K.T. Thomas Monitoring Committee (2-year term) to audit affidavits against ground realities; report circulated to States/UTs 4-10-2010.
  • 16-10-2012: fresh status reports ordered (due 4-12-2012); MHA filed affidavit 26-2-2013.

10.2 The two decisive follow-ups on DGP appointments

  1. Order dated 3-7-2018 — end of “acting DGPs”: States barred from appointing acting/interim DGPs; DGP must be chosen from a UPSC-empanelled panel; proposal to be sent to UPSC 3 months before the incumbent's retirement — killing the practice of bypassing tenure through officiating arrangements.
  2. Order dated 13-3-2019 (CJI Ranjan Gogoi bench): on Prakash Singh's own application (alleging misuse in U.P.), clarified that the appointee must have a minimum residual tenure of 6 months and appointment must be strictly on merit from the panel — tenure protection given teeth against last-month appointments.

10.3 Ground-level compliance snapshot (from the MHA Status Note)

  • UTs: Security Commissions for all UTs (HM-Secretary-chaired; Delhi under L-G); PCAs constituted (23-3-2010); PEBs in all UTs; separation of investigation implemented in Delhi (only UT with 10L+ cities) + major Puducherry stations + Chandigarh enabling provision.
  • States: wide variation — new Police Acts in ~15 States but dilution complaints (official-dominated SSCs, recommendatory PCAs).

11. MHA Status Note on Police Reforms DU CORE (starred PDF)

Document
“Status Note on Police Reforms in India”, Ministry of Home Affairs (mha.gov.in — PoliceReforms(E)181013.pdf), the Government's own stock-taking cited in the DU syllabus.
Opening diagnosis
Reforms on agenda since independence, yet police seen as “selectively efficient, unsympathetic to the under-privileged”, accused of politicisation and criminalisation; the 1861 framework is obsolete for modern expectations, human rights and new crime forms.

Structure of the Note (learn the heads)

  1. Committees/Commissions — NPC (1978–82), Ribeiro (1998), Padmanabhaiah (2000), Malimath (2002–03).
  2. Constitutional limitations of the Centre — Police is a State subject; States must implement; Centre only persuades (successive Home Ministers' letters to CMs).
  3. NPC reports — 8 reports; CrPC amendments via 1994 Bill (passed 2005); IPS syllabus, DCPW, private-security-agencies Act.
  4. Ribeiro reports — endorsed NPC with modifications; filed in SC 1998–99.
  5. Padmanabhaiah — 240 recs; 23 rejected, 154 implementable without structural change.
  6. Malimath — 158 recs (training, FSLs, new Police Act, federal agency, investigation separation, SSC).
  7. MHA Review Committee (Dec 2004) — 49 crucial recs under 5 heads; follow-up reviews Sept 2005/Feb 2006; NIC sub-committee 29-7-2006.
  8. Model Police Act (30-10-2006) — functional autonomy, professionalism, accountability, service conditions; circulated 31-10-2006.
  9. Prakash Singh judgment & follow-up — 7 directives restated (6 for States/UTs + NSC for Centre); K.T. Thomas Committee (16-5-2008); Centre's NSC order (2-1-2007); UT compliance; 11-1-2007 self-executory order; status reports 2012–13; matter sub judice.

12. PYQ + Best Model Answer & Expected Questions

12.1 DU 2017 — Q. 8(a): “Discuss the police reforms directed by the Supreme Court in Prakash Singh v. Union of India.”

⭐ Best model answer (15-mark structure — introduction → 7 directives → enforcement → critique → conclusion)

Introduction — the context: In Prakash Singh v. Union of India, (2006) 8 SCC 1 — W.P.(C) 310/1996 decided 22-9-2006 by Y.K. Sabharwal, C.J., C.K. Thakker and P.K. Balasubramanyan, JJ. — the Supreme Court confronted three decades of unimplemented expert reports (NPC 1979–81; Ribeiro 1998–99). Invoking Arts. 32 r/w 142 on the Vineet Narain (1998) pattern, the Court issued seven binding structural directives to the Centre, States and UTs, operative till appropriate legislations are framed, holding that politicised, unaccountable policing violates Arts. 14 and 21.

The seven directives (para 31): (1) State Security Commission in every State (NHRC/Ribeiro/Sorabjee models; CM/HM chair; binding policy + evaluation reports to legislature) to block unwanted governmental influence. (2) DGP selection from three UPSC-empanelled senior-most officers with 2-year tenure irrespective of superannuation, removable only via SSC consultation on AIS-rules/conviction/incapacity grounds. (3) 2-year tenure for IG (Zone), DIG (Range), SP (district) and SHO. (4) Separation of investigation from law-and-order, starting in 10-lakh-plus cities. (5) Police Establishment Board (DGP + 4 officers) for transfers/postings/promotions up to DySP rank and recommendations above it. (6) Police Complaints Authorities at district (retd. District Judge; up to DySP) and State level (retd. HC/SC Judge; SP and above) for serious misconduct — custodial death, grievous hurt, rape — with binding recommendations. (7) National Security Commission at the Union level (HM chair) for panels to head Central Police Organisations with 2-year tenures. Compliance by 31-12-2006, affidavits by 3-1-2007.

Enforcement machinery: order dated 11-1-2007 declared Directives (2), (3) and (5) self-executory (4 weeks); Justice K.T. Thomas Monitoring Committee (16-5-2008); no “acting DGPs” + UPSC panel + 3-month proposal rule (3-7-2018); 6-month residual tenure + merit rule (13-3-2019); and T.P. Senkumar v. UOI (24-4-2017) — the first reinstatement of a removed DGP — holding that “dissatisfaction” must rest on objective, verifiable material.

Critical appraisal: the judgment supplied a uniform national template pairing functional autonomy with external accountability, spurring ~15 new State Police Acts and Delhi's investigation separation; but compliance is patchy — official-packed SSCs, recommendatory PCAs, and federal limits (“Police” being a State subject, conceded even in the MHA's own Status Note).

Conclusion: Prakash Singh converted the NPC's 1981 vision into enforceable law — a ruler's police into a people's police service — but, as continuing monitoring shows, judicial directions can supply the skeleton; only political will can supply the flesh.
