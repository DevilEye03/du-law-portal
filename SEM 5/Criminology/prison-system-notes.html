2. Evolution of Prisons: USA, UK, Russia & the UN DU CORE

2.1 Why prisons? — the four objectives (Paranjape)

Imprisonment is a “curious combination” of objectives: (1) deterrence, (2) retribution/vengeance, (3) prevention (incapacitation + elimination of criminals from society), and (4) reformation/rehabilitation. Society's attitude decides the prison regime — punitive if retributive, therapeutic if reformative. Modern penology treats crime as a social disease, favouring non-penal methods (probation, parole, open jails).

2.2 The American prison system

  • Medieval colonies: barbarism — death, branding, whipping, pillory; prisons only for undertrials/political/religious offenders.
  • Penn's Charter of 1682 (printed “1862” in text — chronological context shows the Quaker reform charter): bail for prisoners; double compensation for wrongful imprisonment; choice of food/lodging; abolition of the pillory.
  • Quaker movement (1775): remodelled Philadelphia prison — classification into incorrigibles (solitary, no labour) and corrigibles (congregate rooms + day work); separate quarters for women/vagrants; principles: day-work + humanitarian treatment.

2.3 The British prison system

  • John Howard, The State of Prisons: damp, vermin-infected, filthy, corrupt prisons. Beccaria first raised voice against harsh treatment; papal cellular prison at Ghent; first English penitentiary Horsham (1776) — solitary cells + day hard labour.
  • Act of 1778: beginning of UK prison reform — day work + night cells + rehabilitation message; by 1833 frequent family visits + outside visitors' complaints.
  • “Ticket on leave” (conditional release); Act of 1877: transfer from municipal to national government — landmark.
  • Gladstone Committee (1894): abolish unproductive labour, group work, better classification, separate juvenile reformatories → Prison Act 1898, Children Act 1908.
  • Transportation to American then Australian colonies: (1) lifers to settle permanently; (2) probation-gangs as road/building labour (option to return or stay — most stayed to escape ex-convict stigma); (3) parole-pass → ticket on leave → conditional pardon. Abandoned: cost, harm to free settlers, womanless settlements, clashes.
  • Standard Minimum Rules: Sir Arthur Waller (Chairman, Prison Commission, England & Wales) proposed general rules at the International Penal & Penitentiary Congress (1925); first SMR drafted; accepted by the UN after WWII.
  • Sir Lionel Fox (Secretary 1925–34, Chairman 1942–60): public transparency (Prison Service Journal, 1960); reconcile deterrence–reformation — “deterrence lies not in severity but in certainty of detection and punishment”; open prisons 1→13 (3 for women); borstals 1→15; Hostel System, Bristol (1953) — preventive detenues/long-termers working in the city by day. Incorporated in regulations under the Criminal Justice Act, 1948. CJA 1982, s. 30: liberalised parole (partially suspended sentence; minimum 12 months or ⅓).
  • Modern British features: group-therapy classification; vocational training; reformation within community; after-care by voluntary bodies; minimum-security institutions recognising basic rights.

2.4 Russian prisons & the UN Congresses

  • Russia (Miesta Lischenja Svobadi — places of withdrawn freedom): open colonies; sentence never <1 year; liberal good-time (2 days' work = 3 days); wages (⅔ cash, ⅓ on release); education, recreation, Council of Culture (self-government); own clothes.
  • IPPC 1929 attempt at universal SMR failed (geographical/political variations); UN 1949 experts → First Congress (Geneva, 1955) adopted SMR: eliminate torture, narrow prison/free-life gap, humane treatment, rehabilitation. GA resolution: World Congress every 5 years: Geneva 1955, London 1960, Stockholm 1965, Kyoto 1970, Geneva 1975, Caracas 1980, Milan 1985, Havana 1990, Cairo 1995, Vienna 2000, Bangkok 2005 (renamed Crime Prevention & Criminal Justice), Salvador 2010.
  • 1975 review: no country claimed full compliance; 62 replied citing funds/staff/space. Alternatives discussed: reparation to victims, suspension of civil rights, compulsory industrial work (Colombia: externment; Norway/Sweden: open camps; Canada/England: family visits; Japan: parole as core of progressive treatment).

3. Prisons in India: Ancient → 1894 Act → Mulla DU CORE

Ancient — Brihaspati favoured closed prisons; Manu opposed; Kautilya (Arthashastra): fortresses as prisons, preferably road-side to reduce monotony; solitary confinement for self-introspection; spiritual/penance model.
Mughal — deterrent object: death, mutilation, flogging, branding, starvation; prisons as places of terror and torture.
1836 — First Prison Enquiry Committee: abolish road-work by prisoners; curb staff corruption.
1855 — First Inspector-General of Prisons appointed (chief administrator; discipline).
1862/1877/1889/1892 — 2nd, 3rd Jail Committees: insanitation deaths, food/clothing/medical care.
1894 — Prisons Act, 1894: uniformity; provincial rules; classification of prisoners; whipping abolished; improved medical/women amenities — but policy still deterrent.
1907+ — Juveniles segregated; reformatories & Borstal institutions on British pattern.
1919–20 — Indian Jail Reforms Committee (Sir Alexander Cardew): visited Burma, Japan, Philippines, Hong Kong, Britain; prisons must deter + reform; no corporal punishment; productive work; after-care; fixed intake capacity; Bombay abolished solitary cells, others followed.
1949 — Pakwasa Committee: prisoners as road labour without intensive supervision; wages for inmates introduced; good-time laws. Model Jail, Lucknow (handlooms); first women's jail, Yerwada (Maharashtra).
1950 — Constitution: “Jails” in State List (with police & law and order) — Centre has no direct responsibility; Five-Year Plans gave prisons lowest priority.
1951–52 — Dr. W.C./Walter Reckless (UN expert) invited; All India Jail Manual Committee (1957) + IGs' Conference — 6 unanimous guidelines: correctional services in Home Dept + Central Bureau of Correctional Services; probation/parole to decongest; State after-care units; abolish solitary confinement; classification; periodic Manual revision.
1980–83 — All India Jail Reforms Committee (Justice A.N. Mulla): report to Home Ministry 31-3-1983 — National Prison Commission; end Union–State dichotomy; total ban on clubbing juveniles with hardened criminals (Tihar inmate case — Sheela Barse v. UOI, AIR 1988 SC 2211; Agra Protective Home → JJ Act 1986→2000); mentally-ill to asylums; scientific classification (Ombudsman suggested); food/clothing/sanitation; trained cadres + All-India Prisons & Correctional Service; after-care/probation integral; media visits; minimum undertrial lodgment + separation + speedy trial + liberal bail; adequate funds.
1988 — National Expert Committee on Women Prisoners (Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer): more women police (cool/defuse/de-escalate), juvenile squads, humane agitation control, unarmed combat.
2005 — CrPC s. 436-A (now s. 479 BNSS): release on personal bond after ½ of maximum term undergone.

4. Problems of Prison Administration DU CORE

4.1 Overcrowding

  • Tihar 1995: 8,500 inmates vs 2,500 capacity. Baneful effects: no segregation (hardened influence others); juveniles mixed with professionals become professionals.
  • Law Commission, 78th Report (1979): liberalise bail, release categories of undertrials; plus fine, civil commitment, probation, parole, remission/leave/premature release (Mulla Ch. 20).

4.2 Prison discipline & riots

  • Taft: prisons = “unpleasant compulsory isolation” — rigid discipline, bare necessities, strict security, monotonous routine, untrained staff.
  • Riot causes: political instigation, crude discipline, monotony, family separation, staff differences, step-motherly treatment.

4.3 Prisoners' health — Prisons Act ss. 37, 39-A to 39-C; Anil Kumar v. State of M.P. (30-11-1999)

Entry-time health enquiry (TB, AIDS); segregation + cure duty. 7 TB-spread factors: (1) delayed diagnosis/poor services; (2) failure to refer/treat; (3) transfers of infectious cases; (4) overcrowding + prolonged cell confinement; (5) no segregation; (6) sub-standard treatment; (7) poor ventilation/nutrition. Remedy: trained inmate nursing volunteers + self-help.

4.4 Criminality inside prisons

  • Sexual deprivation → homosexuality, sodomy, assaults. Advanced countries allow conjugal visits; opponents: deprivation is inherent suffering; most terms ≤6 months; many unmarried; furlough/parole is more natural. India rejects conjugal visits — furlough/parole under the 1894 Act preferred; also morality/cultural grounds.
  • Fights (superiority tales, group rivalry, bread/toilets/warden disputes); petty thefts (soap, oil, utensils, bread); distrust of authorities (rough/corrupt staff → cold war).

4.5 Self-government in prisons

  • Osborne (USA): full inmate self-government — negligible escapes. India: partial — good-record prisoners attached to wardens as link; facilities + occasional outside movement → duty/loyalty + psychological incentive (sentence reduction hope).

4.6 Prison labour — State of Gujarat v. High Court of Gujarat, AIR 1998 SC 3164

  • Hague Conference (1950, XIIth): work = best alternative — fitness, self-confidence, family support from wages, remunerative to State; conditions at par with free workers; compensation for injuries.
  • SC: compulsory labour protected by Art. 23 (reformative policy); minimum wages after deducting maintenance; unpaid labour = slavery/begar; rates fair, equitable, standardised (not nominal); States may deduct food/clothes cost; States should legislate to divert part of wages as victim compensation. Mulla: work = rehabilitation, not additional punishment.

4.7 Prison community, classification & prison rules

  • Sutherland: newcomer learns prison culture like a child learns group ways — socialisation → “prisonisation”; informal groups; inmate “leaders” collude with staff (racketeering in food/mess/toilets) for unofficial privileges; administrators “enlist some inmates to control others.”
  • Classification — modern cardinal principle: individualisation; hardened → conventional jail; casual → medium-custody/borstal/probation. US model: Central Classification Centre (psychologists/psychiatrists) → institution Classification Committee (treatment plan) → Reception Centre (month-long orientation). Mulla (1980–83): segregation “only on paper” — recommended 5 institution types: (1) undertrial prisons/annexes; (2) women; (3) semi-open for juveniles; (4) maximum-security for professionals/gangsters; (5) camps for satyagrahi/political arrestees. Accepted in principle; funds lacking.
  • Prison Rules (State subject; 12 standard rules): prisoner register; no lodging without commitment order; segregation (male/female; undertrial/convict; civil/criminal; juveniles to Juvenile Homes); space/light/ventilation/sanitation; food/water/medical officer; exercise/entertainment; pre/post-natal care + babies with mothers; ban on handcuffs/chains/irons; supervised family meetings; education/library/vocational training; property protection; periodical inspection.

4.8 Rights of prisoners

  • Convict ≠ non-person: S.P. Anand v. State of M.P. (reasonable accommodation — M.P. Rules 22, 29, 30); State of Gujarat v. HC of Gujarat (wages); Procunier v. Martinez (US): curtailed liberty makes remaining liberty more substantial.
  • Labour classification: hard/medium/light; priority: (i) lifers with RI, (ii) other RI, (iii) willing SI prisoners, (iv) willing undertrials (equitable wages).

5. Undertrial Prisoners & Bail Jurisprudence DU CORE

5.1 The crisis

  • Thousands languish longer than their potential sentence; causes: court backlog, prolonged investigation, harsh bail system, no legal aid. Poor suffer most — cannot furnish sureties/bonds; family/morale/economic ruin. Krishna Iyer on grave consequences of pre-trial detention.
  • Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, Bihar, AIR 1979 SC 1360: incarceration beyond sentence-period = illegal, violates Art. 21; speedy trial is a constitutional mandate — State cannot plead financial/administrative inability. Result: 18,000 undertrials released in Bihar (1981); other States followed. Yet >1 lakh still languish; ss. 167/436–437 + legal aid + bail liberalisation insufficient.

5.2 Bail law & the “roots in community” test

  • CrPC: bailable (s. 436 → BNSS s. 484) vs non-bailable (court must be satisfied: attendance, no tampering, no fresh offence). Bail discriminates against the poor (Bhagwati Legal Aid Committee Report, Gujarat 1971).
  • 8 “roots” factors (Hussainara): length of residence; employment/finances; family ties; reputation/antecedents; prior record; responsible community vouchees; nature of offence/conviction likelihood; any other community-tie factor → release on personal bond.
  • 4 bail-denial grounds: grave offence; witness interference; repeat-offence risk; absconding risk. See also State of Rajasthan v. Balchand (1977); Gudikanti Narasimhulu (1978); Common Cause (1996).
  • Kalyan Chandra Sarkar v. Pappu Yadav: undertrial's Art. 21 not absolute — circumscribed by manuals/statutes; SC transferred accused holding jail “darbars” from Bihar to Maharashtra under Art. 142.

5.4 Handcuffing & solitary confinement

  • Prem Shankar Shukla v. Delhi Admn., AIR 1980 SC 1535: handcuffing “prima facie inhuman… zoological strategies repugnant to Art. 21”; extreme cases only + reasons + prior judicial approval. Sunil Batra/Sobraj (1978): violates Arts. 14/19/21; locomotion = facet of liberty; fetters only briefly with grounds disclosed.
  • Solitary confinement: Sunil Batra — keeping a “finally executable” death-sentence prisoner segregated is not solitary confinement (part of execution procedure); parameters in Kishore Singh v. Rajasthan (1981).

6. Paranjape's 14 Reform Suggestions + Rama Murthy Link DU CORE

Mohammad Giasuddin v. State of A.P., AIR 1977 SC 1926 — “the Gandhian diagnosis of offenders as patients… prisons as hospitals… Criminality is a curable deviance. Our prisons should be correctional houses, not cruel iron arching the soul.”
  1. Minimum incarceration: prison = costly burden; eliminate long terms; remission ~1 month/year for >1-year terms; periodical furlough for home visits.
  2. Women prisoners: generous treatment, frequent child meetings, liberal correctional/educational programmes, sympathy for sex-offence victims + illegitimate children; handled only by women officials; separate women jails too costly (but post-R.D. Upadhyaya 12 States + Delhi built them — AP 320, TN 513, Delhi 400, Maharashtra 262, Rajasthan 200, Punjab 150, WB 100, Bihar 83, UP 70, Kerala 60, Orissa 55, Tripura 30). R.D. Upadhyaya v. A.P., AIR 2006 SC 1946: children in jail = rights to food/shelter/medical/clothing/education/recreation; birth place ≠ “prison”; no child >6 years with mother; manuals to be amended. (6496 undertrial + 1873 convicted women with 1053 + 206 children as on 23-1-2002 — WARLAW petition.)
  3. Segregation: undertrials/minors/recidivists/first offenders/political (non-violent) separate; no young boys with sex-starved/hardened inmates.
  4. Scientific classification: crime, age, sex, character, propensities, education, treatment-response (Sunil Batra).
  5. Peasant prisoners: harvest-season “ticket on leave” for fields → family unity + rehabilitation.
  6. Private family meetings: guarded meetings embarrassing; communication/meeting rights minimally restricted (Mir Mehraj-ud-din: visits → post-release acceptance).
  7. Festivals: celebrate with rejoicings/programmes, not just special dishes (e.g., MP half-day leave for 30-3-2011 World Cup semi-final).
  8. Liberalise mail scrutiny → trust.
  9. Compensation remedy for wrongful detention/negligent injuries (Sanjay Suri, 1988; Bhuvan Mohan Patnaik).
  10. Education beyond 3 R's: vocational training, IGNOU/correspondence, tailoring/doll-making/embroidery for women; educated prisoners → mental-cum-manual work; yoga (Gujarat/Maharashtra lead).
  11. Liberal good-time laws + honour system; oppose festival mass-releases as political expediency (Rajendra Prasad v. U.P. — Gandhi Jayanti releasee murdered again).
  12. Intensive after-care: Seva-Sadans, Nari-Niketans, Reformation Homes, open-air camps, Prisoners' Aid Societies.
  13. Public attitude: media/press visits, publicity (Prabha Dutta v. UOI, (1982) 1 SCC 1 — interview of condemned Ranga-Billa).
  14. Replace the Prisons Act, 1894 — obsolete, redundant (NHRC endorsed; → Model Prisons Act 2023).

7.2 Yoga, Vipassana & Bhajans

  • Justice Ram Pal Singh (MP HC): body = temple of Atma/Parmatma; yoga-sadhna → healthy, crime-free society. Gujarat, Maharashtra, MP made yoga + discourses compulsory.

  • Sheela Barse v. Maharashtra directives: (1) women guarded by female guards/constables, separate wards; (2) interrogation in presence of women officials; (3) immediate intimation to relatives; (4) immediate information to nearest Legal Aid Committee.

8. Judicial Mandates: 18 Prisoners' Rights + 15 Admin Directives DU CORE

8.1 Eighteen mandates on rights of prisoners & detenues (Paranjape's compilation)
  1. No additional punishment by administrators beyond the court's sentence.
  2. Death-sentence prisoner in separate cell only after sentence becomes executable — and then within sight/sound/company of others (Sunil Batra-I).
  3. Death-row amenities: games, newspapers, movement, meetings — subject to reasonable regulation.
  4. Solitary confinement only in extreme necessity made out by authorities (Kishore Singh).
  5. Loneliness for prayer/remorse/family meetings — liberally granted.
  6. Undertrials: more relaxed conditions than convicts (custody ≠ sentence).
  7. No handcuffs in transit except extreme cases + recorded reasons (Prem Shankar).
  8. Hard labour ≠ harsh labour — congenial jobs assignable (Sunil Batra-II).
  9. Right to society of family/fellowmen under Art. 19, subject to search/discipline/security.
  10. Detenue–legal adviser interviews (COFEPOSA: official may watch but beyond hearing distance — Francis Coralie).
  11. Accused may sit during long trials except for identification.
  12. Undertrials: no leg-irons (Kadra Pahadiya); no outside-wall labour (ILO forced-labour conventions).
  13. Periodic parole as prisoner–family link, with safeguards (Hiralal Mallick).
  14. Only deprivations necessitated by incarceration/sentence; all else retained: read/write, exercise, meditation, climate protection, dignity (no nudity/forced sodomy), intra-campus movement, self-expression, skills (Sunil Batra-II).
  15. Press may interview willing death-row prisoners absent weighty contrary reasons (Prabha Dutta).
  16. Before execution, Superintendent must verify co-accused's commutation and escalate (Harbans Singh).
  17. Commutation cannot be demanded as of right.
  18. Right to legal assistance inside jail — undertrial or convict (Sheela Barse).
8.2 Fifteen directives for prison administration (Sunil Batra-II lineage)
  1. Lawyers nominated by DM/Sessions Judge/HC/SC: interviews, visits, confidential communication; periodical reports to courts.
  2. DMs & Sessions Judges to visit prisons (personally/by surrogate), hear grievances, enquire, remedy; habeas reference to HC where needed.
  3. Grievance Deposit Boxes under DM/Sessions Judge; frequent opening; access to all.
  4. Prisoner's Handbook in Hindi/regional languages; jail bulletins; prisoners' wall-paper (s. 61, Prisons Act).
  5. Writ + contempt shield; free legal services (Free Legal Aid SC Society; District Bar prisoner-relief cell; comprehensive State programmes).
  6. Notice boards of rights/duties in local language at prominent places.
  7. No penal transfer without Sessions Judge's judicial appraisal; emergency transfers intimated within 2 days; no punitive cells/privilege withdrawal.
  8. Scrap status-based classification; scientific classification (crime, behaviour, character, age, sex, education, response) — Sunil Batra-I.
  9. Segregate undertrials/minors/recidivists/first offenders/non-violent politicals — Vijay Kumar v. PP.
  10. Comply with UN Standard Minimum Rules (work/wages, dignity, community contact, correctional strategies).
  11. Overhaul Prison Act & Manual; correctional-orientation course for staff (constitutional values, therapy, tension-free management).
  12. Inmates' petitions to officials → direct to courts, not via hierarchy.
  13. Natural justice before liberty-affecting steps (solitary, fetters); appeal to judicial organ — Kishore Singh.
  14. All States to convert rulings into rules/instructions forthwith.
  15. Nathu v. Rajasthan, AIR 2007 SC 1: jail SLPs/appeals need immediate attention (suspension/bail); Registry not to mechanically call records (SC Rules r. 8 read with FRs).

9. Rehabilitation, After-care & Repatriation DU CORE

9.1 Eight obstacles to rehabilitative prisons (Chaturvedi)

  1. No infrastructure/scientific classification; (2) don't aggravate inherent suffering; (3) zero custodial torture; (4) vocational training for poor/illiterate/unskilled majority; (5) selective non-custodial dispositions (furlough/parole/remission/pardon); (6) education/vocational emphasis (IGNOU material at State cost); (7) semi-institutions (half-way houses, day centres); (8) Sunil Batra triad: custody ≠ non-person + human rights within limits + no aggravated suffering.

9.2 After-care history

  • 1877 Indian Jail Conference: first recognised need. Discharged Prisoners' Aid Societies: UP 1894, Bengal 1907, Bombay 1914 (failed — no Government/public support).
  • Post-independence: Gore Committee → Central Social Welfare Board + after-care Homes (1955–60); juvenile correctional institutions.
  • Released prisoners: shunned by society and family; shattered homes; lost jobs — need Government + NGO + society + Probation Officer supervision + welfare-officer counselling/legal/financial help. After-care ≠ charity — preparation for “the unkind world outside.”

9.3 The Repatriation of Prisoners Act, 2003 (w.e.f. 1-1-2004)

  • Object: transfer of sentenced prisoners India ↔ foreign country under bilateral arrangements (rising international crime; Indians offending abroad, aliens in India).
  • Mechanism: Central Government notifies arrangement countries; custody transfer by notification; prisoner's record transferred (s. 10); every repatriation order laid before both Houses of Parliament (s. 16(2)). Real example: operated via treaties with UK, France, etc. for transfer to serve sentences near home.

Petitioner (epistolary): wages denied; inedible food; torture — systemic neglect of 1155 prisons violating Art. 21; invoked Sunil Batra/Charles Sobraj “beacon lights.”

  1. Maharashtra v. Prabhakar (1966) — Art. 21 to prisoners (read/write books).
  2. Suresh Chandra; Krishan Lal (1976) — parole to check recidivism.
  3. Bhuvan Mohan Patnaik v. A.P. (1974) — no oppressive quarantine of Naxalites; but no FR to escape (live-wire walls OK).
  4. Charles Sobraj (1978) — Court intervenes when constitutional/statutory prescriptions transgressed (incarceratory torture).
  5. Sunil Batra-I (1978); Kishore Singh (1981) — solitary confinement parameters.
  6. Prem Shankar (1980) — no leg-irons for undertrials.
  7. Sunil Batra-II (1980) — prison vices vs Art. 21; “prisons are built with the stones of law.”
  8. Francis Coralie (1981) — one-interview-a-month rule violative of Art. 21.

10.5 The NINE problems & what the Court said on each

(1) Overcrowding (paras 15–24)

Chapman (1981 — no 8th-Amendment violation; “Constitution does not mandate comfortable prisons”); India: Art. 21 bars cruel punishment (Deena v. UOI, 1983). Held: even if not constitutionally impermissible, overcrowding damages health/hygiene → must be tackled. Remedies: bail-release of UTP bulk; Law Commission 78th Report Ch. 9 (decision within 6 months — para 20A; Babu Singh; Gurbaksh Sibbia — harsh bail conditions violate Art. 21); alternatives: fine, civil commitment, probation (Orland); parole (Orland Ch. 11; Suresh Chandra/Krishan Lal); Mulla Ch. 20 (remission/leave/premature release — decision in 6 months; British White Paper 1990 Ch. 6; Ananthachari on “cold storage”); segregation failure (hardened↔minor; juveniles spoiled).
(2) Delay in trial (paras 25–28)
Not causes but hardship-relief: implement UTP bail judgments fully. Non-production on remand dates (escort shortage) violates statutory duty + hides ill-treatment → consider entrusting production to prison staff (with escort vehicles) — decision in 6 months.
(3) Torture & ill-treatment (paras 29–34)
Cellular Jail horrors; fetters/iron bars under Manual colour — limits already spelt out, not repeated. Demand: new model All-India Jail Manual + replace the century-old Prisons Act, 1894 (NHRC paras 4.18/4.21; Datir — flogging retained in some Manuals, Art. 21-barred). US: Hudson v. McMillian (7:2 — excessive force = cruel & unusual even without serious injury). Discipline-punishment distinct from torture.
(4) Neglect of health & hygiene (paras 35–36)
Mulla Chs. 6–7 (pathetic), recs in Ch. 29 — decide in 6 months. Society's double-reason duty: prisoners lack free citizens' medical access (no choice/second opinion) and face greater hazards — double handicap. US debate (pp. 411–13) for policy-makers.
(5) Insubstantial food & inadequate clothing (para 37)
Manuals not complied → inspections at shortest notice; Tihar-style complaint box in all jails; fair inquiry + action; assurance of no reprisal for complainants.
(6) Prison vices (paras 38–39)
Sunil Batra-II suffices; vices rooted in snapped marital life/sexual frustration → keep family thread unbroken (visits — infra); sympathetic + disciplinary mix.
(7) Deficiency in communication (para 40)
Snapped outside contact = added trauma → liberalised kith-and-kin communication via model Manual; no plausible reason to deny easy postal facility.
(8) Streamlining jail visits (paras 41–44)
3 visit categories: relatives/friends (spouse special — majority inmates aged 18–34, married, conjugal denial = emotional harm); professionals (lawyer — Art. 22(1) for pre-trial detainees); lay persons. Problems: geographic isolation, degrading facilities, no privacy, screening emphasises separation. Mir Mehraj-ud-din (Ch. VI — visits → family acceptance + psychological communion).
(9) Management of open-air prisons (paras 45–48)
B. Chandra, Open Air Prisons: most successful individualisation for social readjustment (probation, home leave, wages, parole, educational/moral/vocational training); far less costly; employs otherwise-unproductive jail population; camps pay for themselves (Ch. 3, p. 150); human/social transformation via agriculture/horticulture, games, discipline, prayers (Ch. 5). Vipassana in Tihar (1993–94) — Tarsem Kumar, Freedom Behind Bars. Problems = mere management, all sortable → open more open-air prisons, starting at every District HQ.

Citation
W.P. (C) No. 1404 of 2023, decided 3-10-2024. Bench: D.Y. Chandrachud, CJI; J.B. Pardiwala & Manoj Misra JJ. Petitioner: journalist Sukanya Shantha (Art. 32).

14. Open Prisons DU CORE

14.1 Definition & philosophy

  • UN Congress, Geneva 1955: “An open institution is characterised by the absence of material and physical precautions against escape (walls, locks, bars, armed guards) and by a system based on self-discipline and innate responsibility towards the group.” = minimum-security device; in India called open jails (also: open-air camps, parole-camps).
  • Sir Lionel Fox: “of all methods… the open prison appears the most effective” for self-respect, self-responsibility, rational life.
  • Dr. C.P. Tandon (IG Prisons, UP, 1959): (a) freedom from physical precautions + (b) regime of self-discipline/group responsibility → self-respect, preparation for freedom; discipline easier, punishment rare, tensions relaxed, life near-normal.
  • 4 founding assumptions (Patterson, UK): (1) sent as punishment, not for punishment; (2) no training for freedom without relaxed restraints; (3) minimise institutional/free-life gap; (4) trust begets trust. → led to UK hostel system; India followed.
  • B. Chandra (cited in Rama Murthy): individualisation for social readjustment; wage system, parole, home leave, training; cheaper than closed prisons; camps pay for themselves.

14.2 Origin & world experience

  • Origin: Witzwil, Switzerland (late 19th c., semi-open); modern: UK 1930s (Patterson, Prison Commission 1922–27), USA 1940s.
  • USA: 19th-c. prison-farms = “slave-camps” under heavy guard — yet encouraging; open-air adopted as correctional integral (negligible escapes; self-reliance via minimum security); decongestion motive in wars. California: 1935 legislation (humane treatment; hopeful/hardened segregation) → Chino farm; 1938 San Quentin riot → new Board; Kenyon Scudder (Chino, opened 10-7-1941, 34 convicts → 2,500; escapes 1–4%): “no regeneration except in freedom; rehabilitation from within, not coercion.” ACA Declaration 1960 (5 principles): no offender deprived of hope of return; education/vocation opportunities; employable offenders must get productive work; group approach; offender as correctional-community member. Philosophy: no physical restraints; freedom without abuse; entry at start or mid-sentence; employment-oriented; selective intake; near-normal life + outside contact. E.g., Seagoville (Texas), Wallkill (NY) = “day-parole”/work-release between imprisonment and probation; usable for initial prisoners on parole-authority recommendation.
  • International: UN Social Defence literature → global adoption; Geneva 1955: minimum security + voluntary discipline; London 1960: integral to Anglo-American system; family visits, women/maternity facilities. Prisonisation harms many → open/semi-open, half-way houses, work-release = viable mid-way between incarceration and freedom.

14.3 Open-air camps in India — history

Ancient — Manu: unjust/harsh punishment makes criminals more dangerous + disreputes administrators; self-realisation surroundings; after-care rehabilitates even the most hardened.
1836 — First All-India Jail Committee: against major public-works employment → disuse for 20 years.
1864/1877 — Second Committee; 1877 Prison Conference: outside labour on canals/dams = “valuable, necessary adjunct” → revived.
1919–20 — Cardew: humane treatment; “most critical moment = coming out”; open-air labour not averse to reformation; building construction suitable; agriculturist-farm idea dropped (guarding over wide area).
1920–27 — Provincial committees: only cottage-industry expansion.
1949 — UP: first open-air camp at Model Prison, Lucknow (Sampurnanand era).
1952 — Walter Reckless (UN expert) report → All-India Jail Committee 1956–57 (3 years): open jails = self-discipline + self-help, no escape precautions.

14.4 Thirteen characteristics + six advantages (Paranjape)

Characteristics: (1) small-group informal living, minimum custody; (2) social-responsibility consciousness; (3) agricultural/allied training; (4) family/friend access for domestic solutions; (5) liberal remission — 15 days/month; (6) health/recreation; (7) qualified trained staff; (8) improved (+special) diet; (9) wages — part to inmate, part to family; (10) liberal bank loans; (11) free staff–inmate contact; (12) supervised paid work = best reformation; (13) no unduly long detention.
Advantages: (1) decongest; (2) lower construction cost; (3) lower operational cost; (4) productive work → fitness; (5) self-improvement/resocialisation; (6) resource conservation + wider rehabilitation. Based on the twin system of probation + parole. Two fundamental differences from closed prisons: (i) no maximum-security hardware; (ii) greater outside-world contact → responsibility.

14.7 Judicial seal + critical appreciation

  • Dharmbir v. U.P., (1979) 3 SCC 645: open prisons protect young offenders from conventional-jail vices → two early-20s accused sent to UP open prison bypassing technicalities.
  • Rama Murthy (1997): individualisation + finance + agriculture/games/prayers → open prisons at every District HQ.
  • Decline: no significant additions after 1980; women-only jail idea unsupported; probation's rise diverts attention. Criticisms: “taming places of miscreants” at public cost; inmates flout rules/dictate terms; staff abdicates to “criminal chiefs.” s. 433-A CrPC (14-year minimum for lifers) negates remission incentives — defeats open-prison purpose. Mulla: open + semi-open kinds; criterion = reformability, not sentence length.
  • Verdict: “help, not hate”; long incarceration futile; State apathy → overcrowding → disease/disorder.

14.8 The Vibhute reading (prescribed) — key takeaways DU CORE

  • Thesis: 55-year review (Max Planck, 2006/2015): open peno-correctional institutions = viable alternative to imprisonment; vocational work → skills, self-confidence, anti-idleness; superiority over closed “lock psychosis.”
  • Conceptual ambiguity: no uniform definition — Maharashtra/TN manuals = any place notified u/s 3(1), 1894 Act; WB 1986 notification = “prison house not surrounded by walls/fencing”; WB Correctional Services Act, 1992, s. 3(4) = open correctional home without boundary wall for long-termers' social association + after-release rehabilitation; Assam = minimum-security nation-building work; Karnataka = greater freedom/responsibility.
  • History: 18th-c. outside labour (deterrent/inhuman) → condemned by 1836–38 Committee → revived 1877 → 1919–20 Committee sceptical → post-1947 paradigm shift (Reckless 1951–52 → 1957–59 Manual Committee → Central Bureau of Correctional Services 1961 → 1968 Study Group → Mulla 1980–83).
  • Transfer criteria (state-wise table): age bands (21–55 typical); ¼–⅓ served in closed prison; exclusions (habituals, escapees, sex/dacoity/NSA-NDPS-type offences, pending cases, women almost everywhere); selection by IG/committees (DIG, Superintendent, MO, welfare officer) — Vibhute notes opacity + reluctance to transfer → chronic under-utilisation (capacity ~25,776 vs ~3,786 housed, c. 2015).
  • Expectations: uniform law, transparent selection, staff contentment (Ch. 4 — staff as corrective instrumentality), post-release employment linkage, expansion to short-termers/women.

15. Executive Clemency, Good-Time Laws & Indeterminate Sentence DU CORE

15.1 Pardoning power — Arts. 72 & 161

  • Art. 72 (President): pardon, reprieve, respite, remission; suspend/remit/commute — (a) court-martial, (b) Union-executive-power offences, (c) death sentences. Art. 161 (Governor): same for State-power offences. Pardon = act of mercy wiping guilt (absolute/conditional — breach revives unexhausted sentence). Rationale: correct miscarriage; incentivise prison discipline; historic decongestion in wars; “better to free the guilty than jail the innocent.” Abuse risk: influence, pardon-seeking over reform.

Taft, CJ (Ex parte Grossman): clemency cures “undue harshness or evident mistake.” Roots: royal prerogative; Hammurabi's Code (~4000 years).

15.2 Commutation, reprieve, amnesty + Good-time laws

  • Commutation = lesser for longer punishment (guilt intact); reprieve/respite = temporary postponement (usually death) for further verification; amnesty = mass release restoring civil rights (US federal ex-prisoners with 1-year Army service; UK 1953 war-deserters).
  • Good-time laws (early 19th c.): reduction for good behaviour — discipline + meaningful custody; usually 1 month/year up to 6 months (Prison/Parole Board discretion); France 1846; all US States by 1860; merit good-time (exceptional behaviour) + industrial good-time (prison industries). India: reduction (prison authorities' discretion) ≠ commutation (executive Head's prerogative); granted routinely so significance diluted; misconduct forfeits; British-era, discipline-successful; honour system as substitute; provisions in Prison Act + State Manuals. Critique: excessive curtailments sever Judge's sentence from actual incarceration — restricts sentencing courts.

15.3 Indeterminate sentence

  • Meaning: Code fixes minimum + maximum; Prison/Parole Board releases after minimum on favourable response — flexibility + individualisation (Sanford Bates: “date of release must be flexible”). “Indefinite” misnomer (implies inordinately long). Hope > fear; future in inmate's hands. Origin: Spanish prisons 1835 (1,000–1,500 “companies” under inmate-commanders; ⅓ remission) → Bolivia + Prison Aid Societies; Marsongy (1846); France + probation 1859; Ireland; NY 1867; Brockway (1870); Elmira 1869 (16–30 yrs + first offenders; Board of Managers — Superintendent + 5 honorary Senate appointees; education; 14–24 months; monthly clergy/teacher-attested reports); Burgess (Illinois, 1928); US Congress 1958 (parole after ⅓ of maximum).
  • Critique (1960s–70s): rehabilitative sentencing? procedure → miscarriage margins. Sutherland: selective (juveniles/first offenders); unsuited for serious/habitual/white-collar (deterrence dominates); legal maximum essential. P.K. Sen: maximum only, no minimum. India: not adopted — probation/parole/open camps serve the purpose; parole = modified indeterminate sentence; magistracy-discretion fears “misleading.”
  • Lord Clove (London 1925): (i) no prolonged terms for petty offenders; (ii) determinate only above 25 years (below = indeterminate unless habitual/serious); (iii) legal maximum, no minimum; (iv) qualified Boards.
  • 6 demerits: (1) uncertainty = psychological punishment (longer-but-definite preferred); (2) Board misjudgment → overstay; (3) no accurate fitness gauge → premature/overdue release; (4) warden-report dependence (flattery vs antagonism); (5) sycophancy over sincere reform; (6) no “debt-paid” self-satisfaction. Alternative: periodic judicial review (Tarde: courts decide guilt; expert committees decide treatment — but = judicial functions to non-judicial bodies).
  • Suspended sentence: guilt adjudged, no institutionalisation (vs indeterminate = uncertain term) → courts' humanitarian preference → evolution of parole & probation (parole rests on indeterminate; probation on suspended sentence). Trend: treatment decisions to experts/Boards (Scandinavia, US States, UK/Scotland). Mills (Britannica): punishment “determined by its adaptation to crime”; Hart: equals alike, unequals unalike (Ankush Shinde, 2009).

16. Parole, Probation & Furlough DU CORE

16.1 Concept & definitions

  • Rustomji (NPC tour-note): personality disintegrates behind bars; overcrowding + torture flout UN SMR. London 1960: latter part of sentence on parole/open institutions with families. Parole = most effective/popular; wider scope than open prisons; dual purpose: protect society + rehabilitate.
  • Military origin: prisoner-of-war release on promise to return. Gillin: release under correctional control to test fitness for unsupervised free life — last stage of correctional scheme (probation = first). Taft: post-part-sentence release, still in custody, returnable on violation. Sutherland: liberation revivable on condition-breach; after ⅓ served, before discharge. Cross: long-term release under continuous State custody. Poonam Lata v. Wadhawan, AIR 1987 SC 1383: partial liberty; status unchanged. Fixed terms >18 months → release on licence; act of grace, not right; provisional release deemed part of imprisonment; reformative process.
  • Parole vs indeterminate sentence: parole needs indeterminate sentence but ≠ identical — indeterminate = uncertainty (itself punishment); parole = pre-intimation of nearing discharge; parolee always starts with a definite term. Grant = quasi-judicial (Parole Board: abode + job + pre-parole orientation).

16.3 Origin & world systems

  • USA: indenturing → Prison Aid Societies → Federal role (1840s) → good-time = release from institution, not custody → Elmira 1869 → nationwide → Parole Reforms Act 1977 (uniformity). (Prisoners Rehabilitation Act: furlough/work-release — community job days, institution nights.)
  • Hungary: Educative Reformatory Work (Act 1950) — judge decides; parole 1 month–2 years; compulsory diminished-wage work; self-supporting; fails beyond 5-year terms; law = social-defence precondition.
  • Britain: transportation abolished → overcrowding → Ticket on Leave (late 18th c.) → failed (no training/after-care → recidivism) → conditional parole (early 19th c.). Categories: (a) >3-year serious (monthly police reporting); (b) habituals/preventive detention; (c) borstal/reformatory juveniles. Twin efficiency conditions: good-behaviour disposition + release as reward. Advisory Council 1973: State responsible for every inmate's after-care. Criticisms: unreformed backdoor exits; indeterminate darkness; secret Board policies (Forst); Hoover: mal-administration + ill-advised clemency harm community.

16.4 Parole in India — structure, conditions, judicial trend

  • Roots: political prisoners' protests + global reformative wave; no planned penal programme (probation haphazard via Social Welfare Boards — judicial function to non-judicial body questioned; parole fully law-conforming). Parole + open camps narrow prison/free-life gap.
  • Structure: Parole Board (respectable members; police opinion distrusted) — quasi-judicial scrutiny + case histories; field workers supervise + report violations. 3 agencies: Board + Case Investigators + Parole Supervisors. USA: psychologist/psychiatrist testing; India: simple in-prison hearing; only serious long-termers.
  • 6 success factors: (1) person-crimes > property-crimes (recidivism); (2) family liabilities = “good risks” (Taft); (3) first offenders > recidivists; (4) higher socio-economic/educational status; (5) waiver near discharge/distrust; (6) good-behaviour inclination via trained diagnosis. 16.5 Violation, 7 essentials & objects

  • Violation: arrest warrant → return without fresh trial → parole-violation hearing (self/counsel) → serve unexpired term; fresh crime → fresh trial, no second parole during new term. India: s. 48-A, 1894 Act — breach = prison offence. USA: voluntary return for training/jobs/treatment (unknown in India).
  • 7 essentials: (1) supervision + guidance (Sutherland & Cressey); (2) pre-release preparation via Classification Committees; (3) criterion = responsiveness, not category/sentence; (4) honourable employment + surroundings; (5) public cooperation; (6) apolitical, able, full-time Boards; (7) qualified trained whole-time staff.
  • Model Prison Manual's 3 objects: family continuity; save from continuous-prison evils; retain self-confidence/interest in life. Mulla: add furlough as of right after specified term (counts toward sentence). Parole = punishment (return-threat deters) + safety/treatment (upright-life opportunities). Workable alternative: executive Board assessment + judicial review (pure judicialisation risks shifting stands + kills reasoned discretion; pure executive invites political pressure — Forst: enhance rule of law).

18.1 DU 2022 — Q.5: NCRB 2019 data (4,03,739 capacity vs 4,78,600 inmates) + “To what extent have Rama Murthy recommendations been applied? Critically evaluate.”

⭐ Best model answer (15-mark structure)

Introduction — issue & frame: The question tests the implementation gap in prison reform: NCRB-2019 shows 118.4% occupancy with ~64% undertrials in inhuman conditions, two decades after Rama Murthy v. Karnataka, AIR 1997 SC 1739 laid down a comprehensive reform charter. The constitutional anchor is Art. 21 (dignity, health, speedy trial — Prabhakar; Sunil Batra; Hussainara), reinforced by ICESCR Art. 12 and UN SMR Rule 24(6) on prisoner health.

The Rama Murthy charter (brief): arising from a prisoner's letter (wages, inedible food, torture) + 300-page District Judge report, the Court diagnosed 9 problems — overcrowding, trial delay, torture, health/hygiene neglect, food/clothing, prison vices, communication gaps, unstreamlined visits, open-prison management — and issued 10 directions: Law Commission 78th Report action; Mulla Ch. 20 (remission/parole); UTP production by prison staff; new Prison Act + model All-India Jail Manual; Mulla Ch. 29 (medical); complaint boxes; liberalised communication; streamlined (conjugal) visits; open prisons at District HQs.

Upadhyaya; (5) open-jail network + parole jurisprudence (Dharamvir; Kesar Singh Guleria); (6) COVID High-Powered Committees (Re: Contagion, 2020 — interim bail/parole for ≤7-year sentences); (7) Model Prisons Act 2023 + Sukanya Shantha (2024, caste discrimination struck down) + transgender advisory (2022).

Conclusion: Rama Murthy supplied the diagnosis and the prescription; 1382 Prisons built the machinery; but without funds, staffing, and political will, reform remains — in the Court's fear — “promises to keep and miles to go.” The exam-ready closing line: “The skeleton is judicial, the flesh must be executive.”
