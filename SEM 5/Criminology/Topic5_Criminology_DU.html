1. Why Topic 5 matters, and how it is examined

Topic 5 is the most heavily weighted single topic in the paper — three sub-heads, eight prescribed readings, eighteen prescribed cases, and a share of every previous-year paper. It is also the topic where the examiner most often sets a problem question, because the material lends itself to it: “X has been convicted of Y; which theory, which section, which case?”

The single organising question
Every part of this topic answers one question: WHY does the State punish, and HOW MUCH? The theories (head i) answer “why”; the kinds of punishment and the sentencing provisions (head ii) answer “how much”; and probation (head iii) is the answer the law gives when it concludes that the right quantum is none at all. Frame every answer around that progression.
The three statutory pegs
s.354(3) CrPC → s.393(3) BNSS — “in the case of a sentence of death, the special reasons for such sentence”. s.235(2) CrPC → s.258(2) BNSS — the mandatory pre-sentence hearing. s.361 CrPC → s.402 BNSS — special reasons for not granting probation. Learn all three verbatim; between them they carry more marks than anything else in the topic.
The three doctrinal moments
1980 — Bachan Singh: the death penalty is constitutional, but “life imprisonment is the rule and death sentence an exception”, to be imposed only “in the rarest of rare cases when the alternative option is unquestionably foreclosed”. 1983 — Machhi Singh: the five categories and the two questions. 2008 — Swamy Shraddananda (2): the middle path — a sentence of life meaning the whole of natural life, or a fixed term beyond fourteen years without remission.
The three answer-winning moves
1. Quote the DU readings verbatim — Ashworth on the five rationales, and Kaul on the discretion in s.354(3). 2. Separate the crime from the criminal and show that Bachan Singh requires both — the error of Rajendra Prasad was to look only at the criminal, and of the trial courts to look only at the crime. 3. In every probation question, ask the three statutory questions first — is the offence punishable with death or life? Is the offender under 21? Has a Probation Officer’s report been called for? Those three questions decide most problems.

2. The concept of punishment — definition, elements and justification

2.1 Ashworth’s opening — reproduce this first

VERBATIM — Andrew Ashworth, “Sentencing”, DU Case Material p. 143 DU “Sentencing amounts to the use of STATE COERCION against a person for committing an offence. The sanction may take the form of some DEPRIVATION, RESTRICTION, or POSITIVE OBLIGATION. Deprivations and obligations are fairly widespread in social contexts — e.g. duties to pay taxes, to complete various forms, etc. But when imposed as a sentence, there is the ADDED ELEMENT OF CONDEMNATION, LABELLING, OR CENSURE of the offender. In view of the direct personal and indirect social effects this can have, IT CALLS FOR JUSTIFICATION.

Much writing about the [rationales] of sentencing has focused on one or more particular justification. In order to unravel punishment as a social institution, however, and to understand the tensions inherent in [any] given ‘system’, there is benefit in identifying the main thrusts of the several approaches. Among the issues to be considered are the BEHAVIOURAL and the POLITICAL PREMISES of each approach, its EMPIRICAL CLAIMS, and its PRACTICAL INFLUENCE.”
This passage gives you the entire analytical structure of head (i). Ashworth does not merely list the theories; he evaluates each on four axes — its behavioural premise (what it assumes about human nature), its political premise (what it assumes about the relation between the individual and the State), its empirical claims (does it work?), and its practical influence. An answer that applies those four axes to each theory will stand out immediately from one that recites definitions. The table at §7.2 does exactly that.

2.3 Why punishment needs justification at all

Punishment is the deliberate infliction by the State of harm on a citizen. Every other such act — assault, confinement, taking of property, taking of life — is itself an offence. What converts the identical conduct into a lawful act of State is nothing but its justification. That is Ashworth’s point: “in view of the direct personal and indirect social effects this can have, it calls for justification.” Three consequences follow, and they should be stated at the start of any essay:

  1. The burden is on the State. The person who would punish must justify; the person who resists needs no justification. This is the foundation of the abolitionist argument on capital punishment.
  2. Different justifications yield different quanta. Retribution says: as much as is deserved, and no more. Deterrence says: as much as is needed to deter. Rehabilitation says: as much as is needed to cure. Incapacitation says: as long as he is dangerous. The four can give radically different answers on identical facts, which is why Indian sentencing is so unpredictable (§33).
  3. The justification determines the offender’s rights. Only desert theory yields a right not to be punished disproportionately. Deterrence “seems to regard individual offenders as mere units in the overall calculation”; rehabilitation “has often failed to recognize any such general right, since it has been invoked in support of indeterminate sentences” (Ashworth, §7.1).

3. ASHWORTH: Desert or Retributive Theories

VERBATIM — Ashworth, DU Case Material p. 143 DU “Desert of [or] Retributive Theories
Retributive theories of punishment have a long history, including the writings of KANT and HEGEL. In their modern guise as the DESERT APPROACH, they came to prominence in the 1970s, to some extent propelled by the alleged excesses and failure of rehabilitative ideas (Bottoms and Preston 1980). Punishment is justified as the natural or appropriate response to crime, a fundamental intuitive claim, and its quantum should be PROPORTIONATE to the degree of wrongdoing. The justification for the institution of punishment also incorporates the consequentialist element of needing to deter crime: without the institution, ANARCHY might well ensue (for a variety of modern writings, see von Hirsch 1976, 1986, 1990a; Duff 1986). The BEHAVIOURAL PREMISE of desert is that individuals are RESPONSIBLE and predominantly RATIONAL DECISION-MAKERS. The POLITICAL PREMISE is that all individuals are entitled to EQUAL RESPECT AND DIGNITY: an offender does not forfeit all rights on conviction, and has a RIGHT NOT TO BE PUNISHED DISPROPORTIONATELY to the crime committed.

PROPORTIONALITY is the key concept in desert theory. CARDINAL PROPORTIONALITY requires that the overall level of the penalty scale should not be out of proportion to the gravity of the conduct: five years imprisonment for shoplifting would clearly breach that principle, but beyond such extreme cases there is much room for debate. Social conventions and cultural traditions tend to determine what levels of sanction are thought appropriate in a particular national or historical context (cf. Downes 1988 on the Netherlands and England; Graham 1990 on Germany). ORDINAL PROPORTIONALITY concerns the RANKING of the relative seriousness of different offences. In practice, much depends here on the evaluation of conduct, especially by sentencers, and on social assumptions about traditional or ‘real’ crime (e.g. street crime) compared with new types of offence (e.g. city fraud, pollution). In theory, ordinal proportionality requires the creation of a scale of values which can be used to assess the gravity of each type of offence: other relevant factors, such as CULPABILITY, AGGRAVATION, and MITIGATION, must then be assimilated into the scale. This task, which is vital to any theory in which proportionality plays a part, is continuing (e.g. von Hirsch and Jareborg 1991; Ashworth 1992: ch. 4).”

3.1 Unpacking the retributive theory

The core idea
Punishment is justified backward-looking: because the offender deserves it, not because any good will come of it. Kant: “Judicial punishment can never be used merely as a means to promote some other good for the criminal himself or for civil society, but instead it must in all cases be imposed on him only on the ground that he has committed a crime”; and the famous island illustration — even if a civil society were to dissolve itself, “the last murderer lying in prison ought to be executed before the resolution was carried out”, so that everyone may “receive the desert of his deeds”. Hegel: punishment is the “annulment of the wrong”, and is the criminal’s right, because to treat him otherwise is to treat him as an animal rather than as a rational being.
The two proportionalities — learn the distinction
CARDINAL proportionality fixes the overall height of the penalty scale (is five years for shoplifting too much?). ORDINAL proportionality fixes the relative ranking of offences on the scale (should fraud rank above or below burglary?). Ashworth’s observation that ordinal proportionality depends on “social assumptions about traditional or ‘real’ crime (e.g. street crime) compared with new types of offence (e.g. city fraud, pollution)” is Topic 3 in a sentence — the reason India punishes theft more severely than a fatal industrial disaster.
The Indian expression
lex talionis — “an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth”, first found in the Code of Hammurabi; the Manusmriti’s danda; and in modern Indian law the principle of proportionality in sentencing, which Kaul’s reading calls “the principle of just desert that serves as the foundation of every criminal sentence that is justifiable” (§16). Judicially: Mahesh v. State of M.P. (1987) 3 SCC 80 — “to give a lesser punishment for the appellants would be to render the justicing system of this country suspect”; Ravji v. State of Rajasthan (1996) 2 SCC 175 (since held per incuriam); State of M.P. v. Bablu Natt (2009) 2 SCC 272.
The criticisms — give these
(1) It offers no social benefit: as Bishnu Deo Shaw puts it, “it is inadequate as a theory since it does not attempt to justify punishment by any beneficial results either to the society or to the persons punished”. (2) It is vengeance dressed up — Stephen’s admission that “the criminal law stands to the passion of revenge in much the same relation as marriage to the sexual appetite”. (3) Measurement is impossible: as Bachan Singh asks, “how is the degree of that culpability to be measured? Can any thermometer be devised to measure its degree?” (4) It assumes a free and equal moral agent, whereas the offender is usually a person of disadvantage. (5) The DU solved paper’s own criticism: “it has been criticised by the utilitarians on the ground that it has no social utility. It is contended that if an individual does not have the right to exact retribution, how can the state acquire such a right?” PYQ
The crucial point that candidates always miss — retribution LIMITS punishment. Bishnu Deo Shaw makes it expressly: “it is necessary to clear a common misunderstanding that the retributive theory justifies the death penalty… It does not mean returning of evil for evil but the righting of a wrong. It implies the imposition of a just but NO MORE THAN A JUST penalty and automatically rules out excessive punishment and, therefore, capital punishment.” A modern retributivist is quoted as saying that “capital punishment… is without foundation in a theory of just punishment. Indeed one could go further and assert that capital punishment is ANTITHETICAL to the purposes and principles of punitive sanctions in the law.” Ashworth makes the same point positively: “it may therefore be seen as a strength of desert theory that it LIMITS punishment to what is proportionate.” Retribution is the offender’s shield, not the State’s sword.

4. ASHWORTH: Deterrence Theories

VERBATIM — Ashworth, DU Case Material p. 144 DU “Deterrence theories regard THE PREVENTION OF FURTHER OFFENCES THROUGH A DETERRENT STRATEGY as the rationale of punishing. As an exercise of state power, sentencing can be justified ONLY BY ITS CONSEQUENCES. The quantum of the sentence depends on the type of deterrent theory.

There is little modern literature on INDIVIDUAL DETERRENCE, which sees the deterrence of further offences by the particular offender as the measure of punishment. A first offender may require little or no punishment. A recidivist might be thought to require an escalation of penalties. THE SERIOUSNESS OF THE OFFENCE BECOMES LESS IMPORTANT THAN THE PREVENTION OF REPETITION. Traces of this approach can certainly be detected in modern sentencing practice, and some critics of desert theory claim that in taking (limited) account of prior record it incorporates a covert deterrent element (for discussion see Ashworth 1992a: ch. 6.2).

More attention has been devoted to GENERAL DETERRENCE, which involves calculating the penalty on the basis of what might be expected to DETER OTHERS from committing a similar offence. Major utilitarian writers such as BENTHAM (1789; and cf. Walker 1991) and economic theorists such as POSNER (1985) develop the notion of setting penalties at levels sufficient to OUTWEIGH THE LIKELY BENEFIT of offending. The BEHAVIOURAL PREMISE is that of responsible and predominantly rational, calculating individuals. The POLITICAL PREMISE is that THE GREATEST GOOD OF THE GREATEST NUMBER represents the supreme value, and that THE INDIVIDUAL COUNTS ONLY FOR ONE: IT MAY THEREFORE BE JUSTIFIABLE TO PUNISH ONE PERSON SEVERELY IN ORDER TO DETER OTHERS EFFECTIVELY.

Satisfactory empirical evidence of the effect of deterrent sentencing on individual behaviour is DIFFICULT TO OBTAIN. The conditions must be such that non-offending can safely be ascribed to the deterrent effect of the legal penalty rather than to any of the other myriad influences on people’s conduct, such as THE PERCEIVED RISK OF DETECTION, the opinions of significant others, etc. (Beyleveld 1980; cf. Walker 1991). Few research findings meet that criterion, and those that do provide support for general deterrent sentencing in ONLY A FEW TYPES OF SITUATION (see Zimring and Hawkins 1973; Beyleveld 1979; Riley 1985; Harding 1990).”

4.1 Unpacking the deterrent theory

Individual v. general deterrence — the distinction that carries marks
Individual (special) deterrence aims at this offender; the measure is whatever will stop him re-offending; hence escalation for recidivists and leniency for first offenders, and hence the seriousness of the offence becomes less important. General deterrence aims at everyone else; the measure is whatever will frighten the public; hence the exemplary sentence — and hence the moral problem, because the offender is used as a means to an end, which is precisely what Kant forbade.
The classical pedigree
Beccaria, Dei Delitti e delle Pene (1764): it is not the severity but the certainty and celerity of punishment that deters. Bentham: the felicific calculus — the penalty must be set so that “the evil of the punishment exceeds the advantage of the offence”. Posner and the economic analysts: the expected cost of crime (probability of detection × severity) must exceed the expected benefit. This is Topic 2, §5–§6, applied.
The famous illustration
The DU solved paper gives it: before the eighteenth century approximately 200 offences were punishable by death in England, including theft of a sheep, and the judicial formula was: “You are to be hanged not because you have stolen [a] sheep but in order that others may not steal sheep.” PYQ That sentence states general deterrence, and its own refutation, at once.
The criticisms — give these
(1) It treats the offender as a means. “The individual counts only for one” (Ashworth). (2) It has no upper limit: if hanging a pickpocket would deter pickpocketing, deterrence alone cannot object. (3) The evidence is weak: “few research findings meet that criterion”. (4) Certainty, not severity, deters — the Advisory Council on the Treatment of Offenders (1960), quoted in Bishnu Deo Shaw: “We were impressed by the argument that the greatest deterrent to crime is NOT THE FEAR OF PUNISHMENT, BUT THE CERTAINTY OF DETECTION.” (5) It cannot reach crimes of passion, which are unpremeditated, or crimes of the powerful, where detection is improbable.
The Indian judicial statementDalbir Singh v. State of Haryana (2000) 5 SCC 82 DU is the purest statement of deterrence in the prescribed cases: “When automobiles have become death traps any leniency shown to drivers who are found guilty of rash driving would be at the risk of further escalation of road accidents… One of the most effective ways of keeping such drivers under mental vigil is TO MAINTAIN A DETERRENT ELEMENT IN THE SENTENCING SPHERE. Any latitude shown to them in that sphere would tempt them to make driving frivolous and a frolic.” See §44.

5. ASHWORTH: Rehabilitative Sentencing

VERBATIM — Ashworth, DU Case Material pp. 144–145 DU “Sentencing aimed at THE REFORMATION OF THE OFFENDER’S CHARACTER has a lengthy history, being evident in the early days of PROBATION and of BORSTAL institutions. The rationale here is to prevent further offending by the individual through the strategy of REHABILITATION, which may involve individual case-work, therapy, counselling, intervention in the family, etc. Still a leading rationale in many European countries, it reached its zenith in the United States in the 1960s and then declined spectacularly in the 1970s. Research by MARTINSON was widely represented as demonstrating that TREATMENT PROGRAMMES USUALLY FAILED, swamping the more qualified judgment in an English survey by Brody and a subsequent clarification or retraction by Martinson himself (Martinson et al. 1974; Brody 1975; Martinson 1979). In terms of effectiveness, the true position is probably (as with deterrence) that CERTAIN REHABILITATIVE PROGRAMMES ARE LIKELY TO WORK FOR SOME TYPES OF OFFENDER IN SOME CIRCUMSTANCES. A humanitarian desire to provide help for those with obvious behavioural problems has ensured that various treatment programmes continue to be developed, and some authors have called for ‘the rehabilitation of rehabilitative theory’ (see Cullen and Gilbert 1982; Hudson 1987; and discussion by von Hirsch and Ashworth 1992a: ch. 1).

Its BEHAVIOURAL PREMISE is that some or many criminal offences are to a significant extent DETERMINED by social pressures, psychological difficulties, or other problems which impinge on individuals. THE LINKS WITH POSITIVIST CRIMINOLOGY ARE STRONG. The POLITICAL PREMISE is that offenders are seen as UNABLE TO COPE AND IN NEED OF HELP FROM EXPERTS, and therefore (perhaps) as LESS THAN FULLY RESPONSIBLE individuals. The rehabilitative approach indicates that sentences should be TAILORED TO THE NEEDS of the particular offender, and PLACES NO LIMITS ON THE EXTENT OF THE INTERVENTION. It lays emphasis on the processes of DIAGNOSIS AND TREATMENT by trained professionals. In practical terms the PRE-SENTENCE REPORT, formerly the SOCIAL INQUIRY REPORT, is an essential element in the pursuit of this approach.”

5.1 Unpacking the reformative theory

The core idea
Punishment is justified only if it looks to the future. As the DU solved paper puts it: “punishment is justified only if it looks to the future and not to the past… ‘it should not be regarded as settling an old account but rather [as] opening a new one’.” PYQ The offender is a product of “conflict between the character and motive of the criminal”, and “punishment is used as a measure to reclaim the offender and not to torture or hurt them”.
The link with positivism — say this
Ashworth: “the links with positivist criminology are strong.” If crime is determined by biological, psychological or social forces (Topic 2, §7–§18), the offender is not fully responsible, and the correct response is treatment, not blame. Hence the reformative theory imports the whole positivist apparatus: diagnosis, classification, individualised treatment and the indeterminate sentence.
The two fatal objections Ashworth identifies
(1) It “places NO LIMITS on the extent of the intervention.” If the sentence is a cure, it must continue until the patient is cured — which justifies the indeterminate sentence and can produce far longer detention than desert would allow. (2) It treats the offender as “less than fully responsible”, which is itself a denial of dignity. Hence Ashworth’s later observation that “rehabilitative theory has often failed to recognize any such general right [not to be punished disproportionately], since it has been invoked in support of indeterminate sentences.”
“Nothing works” and its retraction
Robert Martinson’s 1974 survey (“What works? — questions and answers about prison reform”) was read as showing that “with few and isolated exceptions, the rehabilitative efforts that have been reported so far have had no appreciable effect on recidivism”, and it demolished the rehabilitative ideal in the United States. Ashworth records two correctives: Brody’s more qualified English survey, and Martinson’s own subsequent retraction (1979). The modern position is the “what works” movement: certain programmes work for certain offenders in certain conditions. Quote Ashworth’s careful formulation.
The Indian statement of the reformative theory — quote both. Narotam Singh v. State of Punjab (1978) 4 SCC 505, cited in the DU solved paper PYQ: “Reformative approach to punishment should be the object of criminal law, in order to promote rehabilitation without offending community conscience, and to secure social justice.” And Mohammad Giasuddin v. State of A.P. (1977) 3 SCC 287, per Krishna Iyer J.: “the sub-culture that leads to anti-social behaviour has to be countered not by undue cruelty but by RE-CULTURALISATION”; and “crime is a pathological aberration… the therapeutic role of punishment… every saint has a past and every sinner a future.” Add Sunil Batra (II) v. 6. ASHWORTH: Incapacitative Sentencing

VERBATIM — Ashworth, DU Case Material pp. 145–146 DU “The INCAPACITATIVE approach is to identify offenders or groups of offenders who are likely to do such serious harm in the future that SPECIAL PROTECTIVE MEASURES should be taken against them. Programmes of SELECTIVE INCAPACITATION have focused on groups of recidivists (cf. Greenwood 1982; Blumstein et al. 1986), but in England the emphasis has been upon identifying individuals who are PREDICTED to be likely to commit serious harm in the future. The discretionary sentence of LIFE IMPRISONMENT has been used increasingly for this purpose, and the Criminal Justice Act 1991 authorizes ‘public protection’ sentences for violent and sexual offenders who are considered likely to do serious harm.

There is NO BEHAVIOURAL PREMISE for the incapacitative approach: it looks chiefly to THE PROTECTION OF POTENTIAL VICTIMS, and can apply whether the offender is a rational calculator or is driven by pressures. The POLITICAL PREMISE is often presented as UTILITARIAN, justifying incapacitation by reference to the greater aggregate benefit, but even a sentencing rationale which emphasizes the continuing rights of a person who has offended must deal with the possibility that those may CONFLICT WITH THE RIGHTS OF A (POTENTIAL) FUTURE VICTIM. Some of these issues were examined in the Floud report, which also found that PREDICTIONS OF ‘DANGEROUSNESS’ TENDED TO BE WRONG MORE OFTEN THAN NOT (Floud and Young 1981; for different analyses cf. Bottoms and Brownsword 1982; Wood 1988). This repeatedly confirmed FALLIBILITY OF PREDICTIVE JUDGMENTS (see also Brody and Tarling 1981; Monahan 1981) calls into question the justification for any lengthening of sentences on grounds of public protection, and yet the political pressure to have some form(s) of incapacitative sentence available to the courts has been felt in most countries. If this is the reality of penal politics, then there is surely a strong case for PROCEDURAL SAFEGUARDS to ensure that the predictive judgments are open to thorough challenge. The provisions of the Criminal Justice Act 1991 are inadequate in this respect (see further Ashworth 1992a: 163–7).”

6.1 Unpacking the preventive theory

The core idea — “disablement”
The DU solved paper states it precisely: the preventive theory “is based on the proposition that one must not avenge crime but PREVENT it, i.e. it aims to prevent the crime by disabling or incapacitating the criminal, either by death or imprisonment for life, etc. According to this theory, punishment of such nature is justified because of social necessities, i.e., by punishing the criminal, the community is able to protect itself against anti-social activities which may pose danger to life and property of the citizens.” PYQ As it is sometimes put: “the offender is disabled from repeating the offence by taking away his liberty, his limb, or his life.”
The distinguishing feature
Ashworth’s observation that “there is NO behavioural premise for the incapacitative approach” is important and examinable. Retribution assumes a responsible agent; deterrence assumes a rational calculator; rehabilitation assumes a determined patient. Incapacitation assumes nothing about the offender at all — it looks only at the potential victim. That is both its strength (it is immune to the objection that the offender is not responsible) and its danger (it dispenses with the offender as a moral subject altogether).
The fatal objection — prediction
The Floud Report (Floud and Young, 1981) found that predictions of “dangerousness” “tended to be wrong MORE OFTEN THAN NOT” — the problem of false positives. To incapacitate all who are predicted dangerous is to imprison a majority who would not in fact have offended. Ashworth’s conclusion is the key sentence: the fallibility of prediction “calls into question the justification for ANY lengthening of sentences on grounds of public protection”, and, if political reality requires such sentences, there is “a strong case for PROCEDURAL SAFEGUARDS”.
The Indian expression
Preventive detention — Art. 22(3)–(7), the National Security Act, 1980, COFEPOSA, 1974, PITNDPS, 1988, the PBMSEC Act, 1980 and the State “goonda” Acts (§47); ss.126–143 BNSS (ss.106–124 CrPC) — security for keeping the peace and for good behaviour, including from habitual offenders (s.129 BNSS / s.110 CrPC); the State Habitual Offenders Acts; disqualification and debarment; cancellation of licences; and, at the extreme, the death sentence itself. The Supreme Court’s formulation of the fourth Bachan Singh mitigating factor — “the probability that the accused would not commit criminal acts of violence as would constitute a CONTINUING THREAT TO SOCIETY” — is incapacitation written into the death-penalty test; and Bariyar (2009) holds that the State must PROVE that the accused is beyond reform (§31).
Why Bishnu Deo Shaw dismisses prevention in one line. The judgment says DU: “The preventive theory is unimportant where the choice is between death and life imprisonment as in India.” The reasoning is unanswerable: a person serving imprisonment for life is already incapacitated so far as society is concerned; killing him adds nothing to public protection. Use this whenever a question asks whether the death penalty can be justified on preventive grounds.

7. ASHWORTH: Restorative, Reparative and Social Theories

VERBATIM — Ashworth, DU Case Material pp. 145–146 DU “Restorative and Reparative Theories
These are NOT THEORIES OF PUNISHMENT. Rather, their argument is that sentences should MOVE AWAY FROM PUNISHMENT of the offender TOWARDS RESTITUTION AND REPARATION, aimed at RESTORING THE HARM DONE and calculated accordingly. Restorative theories are therefore VICTIM-CENTRED (see e.g. Wright 1991), although in some versions they encompass the notion of reparation to the community for the effects of crime. They envisage less resort to custody, with onerous community-based sanctions requiring offenders to work in order to compensate victims, and also contemplating support and counselling for offenders to REINTEGRATE them into the community. Such theories therefore tend to act on a behavioural premise similar to rehabilitation, but their POLITICAL PREMISE is that COMPENSATION FOR VICTIMS should be recognized as MORE IMPORTANT THAN NOTIONS OF JUST PUNISHMENT ON BEHALF OF THE STATE.

Legal systems based on a restorative rationale are rare, but the increasing tendency to insert victim-orientated measures such as COMPENSATION ORDERS into sentencing systems structured to impose punishment provides a fine example of GARLAND’s observation that ‘institutions are the scenes of particular conflicts as well as being means to a variety of ends, so it is no surprise to find that each particular institution combines a number of often INCOMPATIBLE OBJECTIVES, and organizes the relations of often ANTAGONISTIC INTEREST GROUPS’ (1990: 282).

Social Theories
There has been a resurgence of writings which emphasize the social and political context of sentencing… Important in this respect are GARLAND’s analysis of the theoretical underpinnings of historical trends in punishment (Garland 1990) and HUDSON’s argument in favour of a shift towards a MORE SUPPORTIVE SOCIAL POLICY as the principal response to the problem of crime (Hudson 1987). Those who have been influenced by H.L.A. HART’s distinction between THE GENERAL JUSTIFYING AIM of punishment (in his view, utilitarian or deterrent) and THE PRINCIPLES OF DISTRIBUTION of punishment (in his view, retribution or desert) (Hart 1968, esp. ch. 1) should consider the challenge to this dichotomy in LACEY’s work. She argues that BOTH these issues raise questions of INDIVIDUAL AUTONOMY and of COLLECTIVE WELFARE and that, rather than denying it, we should address this conflict and strive to ensure that NEITHER VALUE IS SACRIFICED ENTIRELY at either stage (Lacey 1988). In developing this view she explores the political value involved in state punishment and argues for a clearer view of the social function of punishing.

The political philosophy underlying the work of BRAITHWAITE AND PETTIT is what they term REPUBLICANISM, at the heart of which lies the concept of DOMINION (Braithwaite and Pettit 1990; for a critique see von Hirsch and Ashworth 1992b). Its essence is LIBERTY, not in the sense of simple freedom from constraint by others, but more in the form of A STATUS OF GUARANTEED PROTECTION from certain kinds of interference. This leads them to propose that PUNISHMENTS SHOULD INCREASE THE DOMINION OF VICTIMS WITH THE LEAST LOSS OF DOMINION TO THE OFFENDERS PUNISHED. Since dominion lays emphasis on reassuring citizens about the prospect of liberty, it might require preventative sentences based on deterrence or incapacitation. They refer to unspecified upper limits on severity, but not lower limits, and their view is that the censuring function of the criminal justice system can and should so far as possible be fulfilled by MEANS OTHER THAN PUNISHMENT. There is thus NO RECOGNITION OF AN INDIVIDUAL’S RIGHT NOT TO BE PUNISHED MORE THAN IS PROPORTIONATE to the seriousness of the crime: all depends on what will ADVANCE OVERALL DOMINION, which might happen to be more or less in any individual case than the ‘deserved’ punishment.”
Why restorative justice is “not a theory of punishment”
Because it does not seek to justify punishment at all — it seeks to replace it. The question shifts from “what does the offender deserve?” to “what does the victim need, and how can the harm be repaired?” Hence the standard formulation: crime is not merely an offence against the State but a rupture of a relationship between offender, victim and community, and justice consists in repairing that rupture.
The Indian expression
s.357 CrPC → s.395 BNSS (compensation out of the fine and otherwise); s.357-A CrPC → s.396 BNSS (the Victim Compensation Scheme, mandatory in every State, payable even where the accused is acquitted or untraced); s.357-B and 357-C → ss.397–398 BNSS; s.359 BNSS (costs); the NALSA Compensation Scheme for Women Victims/Survivors of Sexual Assault, 2018; the definition of “victim” in s.2(y) BNSS (s.2(wa) CrPC) and the victim’s right of appeal under the proviso to s.413 BNSS (s.372 CrPC); plea bargaining under ss.289–300 BNSS, which requires “mutually satisfactory disposition” including compensation to the victim; and the Lok Adalat and mediation machinery. Judicially: Dr. Jacob George v. State of Kerala (1994) 3 SCC 430 DU (Topic 6), State of Punjab v. Ajaib Singh (1995) 2 SCC 486 DU, Chairman, Railway Board v. Chandrima Das (2000) 2 SCC 465 DU, and Ankush Shivaji Gaikwad v. State of Maharashtra (2013) 6 SCC 770 — the duty to apply the mind to compensation in every criminal case.
Hart’s two questions — the key analytical tool
General justifying aim: why have the institution of punishment at all? Hart’s answer: utilitarian — the prevention of crime. Principles of distribution: whom may we punish, and how much? Hart’s answer: retributive — only the guilty, and only in proportion. This “mixed theory” is the orthodox modern position and is the best single framework for an essay: it allows you to say that deterrence supplies the aim while desert supplies the limits. Then add Lacey’s challenge, as Ashworth reports it: both questions raise issues of individual autonomy and collective welfare, and “neither value [should be] sacrificed entirely at either stage”. (Note: this is the same Nicola Lacey whose work on criminalisation is prescribed for Topic 1.)
Braithwaite and Pettit — republicanism and dominion
“Dominion” is not bare freedom from interference but “a STATUS of guaranteed protection” — liberty that one can rely on and that others recognise. The prescription: maximise the dominion of victims with the least loss of dominion to offenders. Ashworth’s criticism is the one to reproduce: the theory recognises upper limits on severity but no lower limits, and therefore contains “no recognition of an individual’s right not to be punished more than is proportionate”. Braithwaite’s companion idea, reintegrative shaming (1989) — censure the act, but reintegrate the actor — is the theoretical foundation of restorative justice and of s.3(viii) and s.3(xiv) of the JJ Act (Topic 4).

8. ASHWORTH: Appraising the Rationales — and the composite table

VERBATIM — Ashworth, DU Case Material pp. 146–148 DU “Appraising the Rationales
In formal terms, the English sentencing system now contains an approximate HIERARCHY of rationales. According to the Criminal Justice Act 1991, DESERT IS THE PRIMARY RATIONALE, except in the relatively rare cases where the conditions for imposing an incapacitative sentence are met. DETERRENCE MAY NOT BE USED TO JUSTIFY A DISPROPORTIONATELY SEVERE SENTENCE. REHABILITATIVE considerations become important WHEN CHOOSING AMONG COMMUNITY ORDERS of a similar severity, and also serve as a justification for probation orders and supervision after early release from custody. COMPENSATION orders appear to have priority over fines and not over custody. This legal framework is hardly conflict-free, but it establishes a greater degree of certainty than existed in the previous ‘cafeteria-style’ system in which sentencers usually had wide choices of approach.

In practical and in theoretical terms, however, the position is less clear-cut. Since punishment is A SOCIAL INSTITUTION, this means that discussions ought to take account of its relation to other social institutions, and the relationship of sentencing to other aspects of the criminal justice system. It also means that, in practice, the form and content of punishment in a given society are likely to reflect A HISTORICAL MIXTURE OF POLITICAL AND SOCIAL EVENTS AND INFLUENCES.… Criminologists have also tended to evaluate the approaches in terms of their effectiveness, and doubts were expressed earlier about the efficacy of strategies of individual deterrence, general deterrence, rehabilitation, and incapacitation. In respect of the last two it may be possible to refine techniques for identifying suitable targets, but it is also important to note that the object of discussion is MARGINAL PREVENTATIVE EFFECTS. Thus a sentencing system based on desert is LIKELY TO DETER AND INCAPACITATE TO A CERTAIN DEGREE, and so the proponents of deterrent or incapacitative theories must seek to justify THE SEARCH FOR EXTRA INCREMENTS OF PREVENTION, by reference to evidence of likely success and the type of measure which must be adopted in order to achieve that success. IT IS THERE THAT CONCRETE EVIDENCE IS WANTING.…

Even if there were satisfactory evidence of efficacy, however, there remain issues about THE RIGHTS INVOLVED IN PUNISHMENT — notably, those of VICTIMS and of OFFENDERS. The victim’s right to receive compensation from the offender is surely undoubted, but in what circumstances should it give way to the offender’s interest in not being utterly impoverished for months or years to come? There is controversy about whether or not victims should be able to express an opinion on sentence to the court, but this surely overlooks the point that SENTENCING IS A STATE FUNCTION WHICH SHOULD BE DETERMINED BY PUBLIC POLICY RATHER THAN PRIVATE PREFERENCE.

What rights should be accorded to offenders? DETERRENCE THEORY SEEMS TO REGARD INDIVIDUAL OFFENDERS AS MERE UNITS IN THE OVERALL CALCULATION; INCAPACITATIVE THEORY may recognize a general right not to be punished more than is proportionate to the offence, BUT OVERRIDES THIS in situations which may be more or less well defined; and REHABILITATIVE THEORY HAS OFTEN FAILED TO RECOGNIZE ANY SUCH GENERAL RIGHT, since it has been invoked in support of INDETERMINATE SENTENCES. IT MAY THEREFORE BE SEEN AS A STRENGTH OF DESERT THEORY THAT IT LIMITS PUNISHMENT TO WHAT IS PROPORTIONATE, AND PROPOSES CRITERIA FOR DETERMINING PROPORTIONALITY (e.g. von Hirsch and Jareborg 1991). Similar limits on state power out of respect for the rights of the offender are proposed in the republicanism of Braithwaite and Pettit (1991) and the communitarianism of Lacey (1988), although neither book works out the detailed implications.

The choice of one or more rationales for punishment MAY NOT DICTATE THE LEVEL OF PUNITIVENESS in a given country, especially in view of the sparse evidence on deterrent and rehabilitative efficacy. It has sometimes been argued that DESERT THEORY LEADS TO HARSH PENALTIES, BUT THERE IS NO SUCH CONNECTION (cf. Hudson 1987; von Hirsch 1990b). CALIFORNIA has harsh penalties, whereas FINLAND AND SWEDEN have relatively low levels of punitiveness, and yet ALL REGARD DESERT AS THE PRIMARY RATIONALE. Much depends on THE POLITICAL CLIMATE AND ON THE ATTITUDES OF KEY ACTORS, but reductions in punitiveness may be better achieved through SPECIFIC PRINCIPLES OF RESTRAINT IN THE USE OF CUSTODY or even PRISON CAPACITY CONSTRAINTS than through the promotion of a general rationale.”
Three propositions from this passage that win marks. (1) The “marginal preventative effects” argument. A desert-based system already deters and incapacitates to some degree; so the deterrence theorist is not arguing for deterrence but for an extra increment of it — and must produce evidence that the extra severity buys the extra prevention. “It is there that concrete evidence is wanting.” This single argument disposes of most exemplary-sentence reasoning. (2) Sentencing is a State function, not a private preference. Ashworth’s answer to the victim-impact-statement debate — important in India, where public and media pressure drives sentencing in notorious cases. (3) Desert does not entail harshness. California, Finland and Sweden all follow desert, with utterly different levels of punitiveness; “much depends on the political climate and on the attitudes of key actors”. Use this against the common claim that “proportionality means severity”.

9. The four theories in Indian law

9.1 Bishnu Deo Shaw’s appraisal of the four theories, applied to capital punishment

VERBATIM — Bishnu Deo Shaw v. State of West Bengal, per Chinnappa Reddy J., paras 3–5 DU “3. Theories of punishment, there are many — reformative, preventive, retributive, denunciatory and deterrent. LET US EXAMINE WHICH CAP FITS CAPITAL PUNISHMENT. THE REFORMATIVE THEORY IS IRRELEVANT WHERE DEATH IS THE PUNISHMENT SINCE LIFE AND NOT DEATH CAN REFORM. THE PREVENTIVE THEORY IS UNIMPORTANT WHERE THE CHOICE IS BETWEEN DEATH AND LIFE IMPRISONMENT AS IN INDIA.

4. The retributive theory is INCONGRUOUS IN AN ERA OF ENLIGHTENMENT. It is INADEQUATE AS A THEORY SINCE IT DOES NOT ATTEMPT TO JUSTIFY PUNISHMENT BY ANY BENEFICIAL RESULTS either to the society or to the persons punished. It is, however, necessary to clear a COMMON MISUNDERSTANDING that the retributive theory justifies the death penalty. According to the retributivist, society has the right and the duty to vindicate the wrong done to it and it must impose a punishment which fits the crime. IT DOES NOT MEAN RETURNING OF EVIL FOR EVIL BUT THE RIGHTING OF A WRONG. IT IMPLIES THE IMPOSITION OF A JUST BUT NO MORE THAN A JUST PENALTY AND AUTOMATICALLY RULES OUT EXCESSIVE PUNISHMENT AND, THEREFORE, CAPITAL PUNISHMENT. According to a modern exponent of the retributive theory of justice, ‘capital punishment… is without foundation in a theory of just punishment. Indeed one could go further and assert that CAPITAL PUNISHMENT IS ANTITHETICAL TO THE PURPOSES AND PRINCIPLES OF PUNITIVE SANCTIONS IN THE LAW. Requital, when properly understood in terms of a concept of just law, undoubtedly does have a legitimate role in punishment. However, neither requital nor punishment in general is A RETURNING OF EVIL FOR EVIL, and, therefore, I see no support for the demand that a murder… be paid for with a life.’ The Biblical injunction ‘AN EYE FOR AN EYE AND A TOOTH FOR A TOOTH’ is often quoted as if it was a command to do retributive justice. IT WAS NOT. Jewish history shows that IT WAS MEANT TO BE MERCIFUL AND TO SET LIMITS TO HARSH PUNISHMENTS which were imposed earlier, including the death penalty for blasphemy, Sabbath breaking, adultery, false prophecy, cursing, striking a parent etc. And, as one abolitionist reminds us, who, one may ask, remembers the voice of the other Jew: ‘Whoever shall smite on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also?’

5. The DENUNCIATORY theory of punishment is only a different shade of the retributive theory but from a sternly moral plane. LORD DENNING advanced the view before the Royal Commission on Capital Punishment: ‘The punishment inflicted for grave crimes should adequately reflect the REVULSION felt by the great majority of citizens for them. It is a mistake to consider the objects of punishment as being deterrent or reformative or preventive and nothing else. THE ULTIMATE JUSTIFICATION OF ANY PUNISHMENT IS NOT THAT IT IS A DETERRENT BUT THAT IT IS THE EMPHATIC DENUNCIATION BY THE COMMUNITY OF A CRIME, and from this point of view there are some murders which in the present state of opinion demand the most emphatic denunciation of all, namely the death penalty’… ‘The truth is that some crimes are so outrageous that society insists on adequate punishment, because the wrong-doer deserves it, irrespective of whether it is a deterrent or not.’ The implication of this statement is that the death penalty is necessary NOT BECAUSE THE PRESERVATION OF SOCIETY REQUIRES IT BUT BECAUSE SOCIETY DEMANDS IT. Despite the high moral tone and phrase, the denunciatory theory, as propounded, is nothing but an echo of the retributive theory as explained by STEPHEN, who had said earlier: ‘THE CRIMINAL LAW STANDS TO THE PASSION OF REVENGE IN MUCH THE SAME RELATION AS MARRIAGE TO THE SEXUAL APPETITE.’ The denunciatory theory is as inadequate as the retributive theory since it does not justify punishment by its results. As Prof. HART points out, the idea that we may punish offenders not to prevent harm or suffering or even the repetition of the offence but simply as a means of emphatically expressing our condemnation, is UNCOMFORTABLY CLOSE TO HUMAN SACRIFICE as an expression of righteousness. And the question remains: ‘WHY SHOULD DENUNCIATION TAKE THE FORM OF PUNISHMENT?’”
This passage is a complete answer to “Discuss the theories of punishment with special reference to capital punishment”. It disposes of all five theories in five paragraphs: reformation is irrelevant because “life and not death can reform”; prevention is unimportant because life imprisonment already incapacitates; retribution, properly understood, excludes the death penalty because it implies “a just but no more than a just penalty”; denunciation is retribution in moral dress and comes “uncomfortably close to human sacrifice”; and deterrence — which the judgment then examines at length (§21) — is unsupported by the evidence. Learn the five one-line dismissals and the Denning–Stephen–Hart exchange.

9.2 The two additional theories you should name

The EXPIATORY theory
Rooted in Indian and other ancient jurisprudence (the Manusmriti; prayaschitta): the offender, by suffering and repentance, expiates or washes away the guilt and is restored to the community. The DU solved paper records Oppenheimer’s version: “retaliation or retribution fulfil a religious mission of punishing the offender; it re-establishes the social harmony affected by the offence and the offender’s guilt is washed away through suffering” PYQ. Its modern descendant is restorative justice. Its weakness: repentance cannot be compelled, and the theory has no answer to the unrepentant offender.
The DENUNCIATORY / EXPRESSIVE theory
Punishment as “the emphatic denunciation by the community of a crime” (Lord Denning, quoted in Bishnu Deo Shaw). Modern form: the communicative theory of Antony Duff — punishment as a form of moral dialogue addressed to the offender, inviting repentance and reconciliation. Also close to Durkheim (Topic 2, §20–§22): the punishment of crime reaffirms the collective conscience and maintains social solidarity. Machhi Singh’s “collective conscience of the community is so shocked” is the denunciatory theory admitted into Indian sentencing law — and, as Hart’s objection shows, it is the most dangerous of the five, because it makes sentencing turn on public sentiment.

12. LIFE IMPRISONMENT — meaning, remission, and the “middle path”

12.1 What “imprisonment for life” means

PropositionAuthority
Imprisonment for life means imprisonment for the WHOLE OF THE REMAINDER OF THE CONVICT’S NATURAL LIFE. It is not, and never was, equivalent to twenty years or fourteen yearsGopal Vinayak Godse v. State of Maharashtra AIR 1961 SC 600 (Constitution Bench): “a sentence of transportation for life must prima facie be treated as transportation for life… unless the said sentence is commuted or remitted by appropriate authority under the relevant provisions of the Indian Penal Code or the Code of Criminal Procedure, a prisoner sentenced to life imprisonment is bound in law to serve the life term in prison.” Reaffirmed in Maru Ram v. Union of India (1981) 1 SCC 107, Naib Singh v. State of Punjab (1983) 2 SCC 454, Zahid Hussein v. State of W.B. (2001) 3 SCC 750, Mohd. Munna v. Union of India (2005) 7 SCC 417 and Union of India v. V. Sriharan (2016) 7 SCC 1 (Constitution Bench)
The “14 years” idea is a misconception arising from s.433-A CrPC → s.475 BNSS, which merely bars the release of a life convict (in cases where death was one of the punishments provided, or where a death sentence has been commuted) until he has served at least fourteen years. That is a floor, not a ceiling, and it is a restriction on the executive power of remission, not a definition of the sentences.475 BNSS (s.433-A CrPC); Maru Ram; Godse
Remission does not alter the sentence; it only affects the period of actual incarceration. Remission earned under prison rules cannot by itself entitle a life convict to releaseGodse; State of Haryana v. Mahender Singh (2007) 13 SCC 606
A court may impose a life sentence with a fixed minimum term beyond 14 years, or for the whole of natural life, as a SUBSTITUTE for the death penalty — the “middle path” or “Shraddananda sentence”Swamy Shraddananda (2) v. State of Karnataka (2008) 13 SCC 767 (§28); approved by the Constitution Bench in Union of India v. V. Sriharan (2016) 7 SCC 1
But only the High Court or the Supreme Court may impose such a special-category sentence — a Sessions Court cannot; and the executive’s power of remission under Arts. 72 and 161 remains untouched by any such judicial directionSriharan (2016), majority (3:2). The minority (Lalit and Sapre JJ.) held that such a sentence encroaches on the executive power
Remission powers — the executive may remit, suspend or commute under ss.473–478 BNSS (ss.432–435 CrPC) and under Arts. 72 and 161. The Government must consider an application for remission and give reasons; a policy must be applied fairly; and the relevant date for the applicable remission policy is the date of convictionLaxman Naskar v. State of W.B. (2000) 7 SCC 626 (the five factors); Sangeet v. State of Haryana (2013) 2 SCC 452 (no “wholesale” remission); Bilkis Yakub Rasool v. Union of India (2024) — remission orders quashed; the appropriate Government is the State in which the accused was sentenced, not where the offence occurred
Why this matters for the death penalty debate. A large part of the argument for retaining capital punishment rested on the belief that “life” meant fourteen years and that a murderer would be back on the street. Swamy Shraddananda (2) destroyed that premise by creating a sentence that is genuinely irreducible or long-fixed, and thereby supplied the courts with the middle option whose absence had driven them to the gallows. Sinha J. put the reasoning with complete candour: the death penalty jurisprudence had become “judge-centric”, and “a far more rational and just option [is] to substitute a death sentence by a sentence of imprisonment for the whole of the convict’s natural life”. When a question asks whether the death penalty should be abolished, this is the constructive answer to give.

Fine
ss.8–10 BNS (ss.63–72 IPC). Where the amount is not expressed, the fine is unlimited but shall not be excessive (s.8(1)). COMMUNITY SERVICE — the new punishment
s.4(f) BNS with its Explanation: “the work which the Court may order a convict to perform as a form of punishment that benefits the community, for which he shall not be entitled to any remuneration.” Six offences carry it (§10.1). Significance: (1) it is the first non-custodial punishment in the general penal law, distinct from probation because it is a punishment, not a substitute for one; (2) it is simultaneously reformative (the offender remains in the community, in employment, with his family) and restorative (the community receives reparation); (3) it responds directly to prison overcrowding — an occupancy rate of about 130% with roughly three-quarters undertrials; and (4) it echoes the JJ Act, s.18(1)(c), which has had community service since 2015. Criticism: the BNS does not define the duration, the nature of the work, the supervising authority or the consequence of breach, which is left to rules the States have yet to frame — a serious drafting gap and an excellent point to make.

The legislative history in three sentences — this is what Kaul’s reading is about. Under s.367(5) of the Code of 1898 as it originally stood, where a court convicted of an offence punishable with death and awarded any other punishment, it had to state why the death sentence was not awarded — death was, in effect, the normal sentence for murder. The Criminal Procedure Code (Amendment) Act, 1955 deleted that requirement, leaving the matter to the discretion of the court. The Code of 1973 reversed the presumption altogether by enacting s.354(3), which requires “special reasons” for a sentence of death — so that life imprisonment became the rule and death the exception. Kaul’s reading (§16) sets this out in terms; Bachan Singh and Machhi Singh build the “rarest of rare” doctrine on it.

16. KAUL: Discretion in the matter of imposition of the death penalty

PRESCRIBED READING DUBushan Tilak Kaul, “Criminal Law”, XXXVIII Annual Survey of Indian Law 181–226 (2002)
VERBATIM — Kaul, DU Case Material pp. 149–150 DU “CAPITAL PUNISHMENT: RECENT DECISIONS ON SENTENCING UNDER THE CRIMINAL PROCEDURE CODE, 1973
Discretion in the matter of imposition of death penalty
In Lehna [(2002) 3 SCC 76] the Supreme Court noticed the changes that the Criminal Procedure Code has undergone in the last three decades clearly indicating that Parliament is taking note of the contemporary criminological thought and THE MOVEMENT FROM DETERRENCE TO REFORMATION. There is a definite swing discernible in the code IN FAVOUR OF LIFE IMPRISONMENT AND AGAINST DEATH PENALTY consistent with the changing criminological thought.

Prior to the amendment of section 367(5) of the old code by the Criminal Procedure Code (Amendment) Act, 1955, on a conviction for an offence punishable with death, if the court sentenced the accused to any punishment OTHER THAN DEATH, THE REASON WHY SENTENCE OF DEATH WAS NOT AWARDED HAD TO BE STATED in the judgment. The matter was left, after the aforementioned amendment, to the discretion of the court. The court had, however, to take into account all the circumstances, and state its reasons for whichever of the two sentences it imposed in its discretion. The formal rule that THE NORMAL PUNISHMENT FOR MURDER WAS DEATH no longer held good and it was now within the discretion of the court to award either of the two sentences envisaged in section 302. The amendment of section 367(5) of the old code did not affect the law regulating punishment under IPC. This amendment relates to the procedure only. The courts were no longer required now to elaborate the reasons for not awarding the death penalty. But they could not depart from sound judicial consideration preferring the lesser punishment.

Further change in the criminological thought IN FAVOUR OF REFORMATION is discernible in the 1973 Code wherein DEATH SENTENCE IS ORDINARILY RULED OUT AND CAN ONLY BE IMPOSED FOR SPECIAL REASONS as provided in section 354(3) of the new code. The theory of reform and rehabilitation in preference to imposing severe punishment is ALSO DISCERNIBLE IN SECTION 361 which is a new provision in the code and makes it MANDATORY for the court to record SPECIAL REASONS FOR NOT RELEASING AN OFFENDER ON PROBATION envisaged in section 360 of the code. These are some indications by the legislature that REFORMATION AND REHABILITATION OF OFFENDERS AND NOT MERE DETERRENCE ARE NOW AMONGST THE FOREMOST OBJECTS of the administration of criminal justice in our country. Sections 354(3) and 361 in the new code have become part of the law as a result of [the] emergent position of acceptance by the legislature of the aforesaid new advances and the trends in criminology.

It would not, therefore, be wrong to assume that THE PERSONALITY OF THE OFFENDER as revealed by his AGE, CHARACTER, ANTECEDENTS and other relevant circumstances and THE TRACTABILITY OF THE OFFENDER TO REFORM must necessarily play THE MOST PROMINENT ROLE in deciding the sentence to be awarded to the offender. THE SPECIAL REASONS required under these provisions HAVE TO BE RELATABLE TO THESE FACTORS.

The emerging trends in criminology reflected in the successive legislative amendments in the code have also been the subject matter of discussion in a number of judicial decisions especially in Ediga Anamma, Bachan Singh and Machhi Singh.”
Why §§354(3) and 361 must be read TOGETHER — the single most elegant point in this reading. Kaul’s observation (which he takes from Bishnu Deo Shaw, para 26, §21 below) is that the 1973 Code inserted two new “special reasons” provisions at the same time, and they point in the same direction: s.354(3) requires special reasons to impose the most severe sentence, and s.361 requires special reasons not to impose the least severe disposition. Between them they establish that “reformation and rehabilitation of offenders, and not mere deterrence, are now amongst the foremost objects of the administration of criminal justice in our country”. That single sentence links head (ii) to head (iii) of the syllabus, and is the best possible bridge between capital punishment and probation in an essay.

17. KAUL: Lehna — the five circumstances and the principle of proportion

VERBATIM — Kaul, DU Case Material pp. 150–151 DU “In Lehna, the Supreme Court observed that criminal justice deals with complex human problems and diverse human beings. A judge has to BALANCE THE PERSONALITY OF THE OFFENDER WITH THE CIRCUMSTANCES, SITUATIONS AND THE REACTION and choose the appropriate sentence to be imposed. The court after surveying its earlier decision[s] and the legislative policy held that LIFE IMPRISONMENT IS THE RULE AND DEATH SENTENCE AN EXCEPTION. Death sentence is to be imposed in RAREST OF RARE CASES when the COLLECTIVE CONSCIENCE of the country is so shocked that it will expect the holders of judicial power centre to inflict death penalty IRRESPECTIVE OF THEIR PERSONAL OPINION as regards [the] desirability or otherwise of retaining death penalty. In the words of the court the community may entertain such [a] sentiment in the following circumstances:

(1) When the murder is committed in an EXTREMELY BRUTAL, GROTESQUE, DIABOLICAL, REVOLTING, OR DASTARDLY MANNER so as to arouse intense and extreme indignation of the community.

(2) When the murder is committed for a MOTIVE WHICH EVINCES TOTAL DEPRAVITY AND MEANNESS; e.g. murder by [a] hired assassin for money or reward; or cold-blooded murder for gains of a person vis-à-vis whom the murderer is in a dominating position or in a position of trust; or murder… committed in the course [of] betrayal of the motherland.

(3) When murder of a member of a SCHEDULED CASTE OR MINORITY COMMUNITY etc. is committed not for personal reasons but in circumstances which AROUSE SOCIAL WRATH, or in case of ‘BRIDE BURNING’ or ‘DOWRY DEATHS’ or when murder is committed in order to remarry for the sake of extracting dowry once again or to marry another woman on account of infatuation.

(4) When the crime is ENORMOUS IN PROPORTION. For instance when MULTIPLE MURDERS, say of all or almost all the members of a family or a large number of persons of a particular caste, community, or locality, are committed.

(5) When the victim of murder is an INNOCENT CHILD, or a HELPLESS WOMAN or OLD or INFIRM PERSON or [a] person vis-à-vis whom the murderer is in a dominating position, or a PUBLIC FIGURE generally loved and respected by the community.

The court observed that if upon taking an OVERALL GLOBAL VIEW of the circumstances in the light of the aforementioned propositions and taking into account the answers to the questions posed by way of the test for the rarest of the rare cases, the circumstances of the case are such that death is warranted, the court would proceed to do so. While awarding [an] adequate sentence the courts have to be cognizant of the fact that A CONVICT HOVERS BETWEEN LIFE AND DEATH when the question of gravity of offence and award of adequate sentence comes for consideration.

Further, an award of punishment in a civilized society wedded to [the] rule of law is the OUTCOME OF DELIBERATIONS IN THE COURT ROOM after adequate hearing is afforded to the parties… It is the outcome of deliberations and the screening of the material by the judge who is the informed man that leads to the determination of the lis. THE PRINCIPLE OF PROPORTION BETWEEN CRIME AND PUNISHMENT IS A PRINCIPLE OF JUST DESERT THAT SERVES AS THE FOUNDATION OF EVERY CRIMINAL SENTENCE THAT IS JUSTIFIABLE. As a principle of criminal justice it is hardly less familiar or less important than the principle that ONLY [THE] GUILTY OUGHT TO BE PUNISHED. Indeed, the requirement that punishment not be disproportionately great, which is a COROLLARY OF JUST DESERT, is dictated by the same principle that does not allow punishment of the innocent, for ANY PUNISHMENT IN EXCESS OF WHAT IS DESERVED FOR THE CRIMINAL CONDUCT IS PUNISHMENT WITHOUT GUILT.

The criminal law adheres in general to the principle of proportionality in prescribing liability according to the culpability of each kind of criminal conduct. Ordinarily some significant discretion is left to the judge in deciding the question of sentence to be awarded in each case, which is presumably done to permit sentence[s] that reflect more subtle considerations of culpability that are raised by the special facts of each case. The court observed:
  “Punishment ought always to fit the crime; yet in practice sentences are determined largely by OTHER CONSIDERATIONS. Sometimes it is the correctional needs of the perpetrator that are offered to justify a sentence; sometimes the desirability of keeping him out of circulation, and sometimes even the terrific results of his crime. INEVITABLY THESE CONSIDERATIONS CAUSE DEPARTURE FROM A JUST DESERT AS A BASIS OF PUNISHMENT AND CREATE CASES OF APPARENT INJUSTICE THAT ARE SERIOUS AND WIDESPREAD.”

The court opined that proportion between crime [and] punishment is a goal respected in principle, and in spite of errant notions it remains a strong influence in the determination of sentences.”
Notice what Kaul’s account of Lehna shows. The five circumstances are substantially the Machhi Singh categories (§23) — manner, motive, anti-social nature, magnitude and the personality of the victim — restated twenty years later. What Lehna adds is the insistence that they are subordinate to the principle of proportion, which is “a principle of just desert that serves as the foundation of every criminal sentence that is justifiable”. The quoted passage — “any punishment in excess of what is deserved for the criminal conduct is PUNISHMENT WITHOUT GUILT” — is the single most powerful sentence in the whole reading, and it is pure Ashworth: desert limits punishment. Deploy it in any essay on sentencing discretion.

17.1 The application in Lehna itself — the DU material’s account of the facts

VERBATIM — Kaul, DU Case Material p. 151 DU “Coming to the facts in Lehna, the court found that the genesis of [the] dispute between the accused and the other members of the family was LAND. The accused seemed to have taken exception to his father taking away the land from him, for which he considered his brother and sister-in-law to be responsible. The accused was responsible for causing [the] death of his brother, sister-in-law and another relation. The court took into account the medical evidence showing that the accused had ALSO SUSTAINED SERIOUS INJURIES in the thrashing that he got from villagers after he had attacked the victims, and the fact that the medical evidence indicated that IT WAS THE MENTAL CONDITION OF THE ACCUSED which had led him to assault his relations, which could not be lost sight of. THE SAME MAY NOT BE RELEVANT TO JUDGE CULPABILITY, BUT IT CERTAINLY IS A FACTOR WHILE CONSIDERING THE QUESTION OF SENTENCE. There was no evidence of any DIABOLIC PLANNING to commit the crime, though cruel was the act. The act of the father taking away the land from the accused had deprived him of earning, the means of his livelihood, exhibiting his displeasure and his resentment. The frequency of quarrels indicated LACK OF ANY SINISTER PLANNING to take away lives of the deceased. In this peculiar background the court held that death sentence was not proper and sentenced the accused to imprisonment for life which it thought was more appropriate.”
The examinable proposition“The same may not be relevant to judge culpability, but it certainly IS A FACTOR WHILE CONSIDERING THE QUESTION OF SENTENCE.” This sentence states the entire distinction between the guilt stage and the sentencing stage. Material that falls short of a defence (here, the accused’s mental condition, which did not amount to unsoundness of mind under s.84 IPC / s.22 BNS) may nonetheless be decisive in mitigation. This is precisely why s.235(2) CrPC / s.258(2) BNSS requires a separate hearing, and why Allauddin Mian (§24) insists that it must not be a formality.

18.1 Ruli Ram — political violence and the seven reasons

VERBATIM — Kaul, DU Case Material pp. 152–153 DU “In Ruli Ram, the court deprecated the recent trends where political battles are increasingly being fought with BULLETS rather than the BALLOTS, resulting in loss of innocent lives who have no role to play therein. In this case close relations of the deceased, who were two boys aged 10 and 12, lost their lives allegedly on account of one such battle… It seems that the accused wanted the family members of these two boys to vote for a particular candidate, but on their refusal to do so the accused/appellants took their revenge by throwing the deceased boys into a pond…

[The trial court had convicted under s.304 Part II; the High Court altered it to s.302. The Supreme Court restored the trial court’s view, finding that] the intention of the accused was not to murder the boys but to create some disturbance at the police station in order to divert the attention of the crowd collected, so that BOOTH CAPTURING could be facilitated… no injuries were caused to the deceased before they were thrown into the pond and… there was no attempt to even strangulate them. However, the court held that the accused/appellants could be attributed with the KNOWLEDGE that the natural and probable consequence of their acts was likely to cause death…

On the question of sentence, the court found that it was a fit case where [the] accused had been rightly sentenced by the trial court with the maximum sentence under section 304 Part II for [the] following reasons:
(1) The victims of the crime were NOT EVEN VOTERS but became victims of political differences of elders.
(2) Political rivalry and differences cannot extend to taking away the lives of others. CRIMINALISATION OF POLITICS is a hot topic causing concern.
(3) In a democracy, THE PATH TO POWER CANNOT BE ALLOWED TO HAVE DEAD BODIES LITTERED OVER IT. It cannot be a case of capturing power (beginning with booth capturing) AT ANY COST. This trend is dangerous and has to be curbed.
(4) In a case linked with political battles, STRINGENT PUNISHMENT IS DESIRABLE WITHOUT EXCEPTION. Choice to vote for a candidate cannot be suppressed by intimidation. That would be against the spirit of democracy.
(5) The punishment has to be ALWAYS PROPORTIONATE TO THE CRIME.
(6) Punishment serves a purpose inasmuch as IT ACTS AS A DETERRENT for those who have the propensity to take the law into their own hands.
(7) The principle of proportion between crime and punishment is a PRINCIPLE OF JUST DESERTS that serves as the foundation of every criminal sentence that is justifiable…

Keeping in view the aforesaid considerations, the court held that the sentence of ten years rigorous imprisonment awarded by the trial court was quite appropriate.”
Use this case for two things(1) It is the clearest statement in the prescribed material that deterrence and desert operate together: reasons (5) and (7) are pure desert, reason (6) is pure deterrence, and reasons (1)–(4) are the aggravating circumstances. (2) It is the bridge to Topic 3: “criminalisation of politics is a hot topic causing concern” and “the path to power cannot be allowed to have dead bodies littered over it” — political graft and the Vohra Committee (Topic 3, §25.4) in the language of a sentencing judgment.

18.2 Ezil, Ram Anup Singh and Vajja Srinivasu

VERBATIM — Kaul, DU Case Material p. 153 DU “In Ezil v. State of Tamil Nadu [(2002) 5 SCC 189] the court has held that a grave act of depravity, to kill an innocent person only for the purpose of enriching themselves with the fortunes brought by the deceased, who unaware of their diabolical scheme got lured into their company for a safe travel, deserves to be dealt with AN IRON HAND. The imposition of ten years rigorous imprisonment for the offence of robbery under section 392 IPC and rigorous imprisonment for life for the offence of murder under section 302 IPC could not be considered to be either harsh or SO GROSSLY DISPROPORTIONATE AS TO SHOCK THE CONSCIENCE OF THE COURT.

In Ram Anup Singh v. State of Bihar [(2002) 6 SCC 868] the court was dealing with a case in which the accused killed all the members of [a] family and the act of the appellants was, in the words of the Supreme Court, ‘certainly inhuman, cruel and dastardly’. The Supreme Court still examined the correctness of the sentence of death… The court, on examination of the records of the case, opined that there was NO EVIDENCE TO SUGGEST THAT THE APPELLANTS ARE A MENACE TO THE SOCIETY considering their past records, which did not show their ever having indulged in such behaviour in the past or having resorted to violence… They appeared to belong to [a] middle class farmer family for whom LAND WAS OF GREAT VALUE and they could not see an outsider — even if it is the son-in-law belonging to another family — being gifted property by their relations. The court found that there was nothing to suggest that THEY MAY REPEAT SUCH BARBARISM IN FUTURE so that they could constitute A CONTINUING THREAT TO THE SOCIETY. Though the crime committed by them was heinous and brutal, yet in the opinion of the court, IT DID NOT FALL IN THE CATEGORY OF THE RAREST OF THE RARE CASES. The court was of the opinion that it had no reason to believe that the appellants COULD NOT BE REFORMED OR REHABILITATED… The court set aside the sentence of death… The court instead sentenced them to rigorous imprisonment for life WITH A CONDITION THAT THEY SHALL NOT BE RELEASED BEFORE COMPLETING THE ACTUAL TERM OF 20 YEARS including the period already undergone by them.

In Vajja Srinivasu v. State of A.P. [(2002) 9 SCC 620] the court has held that the disability that the provisions of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 are INAPPLICABLE to the offence under [the] NDPS Act CANNOT PRECLUDE THE ACCUSED FROM PLEADING THAT A LESSER SENTENCE BE AWARDED, particularly in view of the AGE FACTOR of the accused. The court in this case, after examining all aspects and [the] fact situation, reduced the sentence from rigorous imprisonment of five years to only one year in view of the fact that the accused was aged only 21 years.”
Three propositions to take from these three cases. (1) Ezil: the test on appeal against a sentence of imprisonment is whether it is “so grossly disproportionate as to shock the conscience of the court” — a high threshold, and the mirror-image of the “rarest of rare” test. (2) Ram Anup Singh: a multiple murder of an entire family, described as “inhuman, cruel and dastardly”, was nevertheless held not to be rarest of rare, because the accused were not a continuing threat and could not be said to be beyond reform — the clearest possible demonstration that Bachan Singh requires regard to the criminal and not only to the crime. It is also the earliest of the “fixed-term life sentence” cases — a life sentence with an actual minimum of twenty years — six years before Swamy Shraddananda (2) made the technique doctrinal. (3) Vajja Srinivasu: even where probation is statutorily barred (s.32 NDPS bars the Probation Act), the age of the offender remains a valid ground for reducing the sentence. This is the answer to a problem question where a young offender is convicted of an offence to which the Probation Act does not apply — a point of very direct examination value (compare PYQ 2022 Q3, §48).

20. The international position and India’s obligations

The treaty framework
Art. 3 UDHR, 1948 — the right to life. Art. 6 ICCPR, 1966 (ratified by India in 1979): “Every human being has the inherent right to life… In countries which have not abolished the death penalty, sentence of death may be imposed only for the MOST SERIOUS CRIMES… and can only be carried out pursuant to a final judgment rendered by a competent court.” Art. 6(4) — the right to seek pardon or commutation. Art. 6(5) — no death sentence for offences committed by persons below eighteen, and no execution of pregnant women. Second Optional Protocol to the ICCPR, 1989 — aiming at abolition; India is not a party. Art. 37(a) CRC — no capital punishment for offences committed below eighteen; India is a party, and s.21 of the JJ Act, 2015 implements it (Topic 4).
The ECOSOC Safeguards (1984)
Nine safeguards guaranteeing protection of the rights of those facing the death penalty: only for the most serious crimes with lethal or other extremely grave consequences; no retrospective application; exemption for persons under 18, pregnant women, new mothers and persons who have become insane; guilt based on clear and convincing evidence leaving no room for an alternative explanation; a fair trial with adequate legal assistance; a right of appeal; a right to seek pardon; no execution pending appeal or mercy; and execution “so as to inflict the minimum possible suffering”.
The global trend
UN General Assembly resolutions calling for a moratorium on the use of the death penalty have been adopted repeatedly since 2007, with increasing majorities; India has consistently voted against them, explaining that the death penalty is exercised in the “rarest of rare” cases. Well over two-thirds of the world’s States have abolished the death penalty in law or in practice. Protocol No. 6 and No. 13 to the ECHR abolish it in Europe. Regional courts — see Soering v. United Kingdom (1989) ECHR on the “death row phenomenon”.
Comparative constitutional decisions to cite
Furman v. Georgia (1972) — the US Supreme Court struck down the death penalty as then administered because it was “wantonly and freakishly imposed”; heavily relied on in Bishnu Deo Shaw and Rajendra Prasad, and discussed and distinguished in Bachan Singh. Gregg v. Georgia (1976) — the death penalty upheld under guided-discretion statutes. Woodson v. North Carolina (1976) — mandatory death sentences unconstitutional (compare Mithu). S. v. Makwanyane (1995) — the Constitutional Court of South Africa abolished the death penalty as inconsistent with the right to life and human dignity; the most frequently cited abolitionist judgment in the common-law world. Reyes v. The Queen (2002) and the Privy Council’s Caribbean line — mandatory death sentences struck down.

21. The background cases — Jagmohan, Ediga Anamma and Rajendra Prasad

You cannot brief Bachan Singh without these three, because the whole of Bachan Singh is a reconciliation of them.

Jagmohan Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh(1973) 1 SCC 20; (1973) 2 SCR 541 — Constitution Benchconstitutionality
Facts and issue
The appellant was convicted of murder and sentenced to death under s.302 IPC. He challenged the constitutionality of the death penalty under Arts. 14, 19 and 21, on the grounds (i) that the unguided discretion to choose between death and life violated Art. 14; (ii) that the deprivation of life violated Art. 19; and (iii) that the procedure under the Code of 1898 provided no separate sentencing hearing and no standards, so that the deprivation was not by a “procedure established by law” under Art. 21.
Arguments
For the appellant: there are no legislative standards; two persons convicted of identical offences may receive different sentences; the discretion is therefore arbitrary; and the American experience (later crystallised in Furman) shows that unguided capital sentencing is intolerable. For the State: the discretion is judicial, not executive, and is exercised on well-recognised principles subject to appeal; standards are impossible to formulate; and the Constituent Assembly and Parliament, knowing of the death penalty, retained it.
Decision
The death penalty was upheld. The two passages reproduced in the DU material DU are: “The impossibility of laying down standards [in the matter of sentencing] is at the very core of criminal law as administered in India, which invests the Judges with a very wide discretion in the matter of fixing the degree of punishment, and… this discretion in the matter of sentence is liable to be corrected by superior courts. THE EXERCISE OF JUDICIAL DISCRETION ON WELL-RECOGNISED PRINCIPLES IS, IN THE FINAL ANALYSIS, THE SAFEST POSSIBLE SAFEGUARD FOR THE ACCUSED.” And: “some [murders] at least are DIABOLICAL IN CONCEPTION AND CRUEL IN EXECUTION. In some others where the victim is a person of high standing in the country, society is liable to be rocked to its very foundation. Such murders cannot be simply washed away by finding alibis in the social maladjustment of the murderer. Prevalence of such crimes speaks, in the opinion of many, for the inevitability of death penalty NOT ONLY BY WAY OF DETERRENCE BUT AS A TOKEN OF EMPHATIC DISAPPROVAL BY THE SOCIETY… A very responsible body (Law Commission) has come to the conclusion after considering all the relevant factors. On the conclusions thus offered to us, it will be difficult to hold that capital punishment as such is unreasonable or not required in the public interest.”
Principle
Capital punishment is constitutional; the absence of legislative standards does not render it arbitrary, because the discretion is judicial and correctable; and impossibility of standardisation is inherent in criminal sentencing. Bachan Singh expressly reaffirms this reasoning. Note what changed after Jagmohan: the Code of 1973 introduced s.235(2) (the pre-sentence hearing) and s.354(3) (special reasons), which supplied exactly the two procedural deficiencies the appellant in Jagmohan had complained of.
Ediga Anamma v. State of Andhra Pradesh(1974) 4 SCC 443; AIR 1974 SC 799 — Krishna Iyer, J.the turn to the criminal
Facts
A young woman was convicted of the murder of her paramour’s wife and child and sentenced to death. Decided on 11 February 1974, before s.354(3) came into force, but anticipating it.
Decision and principle
Krishna Iyer J. commuted the sentence, and laid down the first systematic Indian list of sentencing factors: “The weapons used and the manner of their use, the horrendous features of the crime and [the] hapless, helpless state of the victim, and the like, steel the heart of the law for a sterner sentence” — a passage expressly approved in Bachan Singh (para 197). He also identified the mitigating factors of youth, sex, socio-economic deprivation, mental condition and the absence of premeditation, and famously observed that the “focus has shifted from the crime to the criminal”, and that “a legal policy on life or death cannot be left for ad hoc mood or individual predilection”. Important for the DU material: Kaul’s reading cites Ediga Anamma alongside Bachan Singh and Machhi Singh as one of the three decisions in which the legislative trend was discussed DU; and Bachan Singh notes that Ediga Anamma’s indicator “was crystallised in that case after paying due regard to the shift in legislative policy embodied in Section 354(3)… although on the date of that decision this provision had not come into force.” (Ediga Anamma is also briefed in Topic 2 for its discussion of female criminality.)
Rajendra Prasad v. State of Uttar Pradesh(1979) 3 SCC 646 — Krishna Iyer and Desai JJ. (Sen J. dissenting)the high-water mark of abolitionism
Holding
By a majority of 2:1, the Court held that after the enactment of s.354(3) the “special reasons” for a death sentence “must relate NOT TO THE CRIME AS SUCH BUT TO THE CRIMINAL”; that “it is constitutionally permissible to swing a criminal out of corporeal existence ONLY IF the security of State and society, public order and the interests of the general public compel that course as provided in Article 19(2) to (6)”; and that “such extraordinary grounds alone constitutionally qualify as special reasons as leave no option to the court but to execute the offender if State and society are to survive. ONE STROKE OF MURDER HARDLY QUALIFIES for this drastic requirement, however gruesome the killing or pathetic the situation, unless the inherent testimony oozing from that act is irresistible that the murderous appetite of the convict is too chronic and deadly that ordered life in a given locality or society or in prison itself would be gone if this man were now or later to be at large. IF HE IS AN IRREDEEMABLE MURDERER, LIKE A BLOODTHIRSTY TIGER, HE HAS TO EMIT HIS TERRESTRIAL TENANCY.” (All quoted verbatim in the DU material’s text of Bachan Singh DU.)
Why it matters
Bishnu Deo Shaw (§21) expressly follows Rajendra Prasad; and it was the perceived conflict between Rajendra Prasad and the Constitution Bench in Jagmohan — set out by Kailasam J. in seven numbered points reproduced verbatim in the DU material (§22.2) — that caused the reference to the Constitution Bench in Bachan Singh. Bachan Singh then rejected the “criminal only” approach: “the Court must pay due regard BOTH to the crime AND the criminal”; and it objected to the word “only” in the Art. 19 passage. In short: Rajendra Prasad is the thesis, Jagmohan the antithesis, and Bachan Singh the synthesis.

22. CASE 1 — Bishnu Deo Shaw v. State of West Bengal (1979) DU verbatim

Bishnu Deo Shaw v. State of West BengalAIR 1979 SC 964; (1979) 3 SCC 714 — Chinnappa Reddy, J., with a concurring judgment of Krishna Iyer, J.Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 176–185
Facts
The appellant was convicted by the Additional Sessions Judge, Alipore, of the murder of his own son and sentenced to death; the Calcutta High Court confirmed the conviction and sentence. There was “some vague evidence” that the appellant suspected that the deceased was not his own son and that “he used to get angry with the deceased for not obeying him”, and “vague evidence that he had killed the mother of the deceased and had suffered [a] sentence of imprisonment for that offence”. The Supreme Court granted special leave confined to the question of sentence only.
The reasons given below
“The reason given by the learned Sessions Judge for giving the sentence of death was that the murder was ‘cruel and brutal’ and that the facts show the ‘grim determination’ of the accused to kill the deceased. The Sessions Judge made no reference to the MOTIVE of the accused for the commission of the murder. The High Court while confirming the sentence observed that the accused had previously murdered his wife, suspecting her infidelity and suspecting that the deceased in the present case was not his own son, that the sentence of imprisonment imposed on him for the murder of his wife had no sobering effect, and that he had murdered his own son without any mercy or remorse and that he, therefore, deserved no mercy.”
Issues
(1) Do the theories of punishment justify the death penalty? (2) What are the “special reasons” contemplated by s.354(3) CrPC? (3) Do the reasons given by the Sessions Judge and the High Court amount to such special reasons?
Arguments
For the appellant: after Rajendra Prasad the “special reasons” must relate to the criminal and must establish that he is beyond redemption; mere epithets describing the crime do not satisfy s.354(3); the death penalty is not supported by any theory of punishment. For the State: counsel, the Court records, “did state that in view of the criteria laid down in Rajendra Prasad case the State did not propose to file any written submissions against commutation to life imprisonment” — that is, the State effectively conceded on sentence.
The judgment — Part I: the paradox and the uniqueness of death
VERBATIM — paras 1–2 DU “1. ‘THE MURDERER HAS KILLED. IT IS WRONG TO KILL. LET US KILL THE MURDERER.’ That was how a Mr Bonsall of Manchester (quoted by Arthur Koestler in his ‘Drinkers of Infinity’), in a letter to the Press, neatly summed up the paradox and the pathology of the Death Penalty…

2. The dilemma of the Judge in every murder case, ‘Death or life imprisonment for the murderer?’ is the question with which we are faced in this appeal. The very nature of the penalty of death appears to make it imperative that at every suitable opportunity LIFE IMPRISONMENT SHOULD BE PREFERRED TO THE DEATH PENALTY. ‘The penalty of death differs from all other forms of criminal punishment, NOT IN DEGREE BUT IN KIND. It is UNIQUE IN ITS TOTAL IRREVOCABILITY. It is unique in its REJECTION OF REHABILITATION of the convict as a basic purpose of criminal justice. And it is unique finally in its ABSOLUTE RENUNCIATION OF ALL THAT IS EMBODIED IN OUR CONCEPT OF HUMANITY’ — per Stewart J. in Furman v. Georgia. ‘DEATH IS IRREVOCABLE, LIFE IMPRISONMENT IS NOT. DEATH, OF COURSE, MAKES REHABILITATION IMPOSSIBLE, LIFE IMPRISONMENT DOES NOT’ (per Marshall J. in Furman v. Georgia).”
The judgment — Part II: which theory fits capital punishment?
Paras 3–5 are set out verbatim at §9.1 above, and should be read here: reformation is irrelevant “since life and not death can reform”; prevention is unimportant where the choice is between death and life; retribution, properly understood, rules out capital punishment; and denunciation is retribution in moral dress, “uncomfortably close to human sacrifice”.
The judgment — Part III: deterrence, examined at length
VERBATIM — paras 6–11 DU “6. The deterrent theory may now be considered. It is important to notice here that the question is NOT whether the penalty of death has [a] deterrent effect on potential murderers BUT WHETHER IT DETERS MORE EFFECTIVELY THAN OTHER PENALTIES — say, a sentence of imprisonment for a long term? Is capital punishment the most desirable and the most effective instrument for protecting the community from violent crime? What is the evidence that it has a UNIQUELY deterrent force compared with the alternative of protracted imprisonment? If the death penalty really and appreciably decreases murder, if there is equally no effective substitute and if its incidents are not injurious to society, we may well support the death penalty. But all studies made on the subject… appear to have led to the conclusion that THE DEATH PENALTY IS INCONSEQUENTIAL AS A DETERRENT.

7. Sir James Fitz James Stephen, a great Victorian Judge and a vigorous exponent of the deterrent theory, said in his Essay on Capital Punishment: ‘This is one of those propositions which it is difficult to prove simply because they are in themselves more obvious than any proof can make them… The whole experience of mankind is in the other direction. The threat of instant death is the one to which resort has always been made when there was an absolute necessity of producing some result. NO ONE GOES TO CERTAIN INEVITABLE DEATH EXCEPT BY COMPULSION. Put the matter the other way, was there ever yet a criminal who, when sentenced to death and brought out to die, would refuse the offer of a commutation of [the] sentence for a severest secondary punishment? Surely not. Why is this? It can only be because ALL THAT A MAN HAS WILL BE GIVEN FOR HIS LIFE. In any secondary punishment however terrible, THERE IS HOPE; BUT DEATH IS DEATH; ITS TERRORS CANNOT BE DESCRIBED MORE FORCIBLY.’

8. Stephen’s statement was admittedly a DOGMATIC ASSERTION, since he himself stated that it was a proposition difficult to prove though, according to him, self-evident. The great fallacy in the argument of Stephen has been pointed out by several criminologists. Stephen makes NO DISTINCTION between a threat of CERTAIN AND IMMINENT punishment which faces the convicted murderer and the threat of a DIFFERENT, PROBLEMATIC punishment which may or may not influence a potential murderer. Murder may be unpremeditated, under the stress of some disturbing emotion, or it may be premeditated, after planning and deliberation. Where the murder is [unpremeditated] any thought of [the] possibility of punishment is BLURRED BY EMOTION and the penalty of death can no more deter than any other penalty. Where [the] murder is premeditated the offender DISREGARDS THE RISK OF PUNISHMENT BECAUSE HE THINKS THERE IS NO CHANCE OF DETECTION. WHAT WEIGHS WITH HIM IS THE UNCERTAINTY OF DETECTION AND CONSEQUENT PUNISHMENT RATHER THAN THE NATURE OF THE PUNISHMENT. The Advisory Council on the Treatment of Offenders appointed by the Government of Great Britain stated in their report in 1960: ‘We were impressed by the argument that THE GREATEST DETERRENT TO CRIME IS NOT THE FEAR OF PUNISHMENT, BUT THE CERTAINTY OF DETECTION.’

9. Prof. HART countered Stephen’s argument with these observations: ‘This (Stephen’s) estimate of the paramount place in human motivation of the fear of death reads impressively but surely contains a suggestio falsi, and once this is detected its cogency as an argument in favour of the death penalty for murder vanishes, for there is really NO PARALLEL between the situation of a convicted murderer over the alternative of life imprisonment in the shadow of the gallows and the situation of the murderer CONTEMPLATING his crime. The certainty of death is one thing; perhaps for normal people nothing can be compared with it. BUT THE EXISTENCE OF THE DEATH PENALTY DOES NOT MEAN FOR THE MURDERER CERTAINTY OF DEATH NOW. IT MEANS NOT VERY HIGH PROBABILITY OF DEATH IN THE FUTURE. And futurity and uncertainty, the hope of an escape, rational or irrational, fast[ly] diminishes the difference between death and imprisonment as deterrent, and may diminish [it] to vanishing point… The way in which the convicted murderer may view the immediate prospect of the gallows AFTER HE HAS BEEN CAUGHT must be a POOR GUIDE to the effect of this prospect upon him WHEN HE IS CONTEMPLATING COMMITTING HIS CRIME.’

10. A hundred and fifty years ago a study was made by the Joint Select Committee appointed by the General Assembly of Connecticut and they reported: ‘Your Committee do not hesitate to express their firm belief that a well devised system of imprisonment, one which should render the punishment CERTAIN AND PERPETUAL, would be FAR MORE EFFECTUAL to restrain from crime than punishment of death.’

11. One of the most comprehensive enquiries ever undertaken on the subject was that made by the Royal Commission on Capital Punishment… The Commission’s conclusions are of significance. They said: ‘THERE IS NO CLEAR EVIDENCE IN ANY OF THE FIGURES WE HAVE EXAMINED THAT THE ABOLITION OF CAPITAL PUNISHMENT HAS LED TO AN INCREASE IN THE HOMICIDE RATE, OR THAT ITS REINTRODUCTION [HAS LED] TO A FALL… prima facie the penalty of death is likely to have a stronger effect as a deterrent to normal human beings than any other form of punishment, and there is some evidence (though NO CONVINCING STATISTICAL EVIDENCE) that this is in fact so. BUT ITS EFFECT DOES NOT OPERATE UNIVERSALLY OR UNIFORMLY and there are many offenders on whom it is limited and may often be negligible. It is accordingly important to view this question in just perspective and NOT TO BASE A PENAL POLICY IN RELATION TO MURDER ON EXAGGERATED ESTIMATES OF THE UNIQUELY DETERRENT FORCE of the [death penalty].’”
The judgment — Part IV: what are “special reasons” under s.354(3)?
VERBATIM — paras 26–28 DU “[On s.360 and s.361 CrPC:] …If the Court refrains from dealing with an offender under Section 360 or under the provisions of the Probation of Offenders Act, or any other law for the treatment, training, or rehabilitation of youthful offenders, where the Court could have done so, SECTION 361, WHICH IS A NEW PROVISION IN THE 1973 CODE, MAKES IT MANDATORY FOR THE COURT TO RECORD IN ITS JUDGMENT THE ‘SPECIAL REASONS’ FOR NOT DOING SO. Section 361 thus casts a duty upon the Court to apply the provisions of Section 360 wherever it is possible to do so and to state ‘special reasons’ if it does not do so. In the context of Section 360, the ‘special reasons’ contemplated by Section 361 MUST BE SUCH AS TO COMPEL THE COURT TO HOLD THAT IT IS IMPOSSIBLE TO REFORM AND REHABILITATE THE OFFENDER after examining the matter with due regard to the age, character and antecedents of the offender and the circumstances in which the offence was committed. THIS IS SOME INDICATION BY THE LEGISLATURE THAT REFORMATION AND REHABILITATION OF OFFENDERS, AND NOT MERE DETERRENCE, ARE NOW AMONG THE FOREMOST OBJECTS OF THE ADMINISTRATION OF CRIMINAL JUSTICE IN OUR COUNTRY. SECTION 361 AND SECTION 354(3) HAVE BOTH ENTERED THE STATUTE BOOK AT THE SAME TIME AND THEY ARE PART OF THE EMERGING PICTURE OF ACCEPTANCE BY THE INDIAN PARLIAMENT OF THE NEW TRENDS IN CRIMINOLOGY. We will not, therefore, be wrong in assuming that THE PERSONALITY OF THE OFFENDER as revealed by his AGE, CHARACTER, ANTECEDENTS and other circumstances and THE TRACTABILITY OF THE OFFENDER TO REFORM must necessarily play THE MOST PROMINENT ROLE in determining the sentence to be awarded. Special reasons must have some relation to these factors.

27. CRIMINAL JUSTICE IS NOT A COMPUTER MACHINE. It deals with complex human problems and diverse human beings. It deals with persons who are OTHERWISE LIKE THE REST OF US, who work and play, who laugh and mourn, who love and hate, who yearn for affection and approval, as all of us do, who think, learn and forget. LIKE THE REST OF US THEY TOO ARE THE CREATURES OF CIRCUMSTANCE. Heredity, environment, home, neighbourhood, upbringing, school, friends, associates, even casual acquaintances, the books that one reads, newspapers, radio and TV, the economics of the household, the opportunities provided by circumstances and the calamities resulting therefrom, the success and failure of one’s undertakings, the affairs of the heart, ambitions and frustrations, the ideas and ideologies of the time — these and several other ordinary and extraordinary incidents of life contribute to a person’s personality and influence his conduct. Differently shaped and differently circumstanced individuals react differently in given situations. A JUDGE HAS TO BALANCE THE PERSONALITY OF THE OFFENDER WITH THE CIRCUMSTANCE, THE SITUATIONS AND THE REACTIONS AND CHOOSE THE APPROPRIATE SENTENCE TO BE IMPOSED. A judge must try to answer a myriad questions such as: was the offence committed without premeditation or was it after due deliberation? What was the motive for the crime? Was it for gain? Was it the outcome of a village feud? Was it the result of a petty, drunken, street brawl, or a domestic bickering between a hapless husband and a helpless wife? Was it due to sexual jealousy? Was the murder committed under some stress, emotional or otherwise? What is the background of the offender? What is his social and economic status? What is the level of his education or intelligence? Do his actions betray a particularly callous indifference towards the welfare of society or, on the other hand, do they show a great concern for humanity…? Is the offender so perpetually and constitutionally at war with society that there is no hope of ever reclaiming him from being a menace to society? Or is he a person who is patently amenable to reform? Well may one exclaim with Prof. Vrij: ‘What audacity is involved in these three tasks: to interpret life, [to] explain an act, [to] predict the latest inclination of a human mind.’

28. ‘SPECIAL REASONS’, we may, therefore, say, are reasons which are SPECIAL WITH REFERENCE TO THE OFFENDER, with reference to CONSTITUTIONAL AND LEGISLATIVE DIRECTIVES, and with reference to THE TIMES — that is, with reference to contemporary ideas in the fields of Criminology and connected sciences. SPECIAL REASONS ARE THOSE WHICH LEAD INEVITABLY TO THE CONCLUSION THAT THE OFFENDER IS BEYOND REDEMPTION, having due regard to his personality and proclivity, to the legislative policy of reformation of the offender and to the advances made in the methods of treatment etc. I will not attempt to catalogue any ‘special reasons’…”
Decision
VERBATIM — para 29 DU “We do not think that either the Sessions Judge or the High Court made the right approach to the question. THE SESSIONS JUDGE WAS WRONG IN IMPOSING THE SENTENCE OF DEATH WITHOUT EVEN A REFERENCE TO THE REASON WHY THE APPELLANT COMMITTED THE MURDER. THE OBSERVATION OF THE HIGH COURT THAT THE APPELLANT DESERVED NO MERCY BECAUSE HE SHOWED NO MERCY SMACKS VERY MUCH OF PUNISHMENT BY WAY OF RETRIBUTION. We have examined the facts of the case… From the vague evidence that is available we gather that the appellant was a MOODY PERSON who had for years been BROODING over the suspected infidelity of his wife and the injustice of having a son foisted on him. WE DO NOT THINK THAT THE MERE USE OF ADJECTIVES LIKE ‘CRUEL AND BRUTAL’ SUPPLIES THE SPECIAL REASONS CONTEMPLATED BY SECTION 354(3) CRIMINAL PROCEDURE CODE. In the light of the principles enunciated in Rajendra Prasad… we do not think that there are any ‘special reasons’ justifying the imposition of the death penalty. WE ACCORDINGLY ALLOW THE APPEAL AS REGARDS SENTENCE, SET ASIDE THE SENTENCE OF DEATH AND IMPOSE IN ITS PLACE THE SENTENCE OF LIFE IMPRISONMENT.”

KRISHNA IYER, J. (concurring): “I wholly agree with his reasoning and conclusion. Indeed, the ratio of Rajendra Prasad v. State of Uttar Pradesh, if applied to the present case, as it must be, leads to the conclusion that death sentence cannot be awarded in the circumstances of the present case… I concur with my learned brother and direct that the appeal, confined to sentence, be allowed and the alternative of life imprisonment imposed.”
Principles evolved
(1) “Special reasons” under s.354(3) are reasons SPECIAL TO THE OFFENDER, referable to constitutional and legislative directives and to contemporary criminological thought, and “lead inevitably to the conclusion that the offender is BEYOND REDEMPTION”. (2) Adjectives are not reasons. “The mere use of adjectives like ‘cruel and brutal’ [does not supply] the special reasons contemplated by Section 354(3).” This is the most practically useful holding in the case, and it is violated by trial courts every day. (3) Motive must be considered. A death sentence imposed “without even a reference to the reason why the appellant committed the murder” is unsustainable. (4) “He deserved no mercy because he showed no mercy” is retribution, not reasoning. (5) ss.354(3) and 361 must be read together as marking Parliament’s acceptance of reformation as among “the foremost objects of the administration of criminal justice”. (6) None of the theories of punishment supports death rather than life (§9.1); and on the evidence “the death penalty is inconsequential as a deterrent”. (7) “Criminal justice is not a computer machine” — the classic statement of individualised sentencing.
What Bachan Singh said about this case
Candidates must know this. Bachan Singh disapproved the reasoning in Bishnu Deo Shaw to the extent that it followed Rajendra Prasad on the “criminal only” approach and on Art. 19: “The same criticism applies to the view taken in Bishnu Deo Shaw v. State of West Bengal which follows the dictum in Rajendra Prasad” DU. But it did not disapprove — and indeed adopted — the case’s central insights: that life is the rule and death the exception, that the court must look at both crime and criminal, that mitigating factors must receive “a liberal and expansive construction”, and that reformation is among the foremost objects of criminal justice. State the disapproval expressly and then state what survives.

23. CASE 2 — Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980) DU verbatim

Bachan Singh v. State of PunjabAIR 1980 SC 898; (1980) 2 SCC 684 — CONSTITUTION BENCH (5 Judges): Y.V. Chandrachud C.J., A.C. Gupta, N.L. Untwalia, R.S. Sarkaria and P.N. Bhagwati JJ.; majority judgment by SARKARIA J., BHAGWATI J. dissentingReproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 186–210
Facts and how the reference arose
VERBATIM — paras 1–3 DU “1. This reference to the Constitution Bench raises a question in regard to the constitutional validity of death penalty for murder provided in Section 302, Penal Code, and the sentencing procedure embodied in Sub-section (3) of Section 354 of the CrPC, 1973.
2. …Bachan Singh, appellant in Criminal Appeal No. 273 of 1979, was tried and convicted and sentenced to death under Section 302, Indian Penal Code for the murders of Desa Singh, Durga Bai and Veeran Bai by the Sessions Judge. The High Court confirmed his death sentence and dismissed his appeal.
3. Bachan Singh’s appeal by special leave came up for hearing before a Bench of this Court (consisting of Sarkaria and Kailasam, JJ.). The only question for consideration in the appeal was, whether the facts found by the courts below would be ‘special reasons’ for awarding the death sentence as required under Section 354(3) of the CrPC, 1973.”
The critical facts of the appellant’s own case: he had been previously convicted of murder, and committed these three murders after serving out the life sentence in the earlier case.
The arguments that produced the reference
VERBATIM — paras 4–8 DU “4. Shri H.K. Puri, appearing as amicus curiae on behalf of the appellant, contended that in view of the ratio of Rajendra Prasad… the courts below were NOT COMPETENT to impose the extreme penalty of death on the appellant. It was submitted that NEITHER the circumstance that the appellant was previously convicted for murder and committed these murders after he had served out the life sentence in the earlier case, NOR the fact that these three murders were extremely heinous and inhuman, constitutes a ‘special reason’ for imposing the death sentence within the meaning of Section 354(3)…

5. Kailasam, J. was of [the] opinion that the majority view in Rajendra Prasad… was CONTRARY TO the judgment of the Constitution Bench in Jagmohan Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh, inter alia, on these aspects: … [(i) the characterisation of Jagmohan’s observations as ‘incidental observations without concentration on the sentencing criteria’, with the remark that ‘Judgments are not Bible for every line to be venerated’; (ii) the Art. 19(2)–(6) proposition; (iii) the proposition that ‘the right to life and the fundamental freedoms is deprived when he is hanged to death, his dignity is defiled when his neck is noosed and strangled’; (iv) the ‘bloodthirsty tiger’ passage; (v) the view that it is beyond the function of a court to evolve ‘working rules for [the] imposition of [the] death sentence bearing the markings of enlightened flexibility and social sensibility’ or to make law ‘by cross-fertilisation from sociology, history, cultural anthropology and current national perils and developmental goals and, above all, constitutional currents’ — ‘This function, in his view, belongs ONLY TO PARLIAMENT. The Court must administer the law as it stands’; and (vi) the view that ‘special reasons’ necessary for imposing [the] death penalty MUST RELATE NOT TO THE CRIME AS SUCH, BUT TO THE CRIMINAL, which Kailasam J. considered ‘not warranted by the law as it stands today’.]

7. Against the above, Kailasam, J. commented: The only change after the Constitution Bench delivered its judgment is the introduction of Section 354(3) which requires special reasons to be given if the court is to award the death sentence. IF WITHOUT THE RESTRICTION OF STATING SUFFICIENT REASONS DEATH SENTENCE COULD BE CONSTITUTIONALLY AWARDED under the IPC and CrPC as it stood before the amendment, IT IS DIFFICULT TO PERCEIVE HOW BY REQUIRING SPECIAL REASONS TO BE GIVEN THE AMENDED SECTION WOULD BE UNCONSTITUTIONAL unless the ‘sentencing sector is made restrictive and least vagarious’.”
Issues before the Constitution Bench
(1) Is the death penalty for murder under s.302 IPC unconstitutional as violative of Arts. 14, 19 and 21? (2) Is the sentencing procedure in s.354(3) CrPC unconstitutional for conferring unguided discretion? (3) Must the “special reasons” relate only to the criminal, as Rajendra Prasad held, or to both the crime and the criminal? (4) Should the Court lay down exhaustive standards for the imposition of the death sentence?
Arguments for the petitioners/appellants
(i) The death penalty serves no penological purpose that life imprisonment does not serve; it is therefore an unreasonable restriction on the right to life. (ii) It is arbitrary: two persons convicted of similar offences may receive different sentences, so Art. 14 is violated. (iii) The words “special reasons” give no guidance; the discretion is standardless, and after Furman v. Georgia such a scheme cannot survive. (iv) The provision offends Art. 19, because the deprivation of life destroys all the freedoms in Art. 19(1); and the restriction is not “reasonable”. (v) The 1973 Code marks a decisive legislative shift towards reformation (ss.354(3) and 361), and the “special reasons” must therefore relate to the criminal and to his capacity for reform.
Arguments for the State
(i) Jagmohan is a binding Constitution Bench decision that has already rejected the challenge under Arts. 14, 19 and 21. (ii) The introduction of s.354(3) and s.235(2) has strengthened, not weakened, the constitutionality of the provision, because it supplies precisely the procedural safeguards whose absence was complained of in Jagmohan. (iii) Standardisation is impossible and, in any event, a matter for Parliament, not for the courts. (iv) The Law Commission’s 35th Report, after full consideration, recommended retention. (v) Penal laws do not, in pith and substance, deal with the subject-matter of Art. 19(1) at all.
Decision — (a) on Article 19
The majority held that penal laws do not attract Art. 19(1). The reasoning, at paras 61–63 (reproduced in the DU material and set out in Topic 3, §4), is that “it cannot, reasonably or rationally, be contended that any of the rights mentioned in Article 19(1) of the Constitution confers the freedom to commit murder”; that the deprivation of freedom consequent on conviction “is not a direct and inevitable consequence of the penal law but is merely incidental”; and that this is “particularly true of crimes inherently vicious and pernicious, which under the English Common Law were classified as crimes MALA IN SE as distinguished from crimes MALA PROHIBITA” DU. Even assuming Art. 19 applied, the provision was held reasonable and in the general public interest.
Decision — (b) on standardisation: why the Court refused to lay down exhaustive standards
VERBATIM — paras 172–176 DU “[First,] According to Cesare Beccaria… ‘crimes are only to be measured by the injury done to society’. But the 20th century sociologists do not wholly agree with this view. In the opinion of Von Hirsch, the ‘seriousness of a crime depends both on the HARM done (or risked) by the act and [the] DEGREE OF THE ACTOR’S CULPABILITY’. But HOW IS THE DEGREE OF THAT CULPABILITY TO BE MEASURED? CAN ANY THERMOMETER BE DEVISED TO MEASURE ITS DEGREE? This is [a] very baffling, difficult and intricate problem.

173. Secondly, criminal cases DO NOT FALL INTO SET BEHAVIOURISTIC PATTERNS. Even within a single-category offence there are INFINITE, UNPREDICTABLE AND UNFORESEEABLE VARIATIONS. NO TWO CASES ARE EXACTLY IDENTICAL… Each case presents its own distinctive features, its peculiar combination of events and its unique configuration of facts. ‘Simply in terms of blameworthiness or desert, criminal cases are different from one another in ways that legislatures cannot anticipate, and limitations of language prevent the precise description of differences that can be anticipated’ (Messinger and Bittner). This is particularly true of murder. ‘THERE IS PROBABLY NO OFFENCE THAT VARIES SO WIDELY BOTH IN CHARACTER AND IN MORAL GUILT AS THAT WHICH FALLS WITHIN THE LEGAL DEFINITION OF MURDER’ — Sir Ernest Gowers, Chairman of the Royal Commission.

174. Thirdly, a STANDARDISATION of the sentencing process which leaves little room for judicial discretion to take account of variations in culpability within [a] single-offence category CEASES TO BE JUDICIAL. It tends to SACRIFICE JUSTICE AT THE ALTAR OF BLIND UNIFORMITY. Indeed, there is a real danger of such mechanical standardisation degenerating into A BED OF PROCRUSTEAN CRUELTY.

175. Fourthly, standardisation of sentencing discretion is A POLICY MATTER WHICH BELONGS TO THE SPHERE OF LEGISLATION. When Parliament, as a matter of sound legislative policy, did not deliberately restrict, control or standardise the sentencing discretion any further than that [which] is encompassed by the broad contours delineated in Section 354(3), the Court would not, by over-leaping its bounds, rush to do what Parliament, in its wisdom, warily did not do.

176. WE MUST LEAVE UNTO THE LEGISLATURE, THE THINGS THAT ARE LEGISLATURE’S. ‘The highest judicial duty is to recognise the limits on judicial power and to permit the democratic processes to deal with matters falling outside of those limits.’ As Judges, we have to resist the temptation to substitute our own value choices for the will of the people… When Judges… take upon themselves the responsibility of setting down social norms of conduct, there is every danger… that they might write their own peculiar view or personal predilection into the law, sincerely mistaking that changeling for what they perceive to be the community ethic. The perception of ‘community’ standards or ethics MAY VARY FROM JUDGE TO JUDGE… JUDGES HAVE NO DIVINING ROD TO DIVINE ACCURATELY THE WILL OF THE PEOPLE. In Furman, the Hon’ble Judges claimed to articulate the contemporary standards of morality among the American people. But, speaking through public referenda, Gallup polls and the state legislatures, THE AMERICAN PEOPLE SHARPLY REBUFFED THEM. We must draw a lesson from the same.”
Decision — (c) crime AND criminal: the rejection of Rajendra Prasad
VERBATIM — paras 195–199 DU “195. In Jagmohan, this Court had held that this sentencing discretion is to be exercised judicially on well-recognised principles, AFTER BALANCING ALL THE AGGRAVATING AND MITIGATING CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CRIME… The only effect [of the legislative changes] is that the application of those principles is now to be guided by the paramount beacons of legislative policy discernible from Sections 354(3) and 235(2), namely: (1) THE EXTREME PENALTY CAN BE INFLICTED ONLY IN GRAVEST CASES OF EXTREME CULPABILITY; (2) IN MAKING [THE] CHOICE OF THE SENTENCE, IN ADDITION TO THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE OFFENCE, DUE REGARD MUST BE PAID TO THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE OFFENDER ALSO.

197. Pre-planned, calculated, cold-blooded murder has always been regarded as one of an aggravated kind. In Jagmohan, it was reiterated… that if a murder is ‘diabolically conceived and cruelly executed’ it would justify the imposition of the death penalty… The same principle was substantially reiterated by V.R. Krishna Iyer, J. in Ediga Anamma: ‘The weapons used and the manner of their use, the horrendous features of the crime and [the] hapless, helpless state of the victim, and the like, steel the heart of the law for a sterner sentence.’

198. …In Rajendra Prasad, however, the majority (of 2:1) has completely reversed the view that had been taken in Ediga Anamma… According to it, after the enactment of Section 354(3), ‘murder most foul’ is not the test. The shocking nature of the crime or the number of murders committed is also not the criterion. It was said that the focus has now completely shifted from the crime to the criminal. ‘Special reasons’ necessary for imposing [the] death penalty ‘must relate not to the crime as such but to the criminal’.

199. With great respect, WE FIND OURSELVES UNABLE TO AGREE to this enunciation. As we read Sections 354(3) and 235(2)… it is quite clear to us that for making the choice of punishment or for ascertaining the existence or absence of ‘special reasons’… THE COURT MUST PAY DUE REGARD BOTH TO THE CRIME AND THE CRIMINAL. What is the relative weight to be given to the aggravating and mitigating factors DEPENDS ON THE FACTS AND CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE PARTICULAR CASE. More often than not, these two aspects are SO INTERTWINED that it is difficult to give a separate treatment to each of them. This is so because ‘STYLE IS THE MAN’. In many cases, the extremely cruel or beastly manner of the commission of murder is itself A DEMONSTRATED INDEX OF THE DEPRAVED CHARACTER OF THE PERPETRATOR. That is why IT IS NOT DESIRABLE TO CONSIDER THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CRIME AND THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CRIMINAL IN TWO SEPARATE WATER-TIGHT COMPARTMENTS. In a sense, TO KILL IS TO BE CRUEL AND THEREFORE ALL MURDERS ARE CRUEL. But such cruelty may vary in its degree of culpability. AND IT IS ONLY WHEN THE CULPABILITY ASSUMES THE PROPORTION OF EXTREME DEPRAVITY THAT ‘SPECIAL REASONS’ CAN LEGITIMATELY BE SAID TO EXIST.”
Decision — (d) THE AGGRAVATING CIRCUMSTANCES (para 200, per Dr. Chitaley)
VERBATIM — para 200 DU “Drawing upon the penal statutes of the States in U.S.A. framed after Furman v. Georgia, in general, and Clauses 2(a), (b), (c) and (d) of the Indian Penal Code (Amendment) Bill passed in 1978 by the Rajya Sabha, in particular, Dr. Chitale has suggested these ‘aggravating circumstances’:
Aggravating circumstances: A Court may, however, in the following cases impose the penalty of death in its discretion:
(a) if the murder has been committed AFTER PREVIOUS PLANNING AND INVOLVES EXTREME BRUTALITY; or
(b) if the murder involves EXCEPTIONAL DEPRAVITY; or
(c) if the murder is of a member of any of the ARMED FORCES of the Union or of a member of any POLICE FORCE or of any PUBLIC SERVANT and was committed — (i) while such member or public servant was ON DUTY; or (ii) in consequence of anything done or attempted to be done by such member or public servant in the lawful discharge of his duty… whether at the time of murder he was such member or public servant… or had ceased to be such…; or
(d) if the murder is of a person who had ACTED IN THE LAWFUL DISCHARGE OF HIS DUTY under Section 43 of the CrPC, 1973, or who had rendered assistance to a Magistrate or a police officer demanding his aid or requiring his assistance under Section 37 and Section 129 of the said Code.

201. Stated broadly, there can be no objection to the acceptance of these indicators, but… we would prefer NOT TO FETTER JUDICIAL DISCRETION by attempting to make an exhaustive enumeration one way or the other. …203. …No exhaustive enumeration of aggravating circumstances is possible. But this much can be said that in order to qualify for inclusion in the category of ‘aggravating circumstances’ which may form the basis of ‘special reasons’ in Section 354(3), [a] circumstance found on the facts of a particular case MUST EVIDENCE AGGRAVATION OF AN ABNORMAL OR SPECIAL DEGREE.”
Decision — (e) THE MITIGATING CIRCUMSTANCES (para 204)
VERBATIM — paras 204–207 DU “204. Dr. Chitaley has suggested these mitigating factors:
Mitigating circumstances: In the exercise of its discretion in the above cases, the Court shall take into account the following circumstances:
(1) That the offence was committed under the influence of EXTREME MENTAL OR EMOTIONAL DISTURBANCE.
(2) THE AGE OF THE ACCUSED. If the accused is young or old, he shall not be sentenced to death.
(3) The probability that the accused WOULD NOT COMMIT CRIMINAL ACTS OF VIOLENCE AS WOULD CONSTITUTE A CONTINUING THREAT TO SOCIETY.
(4) The probability that THE ACCUSED CAN BE REFORMED AND REHABILITATED. THE STATE SHALL BY EVIDENCE PROVE THAT THE ACCUSED DOES NOT SATISFY THE CONDITIONS 3 AND 4 ABOVE.
(5) That in the facts and circumstances of the case the accused BELIEVED THAT HE WAS MORALLY JUSTIFIED in committing the offence.
(6) That the accused ACTED UNDER THE DURESS OR DOMINATION OF ANOTHER PERSON.
(7) That the condition of the accused showed that HE WAS MENTALLY DEFECTIVE and that the said defect IMPAIRED HIS CAPACITY TO APPRECIATE THE CRIMINALITY of his conduct.

205. We will do no more than to say that these are undoubtedly RELEVANT CIRCUMSTANCES AND MUST BE GIVEN GREAT WEIGHT in the determination of sentence. Some of these factors like EXTREME YOUTH can instead be of COMPELLING IMPORTANCE.…

206. According to some Indian decisions, the post-murder remorse, penitence or repentance by the murderer is not a factor which may induce the Court to pass the lesser penalty… But those decisions can NO LONGER be held to be good law in view of the current penological trends and the sentencing policy outlined in Sections 235(2) and 354(3).

207. There are numerous other circumstances justifying the passing of the lighter sentence… ‘We cannot obviously feed into a judicial computer all such situations since there are astrological imponderables in an imperfect and undulating society.’ Nonetheless, it cannot be over-emphasised that THE SCOPE AND CONCEPT OF MITIGATING FACTORS IN THE AREA OF DEATH PENALTY MUST RECEIVE A LIBERAL AND EXPANSIVE CONSTRUCTION BY THE COURTS in accord with the sentencing policy writ large in Section 354(3). JUDGES SHOULD NEVER BE BLOODTHIRSTY. HANGING OF MURDERERS HAS NEVER BEEN TOO GOOD FOR THEM. Facts and figures, albeit incomplete, furnished by the Union of India, show that in the past Courts have inflicted the extreme penalty WITH EXTREME INFREQUENCY — a fact which attests to the caution and compassion which they have always brought to bear on the exercise of their sentencing discretion in so grave a matter. It is, therefore, imperative to voice the concern that courts, aided by the BROAD ILLUSTRATIVE GUIDELINES indicated by us, will discharge the onerous function with evermore scrupulous care and humane concern, directed along the highroad of legislative policy outlined in Section 354(3), viz., THAT FOR PERSONS CONVICTED OF MURDER, LIFE IMPRISONMENT IS THE RULE AND DEATH SENTENCE AN EXCEPTION. A REAL AND ABIDING CONCERN FOR THE DIGNITY OF HUMAN LIFE POSTULATES RESISTANCE TO TAKING A LIFE THROUGH LAW’S INSTRUMENTALITY. THAT OUGHT NOT TO BE DONE SAVE IN THE RAREST OF RARE CASES WHEN THE ALTERNATIVE OPTION IS UNQUESTIONABLY FORECLOSED.”
The order
VERBATIM — paras 208–213 DU “208. For all the foregoing reasons, we reject the challenge to the constitutionality of the impugned provisions contained in Sections 302 Penal Code and 354(3) of the CrPC, 1973.

BHAGWATI, J. (Minority) — 210. …I am of the view that Section 302 of the Indian Penal Code in so far as it provides for imposition of death penalty as an alternative to life sentence is ULTRA VIRES AND VOID as being violative of Articles 14 and 21 of the Constitution SINCE IT DOES NOT PROVIDE ANY LEGISLATIVE GUIDELINES AS TO WHEN LIFE SHOULD BE PERMITTED TO BE EXTINGUISHED BY IMPOSITION OF DEATH SENTENCE. 211. I would therefore STRIKE DOWN SECTION 302 as unconstitutional and void in so far as it provides for imposition of death penalty as an alternative to imprisonment for life. I shall give my reasons for this view on the day on which the Court reopens after the summer vacation.

ORDER — 212. In accordance with the majority opinion the challenge to the constitutionality of Section 302 of the Penal Code in so far as it provides for the death sentence as also the challenge to the constitutionality of Section 354(3) of the CrPC, 1973 fails and is rejected.”
(Bhagwati J.’s full dissent was delivered in 1982 and is reported at (1982) 3 SCC 24.)
Principles evolved — the seven propositions of Bachan Singh
(1) The death penalty for murder under s.302 IPC and the procedure in s.354(3) CrPC are CONSTITUTIONAL; they do not violate Arts. 14, 19 or 21. (2) “LIFE IMPRISONMENT IS THE RULE AND DEATH SENTENCE AN EXCEPTION.” (3) Death may be imposed only “in the RAREST OF RARE CASES when the alternative option is UNQUESTIONABLY FORECLOSED” — a two-limbed test: the case must be rarest of rare, and life imprisonment must be shown to be unquestionably inadequate. (4) The court must have regard BOTH to the CRIME AND to the CRIMINAL; Rajendra Prasad’s “criminal only” approach is wrong, and the two cannot be placed in “water-tight compartments”. (5) A balance sheet of aggravating and mitigating circumstances must be drawn; the illustrative lists in paras 200 and 204 are “relevant circumstances [which] must be given great weight”, but are not exhaustive and do not fetter discretion. (6) Mitigating circumstances must receive “a LIBERAL AND EXPANSIVE CONSTRUCTION”; and — the most under-used holding — “THE STATE SHALL BY EVIDENCE PROVE” that the accused is a continuing threat and cannot be reformed. The burden is on the prosecution, a point given full effect in Bariyar (§31). (7) The Court will not lay down exhaustive standards: standardisation “ceases to be judicial”, risks “a bed of Procrustean cruelty”, and “belongs to the sphere of legislation”.
Criticism
(i) Bhagwati J.’s dissent remains unanswered: a discretion without legislative guidelines is, on ordinary Art. 14 principles, arbitrary. (ii) The Court’s refusal to standardise, combined with the illustrative lists, produced the worst of both worlds — “guidelines” that are neither binding nor exhaustive. (iii) The proof requirement in mitigating factors (3) and (4) has been almost universally ignored by trial courts. (iv) Sangeet v. State of Haryana (2013) 2 SCC 452 held that the balance sheet has not been applied as intended and that courts have drifted into a “crime-centric” rather than “crime-and-criminal-centric” approach; and that Machhi Singh’s categories have been treated as a substitute for the Bachan Singh test (§33). (v) The NLUD data (§19.2) shows the discretion operating along lines of class and community.

24. CASE 3 — Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab (1983) DU verbatim

Machhi Singh v. State of PunjabAIR 1983 SC 957; (1983) 3 SCC 470 — M.P. Thakkar, J.Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 211–215
The opening — the synthesis
VERBATIM — para 1 DU “1. Protagonists of the ‘AN EYE FOR AN EYE’ philosophy demand ‘DEATH-FOR-DEATH’. The ‘HUMANISTS’ on the other hand press for the other extreme, viz., ‘DEATH-IN-NO-CASE’. A SYNTHESIS HAS EMERGED IN BACHAN SINGH v. STATE OF PUNJAB wherein the ‘RAREST-OF-RARE-CASE’ FORMULA for imposing death sentence in a murder case has been evolved by this Court. IDENTIFICATION OF THE GUIDELINES spelled out in ‘Bachan Singh’ in order to determine whether or not death sentence should be imposed is one of the problems engaging our attention…”
Facts
VERBATIM — paras 2–3 DU “2. A FEUD BETWEEN TWO FAMILIES has resulted in tragic consequences. SEVENTEEN LIVES WERE LOST in the course of A SERIES OF FIVE INCIDENTS which occurred in quick succession in FIVE DIFFERENT VILLAGES, situated in the vicinity of each other in Punjab, on a night one would like to forget but cannot forget, THE NIGHT BETWEEN AUGUST 12 AND AUGUST 13, 1977. The seventeen persons who lost their lives and the three who sustained injuries included MEN, WOMEN AND CHILDREN related to one Amar Singh and his sister Piaro Bai.

3. In this connection one Machhi Singh and his eleven companions, close relatives and associates, were prosecuted in FIVE SESSIONS CASES, each pertaining to the concerned village in which the killings took place. Machhi Singh was the common accused at each trial… At the conclusion of the series of trials the accused found guilty were convicted under appropriate provisions. FOUR of them were awarded [the] death sentence, whereas [a] sentence of imprisonment for life was imposed on NINE of them… The order of conviction and sentence gave rise to five murder references and fourteen appeals by the convicts before the High Court of Punjab and Haryana…”
Motive: “Suffice it to say that REPRISAL was the motive for the commission of the crime.”
The “common criticism” on the evidence — worth knowing
VERBATIM — paras 5–6 DU “The criticism is this. It was a dark night. Electricity had not yet reached the concerned village at the material time. In each crime the appreciation of evidence regarding identification has to be made in the context of the fact-situation that A LIGHTED LANTERN was hanging in the court-yard where the victims were sleeping on the cots. The light shed by the lantern cannot be considered to be sufficient enough… to enable the eye witnesses to identify the culprits. This argument has been rightly rebuffed… on the ground that villagers living in villages where electricity has not reached as yet GET ACCUSTOMED TO SEEING THINGS IN THE LIGHT SHED BY THE LANTERN. THEIR EYESIGHT GETS CONDITIONED AND BECOMES ACCUSTOMED TO THE SITUATION. Moreover, identification did not pose any serious problem AS THE ACCUSED WERE KNOWN TO THE WITNESSES. In fact they were embroiled in a long standing FAMILY FEUD. As the culprits had not covered their faces to conceal their identity, it was not difficult to identify them from their FACIAL FEATURES, BUILD, GAIT etc.”
Issue
What are the guidelines, culled from Bachan Singh, for determining whether a case falls within the “rarest of rare” category; and do the facts of these appeals satisfy them?
Decision — (a) the philosophical foundation: why the community sanctions death
VERBATIM — para 32 DU “32. The reasons why the community as a whole does not endorse the humanistic approach reflected in ‘death sentence-in-no-case’ doctrine are not far to seek. In the first place, THE VERY HUMANISTIC EDIFICE IS CONSTRUCTED ON THE FOUNDATION OF ‘REVERENCE FOR LIFE’ PRINCIPLE. WHEN A MEMBER OF THE COMMUNITY VIOLATES THIS VERY PRINCIPLE BY KILLING ANOTHER MEMBER, THE SOCIETY MAY NOT FEEL ITSELF BOUND BY THE SHACKLES OF THIS DOCTRINE. Secondly, it has to be realized that every member of the community is able to live with safety without his or her own life being endangered because of THE PROTECTIVE ARM OF THE COMMUNITY and on account of THE RULE OF LAW enforced by it… EVERY MEMBER OF THE COMMUNITY OWES A DEBT TO THE COMMUNITY FOR THIS PROTECTION. WHEN INGRATITUDE IS SHOWN INSTEAD OF GRATITUDE BY ‘KILLING’ A MEMBER OF THE COMMUNITY WHICH PROTECTS THE MURDERER HIMSELF FROM BEING KILLED, OR WHEN THE COMMUNITY FEELS THAT FOR THE SAKE OF SELF-PRESERVATION THE KILLER HAS TO BE KILLED, THE COMMUNITY MAY WELL WITHDRAW THE PROTECTION BY SANCTIONING THE DEATH PENALTY. BUT THE COMMUNITY WILL NOT DO SO IN EVERY CASE. IT MAY DO SO (IN [THE] RAREST OF RARE CASE[S]) WHEN ITS COLLECTIVE CONSCIENCE IS SO SHOCKED THAT IT WILL EXPECT THE HOLDERS OF THE JUDICIAL POWER CENTRE TO INFLICT DEATH PENALTY IRRESPECTIVE OF THEIR PERSONAL OPINION AS REGARDS DESIRABILITY OR OTHERWISE OF RETAINING DEATH PENALTY. The community may entertain such a sentiment when the crime is viewed from the platform of the MOTIVE for, or the MANNER of commission of the crime, or the ANTI-SOCIAL OR ABHORRENT NATURE of the crime, such as for instance:”
Decision — (b) THE FIVE CATEGORIES
VERBATIM — paras 33–36 DU “I — MANNER OF COMMISSION OF MURDER
33. When the murder is committed in an EXTREMELY BRUTAL, GROTESQUE, DIABOLICAL, REVOLTING, OR DASTARDLY MANNER so as to arouse INTENSE AND EXTREME INDIGNATION of the community. For instance,
(i) When THE HOUSE OF THE VICTIM IS SET AFLAME with the end in view to roast him alive in the house.
(ii) When the victim is subjected to INHUMAN ACTS OF TORTURE OR CRUELTY in order to bring about his or her death.
(iii) When THE BODY OF THE VICTIM IS CUT INTO PIECES or his body is DISMEMBERED in a fiendish manner.

II — MOTIVE FOR COMMISSION OF MURDER
34. When the murder is committed for a motive which EVINCES TOTAL DEPRAVITY AND MEANNESS. For instance when (a) A HIRED ASSASSIN commits murder for the sake of money or reward; (b) a COLD-BLOODED MURDER is committed with a deliberate design in order to INHERIT PROPERTY or to gain control over property of a ward or a person under the control of the murderer or vis-à-vis whom the murderer is IN A DOMINATING POSITION OR IN A POSITION OF TRUST; (c) a murder is committed in the course [of] BETRAYAL OF THE MOTHERLAND.

III — ANTI-SOCIAL OR SOCIALLY ABHORRENT NATURE OF THE CRIME
(a) When murder of a SCHEDULED CASTE OR MINORITY COMMUNITY etc. is committed NOT FOR PERSONAL REASONS but in circumstances which AROUSE SOCIAL WRATH. For instance when such a crime is committed in order to TERRORIZE such persons and frighten them into FLEEING FROM A PLACE or in order to deprive them of… [their rights, or to reverse] past injustices and in order to restore the social balance.
(b) In cases of ‘BRIDE BURNING’ and what are known as ‘DOWRY-DEATHS’ or when murder is committed in order to REMARRY for the sake of extracting dowry once again or to marry another woman on account of INFATUATION.

IV — MAGNITUDE OF CRIME
35. When the crime is ENORMOUS IN PROPORTION. For instance when MULTIPLE MURDERS, say of all or almost all the members of a family or a large number of persons of a particular caste, community, or locality, are committed.

V — PERSONALITY OF [THE] VICTIM OF MURDER
36. When the victim of murder is (a) an INNOCENT CHILD who could not have or has not provided even an excuse, much less a provocation, for murder; (b) a HELPLESS WOMAN or a person rendered helpless by OLD AGE OR INFIRMITY; (c) when the victim is a person vis-à-vis whom the murderer is IN A POSITION OF DOMINATION OR TRUST; (d) when the victim is a PUBLIC FIGURE generally loved and respected by the community for the services rendered by him and the murder is committed for POLITICAL OR SIMILAR REASONS other than personal reasons.”
Decision — (c) THE FOUR PROPOSITIONS FROM BACHAN SINGH
VERBATIM — para 37 DU “37. In this background the guidelines indicated in Bachan Singh case will have to be culled out and applied to the facts of each individual case… The following propositions emerge from Bachan Singh case:
(i) THE EXTREME PENALTY OF DEATH NEED NOT BE INFLICTED EXCEPT IN GRAVEST CASES OF EXTREME CULPABILITY;
(ii) BEFORE OPTING FOR THE DEATH PENALTY THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE ‘OFFENDER’ ALSO REQUIRE TO BE TAKEN INTO CONSIDERATION ALONGWITH THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE ‘CRIME’;
(iii) LIFE IMPRISONMENT IS THE RULE AND DEATH SENTENCE IS AN EXCEPTION. In other words DEATH SENTENCE MUST BE IMPOSED ONLY WHEN LIFE IMPRISONMENT APPEARS TO BE AN ALTOGETHER INADEQUATE PUNISHMENT having regard to the relevant circumstances of the crime, and provided, and only provided, THE OPTION TO IMPOSE SENTENCE OF IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE CANNOT BE CONSCIENTIOUSLY EXERCISED having regard to the nature and circumstances of the crime and all the relevant circumstances;
(iv) A BALANCE SHEET OF AGGRAVATING AND MITIGATING CIRCUMSTANCES HAS TO BE DRAWN UP AND IN DOING SO THE MITIGATING CIRCUMSTANCES HAVE TO BE ACCORDED FULL WEIGHTAGE AND A JUST BALANCE HAS TO BE STRUCK BETWEEN THE AGGRAVATING AND THE MITIGATING CIRCUMSTANCES BEFORE THE OPTION IS EXERCISED.”
Decision — (d) THE TWO QUESTIONS
VERBATIM — paras 38–39 DU “38. In order to apply these guidelines, inter alia, THE FOLLOWING QUESTIONS MAY BE ASKED AND ANSWERED:
(a) IS THERE SOMETHING UNCOMMON ABOUT THE CRIME WHICH RENDERS SENTENCE OF IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE INADEQUATE AND CALLS FOR A DEATH SENTENCE?
(b) ARE THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CRIME SUCH THAT THERE IS NO ALTERNATIVE BUT TO IMPOSE DEATH SENTENCE EVEN AFTER ACCORDING MAXIMUM WEIGHTAGE TO THE MITIGATING CIRCUMSTANCES WHICH SPEAK IN FAVOUR OF THE OFFENDER?

39. If upon taking AN OVERALL GLOBAL VIEW of all the circumstances in the light of the aforesaid proposition[s] and taking into account the answers to the questions posed hereinabove, the circumstances of the case are such that death sentence is warranted, the court would proceed to do so.”
The result
The Court applied the test to the individual appellants. In relation to Machhi Singh it quoted, with approval, the High Court’s reasons — that “it was a cold-blooded murder and the victims were helpless and undefended. And what was their fault, except that they were the immediate family of Amar Singh[?] THE OFFENCE COMMITTED WAS OF AN EXCEPTIONALLY DEPRAVED AND HEINOUS CHARACTER. THE MANNER OF ITS EXECUTION AND ITS DESIGN WOULD PUT IT AT THE LEVEL OF EXTREME ATROCITY AND CRUELTY. The deceased woman and her children had offered no offence to Machhi Singh…” The death sentences of Machhi Singh, Kashmir Singh and Jagir Singh were considered; the Court confirmed the death sentence on Machhi Singh (who was the prime mover, present at each of the five incidents, and responsible for multiple killings) and commuted the sentences of the others to imprisonment for life, applying the test individually — which is itself the best illustration of the doctrine in operation.
Principles evolved
(1) The FIVE CATEGORIES — manner, motive, anti-social nature, magnitude and personality of the victim — the most cited list in Indian criminal law. Learn them as M-M-A-M-P. (2) The FOUR PROPOSITIONS culled from Bachan Singh, including that death is warranted only when “the option to impose sentence of imprisonment for life cannot be conscientiously exercised”. (3) The TWO QUESTIONS — the practical operative test, which every sentencing judgment is expected to answer expressly. (4) The rationale of the “collective conscience” — the community withdraws its protection where its conscience is “so shocked” — and the important qualification that the judge must act “IRRESPECTIVE OF [HIS] PERSONAL OPINION as regards [the] desirability or otherwise of retaining [the] death penalty”. (5) An “overall global view” must be taken; the categories are illustrative, not a checklist.
Criticism — essential for a good answer
(i) Machhi Singh is a gloss, not a substitute. Sangeet v. State of Haryana (2013) 2 SCC 452 held that the categories have come to be applied as a self-contained code, displacing the Bachan Singh balance sheet, and that this has produced a “crime-centric” rather than a “crime-and-criminal-centric” approach. (ii) The “collective conscience” criterion imports public and media sentiment into a judicial decision and is incapable of objective determination — the very vice Bachan Singh warned of when it said judges “have no divining rod to divine accurately the will of the people”. (iii) Bariyar (2009) pointed out that Machhi Singh expanded the Bachan Singh categories of aggravation while saying nothing new about mitigation, so that the balance sheet became lopsided. (iv) Shankar Kisanrao Khade v. State of Maharashtra (2013) 5 SCC 546 added a third test — the “rarest of rare test”, the “crime test” and the “criminal test” must all be satisfied, and even then the judge must ask whether “the society will approve the awarding of death sentence”.

25. CASE 4 — Allauddin Mian v. State of Bihar (1989) DU verbatim

Allauddin Mian & Sharif Mian v. State of BiharAIR 1989 SC 1456; (1989) 3 SCC 5 — A.M. Ahmadi and S. Natarajan, JJ.Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 216–221
Facts
VERBATIM DU “Briefly stated the prosecution case is that on the afternoon of 25th July, 1985 around 4.30 p.m., when PW 6 Baharan Mian was sitting at the entrance of his house, the aforesaid six accused persons came from the west armed with deadly weapons; accused Nos. 1 and 2 were carrying ‘farsas’, accused Nos. 3 and 4 were armed with spears (bhalas) and accused Nos. 5 and 6 were armed with sticks (lathis). On seeing them PW 6 got up and went to the ‘osra’ (verandah)… Accused No. 3 began to untie the buffalo tethered in front of the house while the other accused persons showered abuses on PW 6, to which the latter objected. Thereupon, accused Nos. 4 and 6 shouted ‘Sale ko jan se mar do’. Immediately thereafter, accused Nos. 1 and 2 moved menacingly towards PW 6. The two infants Sahana Khatoon and Chand Tara were then playing in the ‘dalan’… On seeing accused Nos. 1 and 2 approaching him fully armed with farsas, PW 6 apprehended trouble and ran into the adjoining room to arm himself with a spear. His wife, PW 5 Laila Khatun… prevented him from going out for fear that he may be done to death. Realising that PW 6 had entered the inner room and was prevented by his wife from coming out, accused No. 1 gave farsa blows on the head, abdomen and left thumb of Sahana Khatoon… Accused No. 2 gave one farsa blow on the head of infant Chand Tara. The neighbours… intervened, pacified the assailants and sent them away… Sahana Khatoon died shortly after she was admitted to the dispensary. Her younger sister Chand Tara succumbed to her injuries on 23rd August, 1985.”
The trial court convicted accused Nos. 1 and 2 (Allauddin Mian and Keyamuddin Mian) of murder and sentenced them to death; the High Court confirmed. Critically, the trial court “recorded the finding of guilt on 31st March, 1987, [and] ON THE SAME DAY, before the accused could absorb and overcome the shock of conviction, they were asked if they had anything to say on the question of sentence, and IMMEDIATELY THEREAFTER the decision imposing the death penalty on the two accused was pronounced.”
Issues
(1) Is the requirement of a pre-sentence hearing under s.235(2) CrPC mandatory, and what does compliance require? (2) Does the death penalty violate Arts. 14, 19 and 21? (3) Do the facts disclose “special reasons” and a “rarest of rare” case?
Arguments for the appellants (Mr. R.K. Garg)
(i) Basing his submission “on what is described as the HUMANITARIAN IDEOLOGY or the REHABILITARIAN PHILOSOPHY”, that “any law which permits the supreme right to life being sacrificed FOR THE FAILURE OF THE STATE TO ESTABLISH A SOCIAL ORDER in which such crimes are not committed must be struck down as offending Articles 14, 19 and 21 of the Constitution”. (ii) That Bachan Singh “needs re-consideration as the learned Judges constituting the majority did not have the benefit of the views of Bhagwati, J., who ruled to the contrary”. (iii) That the trial court had treated s.235(2) as a mere formality, and that the accused had never had a real opportunity to place mitigating material. (iv) That, on the facts, the case was not rarest of rare: the motive was obscure, the killing of the infants was not the object, and the targets had been the father.
Arguments for the State
That the murders were of two helpless infants, committed without provocation and in cold blood, in a barbarous manner; that they fell squarely within Machhi Singh category V(a) (“an innocent child who could not have… provided even an excuse, much less a provocation”) and category I (manner); and that the constitutional challenge was concluded against the appellants by Bachan Singh.
Decision — (a) the constitutional challenge
VERBATIM DU “While rejecting the demand of the protagonist[s] of the reformatory theory for the abolition of the death penalty, the legislature in its wisdom thought that the ‘SPECIAL REASONS CLAUSE’ SHOULD BE A SUFFICIENT SAFEGUARD AGAINST ARBITRARY IMPOSITION OF THE EXTREME PENALTY.… In the face of this statutory provision which is consistent with Article 21 of the Constitution… we are unable to countenance counsel’s extreme submission of ‘death in no case’. The submission that the death penalty violates Articles 14, 19 and 21 of the Constitution was negatived by this Court in Bachan Singh… We are not impressed by this submission for the simple reason that the reasons which prevailed with Bhagwati, J. could not have been unknown to the learned Judges constituting the majority.”
Decision — (b) the duty to give reasons: the most quoted passage
VERBATIM DU “Section 302, IPC casts a heavy duty on the Court to choose between death and imprisonment for life. When the Court is called upon to choose between the convict’s cry ‘I WANT TO LIVE’ and the prosecutor’s demand ‘HE DESERVES TO DIE’, it goes without saying that the Court must show A HIGH DEGREE OF CONCERN AND SENSITIVENESS in the choice of sentence.…

This provision [s.354(3)] makes it obligatory… to assign reasons in support of the sentence awarded to the convict, and further ordains that in case the Judge awards the death penalty, ‘SPECIAL REASONS’ for such sentence shall be stated in the judgment. WHEN THE LAW CASTS A DUTY ON THE JUDGE TO STATE REASONS IT FOLLOWS THAT HE IS UNDER A LEGAL OBLIGATION TO EXPLAIN HIS CHOICE OF THE SENTENCE.… Where a sentence of severity is imposed, it is imperative that the Judge should indicate the basis upon which he considers a sentence of that magnitude justified. UNLESS THERE ARE SPECIAL REASONS, SPECIAL TO THE FACTS OF THE PARTICULAR CASE, WHICH CAN BE CATALOGUED AS JUSTIFYING A SEVERE PUNISHMENT, THE JUDGE WOULD NOT AWARD THE DEATH SENTENCE. IT MAY BE STATED THAT IF A JUDGE FINDS THAT HE IS UNABLE TO EXPLAIN WITH REASONABLE ACCURACY THE BASIS FOR SELECTING THE HIGHER OF THE TWO SENTENCES, HIS CHOICE SHOULD FALL ON THE LOWER SENTENCE.… It must at once be conceded that offenders of some particularly grossly brutal crimes which send tremors in the community have to be firmly dealt with to protect the community… Where the incidence of a certain crime is rapidly growing and is assuming menacing proportions, for example, ACID POURING or BRIDE BURNING, it may be necessary for the Courts to award exemplary punishments to protect the community and to deter others…”
Decision — (c) s.235(2): the pre-sentence hearing is MANDATORY
VERBATIM DU “The choice has to be made after following the procedure set out in sub-section (2) of Section 235 of the Code… THE REQUIREMENT OF HEARING THE ACCUSED IS INTENDED TO SATISFY THE ‘RULE OF NATURAL JUSTICE’. IT IS A FUNDAMENTAL REQUIREMENT OF FAIRPLAY that the accused who was hitherto concentrating on the prosecution evidence on the question of guilt should, on being found guilty, BE ASKED IF HE HAS ANYTHING TO SAY OR ANY EVIDENCE TO TENDER ON THE QUESTION OF SENTENCE. This is all the more necessary since the Courts are generally required to make the choice from a wide range of discretion in the matter of sentencing. …The said provision therefore satisfies A DUAL PURPOSE: IT SATISFIES THE RULE OF NATURAL JUSTICE BY ACCORDING TO THE ACCUSED AN OPPORTUNITY OF BEING HEARD ON THE QUESTION OF SENTENCE AND AT THE SAME TIME HELPS THE COURT TO CHOOSE THE SENTENCE TO BE AWARDED. Since the provision is intended to give the accused an opportunity to place before the Court ALL THE RELEVANT MATERIAL having a bearing on the question of sentence, there can be no doubt that THE PROVISION IS SALUTARY AND MUST BE STRICTLY FOLLOWED. IT IS CLEARLY MANDATORY AND SHOULD NOT BE TREATED AS A MERE FORMALITY.

Mr. Garg was, therefore, justified in making a grievance that the Trial Court actually treated it as A MERE FORMALITY, as is evident from the fact that it recorded the finding of guilt on 31st March, 1987, [and] on the same day, BEFORE THE ACCUSED COULD ABSORB AND OVERCOME THE SHOCK OF CONVICTION, they were asked if they had anything to say on the question of sentence and immediately thereafter the decision imposing the death penalty… was pronounced. IN A CASE OF LIFE OR DEATH… THE PRESIDING OFFICER MUST SHOW A HIGH DEGREE OF CONCERN FOR THE STATUTORY RIGHT OF THE ACCUSED AND SHOULD NOT TREAT IT AS A MERE FORMALITY TO BE CROSSED BEFORE MAKING THE CHOICE OF SENTENCE. IF THE CHOICE IS MADE, AS IN THIS CASE, WITHOUT GIVING THE ACCUSED AN EFFECTIVE AND REAL OPPORTUNITY TO PLACE HIS ANTECEDENTS, SOCIAL AND ECONOMIC BACKGROUND, MITIGATING AND EXTENUATING CIRCUMSTANCES, ETC., BEFORE THE COURT, THE COURT’S DECISION ON THE SENTENCE WOULD BE VULNERABLE.

We need hardly mention that in many cases a sentencing decision has FAR MORE SERIOUS CONSEQUENCES on the offender and his family members than in the case of a purely administrative decision; A FORTIORI, THEREFORE, THE PRINCIPLE OF FAIRPLAY MUST APPLY WITH GREATER VIGOUR IN THE CASE OF THE FORMER THAN THE LATTER. An administrative decision having civil consequences, if taken without giving a hearing, is generally struck down as violative of the rule of natural justice. LIKEWISE A SENTENCING DECISION TAKEN WITHOUT FOLLOWING THE REQUIREMENTS OF SUB-SECTION (2) OF SECTION 235 OF THE CODE IN LETTER AND SPIRIT WOULD ALSO MEET A SIMILAR FATE and may have to be replaced by an appropriate order.

The sentencing court MUST APPROACH THE QUESTION SERIOUSLY and must endeavour to see that ALL THE RELEVANT FACTS AND CIRCUMSTANCES BEARING ON THE QUESTION OF SENTENCE ARE BROUGHT ON RECORD. Only after giving due weight to the mitigating as well as the aggravating circumstances placed before it, it must pronounce the sentence. WE THINK AS A GENERAL RULE THE TRIAL COURTS SHOULD, AFTER RECORDING THE CONVICTION, ADJOURN THE MATTER TO A FUTURE DATE AND CALL UPON BOTH THE PROSECUTION AS WELL AS THE DEFENCE TO PLACE THE RELEVANT MATERIAL BEARING ON THE QUESTION OF SENTENCE BEFORE IT, AND THEREAFTER PRONOUNCE THE SENTENCE to be imposed on the offender. In the present case… the learned Trial Judge did not attach sufficient importance to the mandatory requirement of sub-section (2) of Section 235… The High Court also had before it ONLY THE SCANTY MATERIAL placed before the learned Sessions Judge when it confirmed the death penalty.”
Decision — (d) on sentence
VERBATIM DU “…in order that the sentences may be properly graded to fit the degree of gravity of each case, it is necessary that THE MAXIMUM SENTENCE PRESCRIBED BY LAW SHOULD… BE RESERVED FOR ‘THE RAREST OF RARE’ CASES WHICH ARE OF AN EXCEPTIONAL NATURE. Sentences of severity are imposed to REFLECT THE SERIOUSNESS OF THE CRIME, to PROMOTE RESPECT FOR THE LAW, to PROVIDE JUST PUNISHMENT for the offence, to AFFORD ADEQUATE DETERRENT to criminal conduct and to PROTECT THE COMMUNITY from further similar conduct. IT SERVES A THREE-FOLD PURPOSE: (i) PUNITIVE (ii) DETERRENT and (iii) PROTECTIVE. That is why this Court in Bachan Singh’s case observed that when the question of choice of sentence is under consideration the Court must not only look to the crime and the victim but also THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CRIMINAL AND THE IMPACT OF THE CRIME ON THE COMMUNITY. UNLESS THE NATURE OF THE CRIME AND THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE OFFENDER REVEAL THAT THE CRIMINAL IS A MENACE TO THE SOCIETY AND THE SENTENCE OF LIFE IMPRISONMENT WOULD BE ALTOGETHER INADEQUATE, THE COURT SHOULD ORDINARILY IMPOSE THE LESSER PUNISHMENT…

In the present case, unfortunately THE MATERIAL FOR CHOICE OF SENTENCE IS SCANTY. THE MOTIVE FOR THE CRIME IS OBSCURE; the one stated, namely, the quarrel between two infants of both sides, does not seem to be correct. THE KILLINGS WERE NOT FOR GAIN. The charge shows that THE TARGET WAS PW 6, THE FATHER, AND NOT THE TWO INFANTS. THE KILLING OF THE TWO INFANTS WAS NOT IN THE CONTEMPLATION OF ANY OF THE ACCUSED. Both the girls were the victims of the offenders’ ire resulting from FRUSTRATION AT THE ESCAPE OF THEIR TARGET. THERE IS NOTHING UNCOMMON ABOUT THE CRIME AS TO MAKE THE CASE AN EXCEPTIONAL ONE. THE MERE FACT THAT INFANTS ARE KILLED, WITHOUT MORE, IS NOT SUFFICIENT TO BRING THE CASE WITHIN THE CATEGORY OF ‘THE RAREST OF RARE’ CASES.…

Unfortunately, as the trial Judge DID NOT GIVE TIME TO THE CONVICTS TO REFLECT ON THE QUESTION OF SENTENCE, THE CHANCE, HOWEVER REMOTE, OF THE TRUE MOTIVE FOR THE CRIME SURFACING WAS LOST. THE ANTECEDENTS OF THE ACCUSED, THEIR SOCIO-ECONOMIC CONDITIONS, THE IMPACT OF THEIR CRIME ON THE COMMUNITY ETC. HAVE NOT COME ON RECORD. THE ABSENCE OF THESE PARTICULARS MAKES THE CHOICE OF PUNISHMENT DIFFICULT.… we think THE EXTREME PUNISHMENT OF DEATH IS NOT WARRANTED.

In the result both the appeals are partly allowed. The conviction of accused Nos. 1 and 2 under all the heads is confirmed but their sentence of death for killing Shahna Khatoon and Chand Tara, respectively, IS CONVERTED TO IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE.…”
Principles evolved
(1) s.235(2) CrPC is MANDATORY and is “not a mere formality”. It serves a dual purpose — natural justice, and assisting the court to choose the sentence. (2) THE GENERAL RULE OF PRACTICE: the trial court should, after recording the conviction, ADJOURN the matter to a future date and call upon both the prosecution and the defence to place material bearing on sentence. This is the single most practically important direction in the whole of Indian capital sentencing law, and it was reaffirmed and expanded in Manoj v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2022) SCC OnLine SC 677 (§34). (3) If the judge “is unable to explain with reasonable accuracy the basis for selecting the higher of the two sentences, his choice should fall on the LOWER sentence.” A rule of construction in favour of life. (4) A sentencing decision taken in breach of s.235(2) is vulnerable and “may have to be replaced by an appropriate order”. (5) Sentences of severity serve a three-fold purpose — punitive, deterrent and protective; but unless the criminal is “a menace to the society” and life imprisonment “would be altogether inadequate”, the court should ordinarily impose the lesser punishment. (6) “The mere fact that infants are killed, WITHOUT MORE, is not sufficient to bring the case within the category of ‘the rarest of rare’ cases.” Even a Machhi Singh category-V case must satisfy the full test. (7) Where the record is bare of the accused’s antecedents and socio-economic condition, the absence of that material tells against the death sentence, not in its favour — because the State bears the burden (Bachan Singh, mitigating factors 3 and 4).
The DU solved paper’s own summary
VERBATIM — DU solved paper, 2017 PYQ “In [the] Allauddin Mian case, the court observed that Section 235(2) of CrPC satisfied a dual purpose: (1) It satisfies the rule of natural justice by providing for [a] pre-sentence hearing. (2) It helps the court to choose [the] sentence [to be] awarded.”

26. CASE 5 — Mohd. Chaman v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2001)

Mohd. Chaman v. State (NCT of Delhi)(2001) 2 SCC 28 — D.P. Mohapatra and Shivaraj V. Patil, JJ.rape and murder of an infant · commutation
Facts
The appellant, aged about 30, a tenant living in the same building, committed rape on a girl of one and a half years (Ritu), the daughter of his landlord’s relatives, causing injuries from which she died. He was convicted under ss.302 and 376 IPC, sentenced to death by the Sessions Court, and the sentence was confirmed by the Delhi High Court.
Issue
Whether the case fell within the “rarest of rare” category so as to justify the death sentence.
Arguments
For the appellant: the crime, though grave, arose out of a perverted lust and not from a diabolical plan; the appellant was not shown to be a menace to society; there was no material on his antecedents; and Bachan Singh required that life be preferred unless the alternative was unquestionably foreclosed. For the State: the victim was an infant of one and a half years — the paradigm Machhi Singh category V(a) — the act was brutal and depraved, and the collective conscience of the community was shocked.
Decision
The Supreme Court commuted the death sentence to imprisonment for life. It held that the crime was “shocking” and had been committed in a “brutal manner”, but that on the material the appellant could not be said to be “such a dangerous person that to spare his life will endanger the community”; that the offence, though heinous, was not shown to be the product of a “depraved mentality” of the kind that would make reform impossible; and that the case did not fall in the category of the rarest of rare. Critically, the Court held that the courts below had erred in treating the brutality of the act alone as sufficient, without an assessment of the offender.
Principles evolved
(1) Brutality alone is not enough. The court must be satisfied that the accused is “a menace to society” and that reform is improbable — the same error identified in Bishnu Deo Shaw (“the mere use of adjectives like ‘cruel and brutal’”). (2) The rarest-of-rare test requires the second limb — that life imprisonment is unquestionably inadequate — to be separately satisfied. (3) The case is listed in Shankar Kisanrao Khade’s survey (quoted verbatim in the DU material’s text of Sunil Dutt Sharma DU) as one of the cases in which commutation was ordered because “the accused was not likely to be a menace or threat or danger to society or the community”.
Criticism and context
The decision is frequently contrasted with Dhananjoy Chatterjee (§27), decided seven years earlier on comparable facts, in which the death sentence was confirmed and carried out. That contrast — rape and murder of a child: death in one case, life in the other — is the single most effective illustration of the inconsistency in Indian capital sentencing, and is precisely what K.I. Vibhute’s prescribed article calls “the Supreme Court’s vacillation sans norms” (§33).

27. CASE 6 — Lehna v. State of Haryana (2002)

Lehna v. State of Haryana(2002) 3 SCC 76 — Doraiswamy Raju and Arijit Pasayat, JJ.Analysed at length in the prescribed reading by Kaul
Facts
As the DU material records DU: “the genesis of [the] dispute between the accused and the other members of the family was LAND. The accused seemed to have taken exception to his father taking away the land from him, for which he considered his brother and sister-in-law to be responsible. The accused was responsible for causing the death of his brother, sister-in-law and another relation.” He was convicted under s.302 IPC and sentenced to death; the High Court confirmed.
Issues
(1) What is the correct approach to the choice between death and life under s.354(3)? (2) Was this a rarest-of-rare case?
Arguments
For the appellant: three killings within a family arising out of a long-running land dispute; the appellant had himself sustained serious injuries in the thrashing he received from the villagers; the medical evidence indicated that his mental condition had led him to assault his relations; there was no diabolical planning; and the deprivation of his land had deprived him of his livelihood. For the State: three murders of family members, cruelly executed, fell within Machhi Singh categories I and IV.
Decision
The Supreme Court undertook the survey of the legislative history and the principles which the DU reading reproduces (§16), held that “life imprisonment is the rule and death sentence an exception”, and set out the five circumstances in which the community’s collective conscience may be shocked. Applying them, and giving weight to the appellant’s injuries, his mental condition (“the same may not be relevant to judge culpability, but it certainly is a factor while considering the question of sentence”), the absence of diabolic planning, the loss of his livelihood and “the frequency of quarrels [which] indicated lack of any sinister planning”, the Court held that “death sentence was not proper” and substituted imprisonment for life.
Principles evolved
(1) The five circumstances (reproduced verbatim at §16) — a restatement of Machhi Singh. (2) “The principle of proportion between crime and punishment is a principle of JUST DESERT that serves as the foundation of every criminal sentence that is justifiable… ANY PUNISHMENT IN EXCESS OF WHAT IS DESERVED FOR THE CRIMINAL CONDUCT IS PUNISHMENT WITHOUT GUILT.” (3) Material falling short of a legal defence — here, the accused’s mental condition — is nevertheless relevant at the sentencing stage. (4) “A judge has to BALANCE THE PERSONALITY OF THE OFFENDER with the circumstances, situations and the reaction[s] and choose the appropriate sentence” — the formula taken from Bishnu Deo Shaw para 27 and now standard. (5) The court must take “an overall global view” and answer the Machhi Singh questions.
Why the DU material selects this case
Because it is the vehicle through which Kaul’s prescribed reading explains the whole legislative history of s.354(3) — the pre-1955 position under s.367(5), the 1955 amendment, and the 1973 Code — and because it links the “rarest of rare” doctrine to the desert theory of the Ashworth reading. If a question asks you to relate the theories of punishment to the law of capital sentencing, Lehna as reported by Kaul is the bridge.

28. CASE 7 — Dhananjoy Chatterjee v. State of West Bengal (1994 / 2004)

Dhananjoy Chatterjee alias Dhana v. State of West Bengal(1994) 2 SCC 220; and the review/mercy proceedings reported at JT 2004 (4) SC 242 — A.S. Anand and N.P. Singh, JJ.death confirmed and executed
Facts
The appellant was a security guard at a residential building in Bhowanipore, Calcutta. Hetal Parekh, a schoolgirl of eighteen, had complained to her mother of his teasing, and her father had complained to the building authorities, in consequence of which the appellant had been ordered to be transferred. On 5 March 1990, in the afternoon, while the girl was alone in the flat, he entered it, raped and murdered her. The case was one of circumstantial evidence, supported by medical evidence, the recovery of the victim’s wrist watch from his residence, and the evidence of the building records. He was convicted under ss.302, 376 and 380 IPC and sentenced to death; the Calcutta High Court confirmed.
Issues
(1) Whether the circumstantial evidence established guilt beyond reasonable doubt. (2) Whether the case was rarest of rare.
Arguments
For the appellant: the case rested wholly on circumstantial evidence; there was no eye-witness; the appellant was a young man of 27 with a family and no previous conviction; and the crime, however grave, did not disclose that he was beyond reform. For the State: the victim was a defenceless schoolgirl; the appellant was in a position of trust as the security guard whose very duty was to protect the residents; the motive was revenge for the complaint that had led to his transfer; and the crime was premeditated, unprovoked and shocking to the conscience of society.
Decision
The Supreme Court confirmed both the conviction and the sentence of death. The reasoning that is always quoted:
“In our opinion, the measure of punishment in a given case must depend upon the atrocity of the crime; the conduct of the criminal and the defenceless and unprotected state of the victim. Imposition of appropriate punishment is the manner in which the courts respond to the society’s cry for justice against the criminals. Justice demands that courts should impose punishment befitting the crime so that the courts reflect public abhorrence of the crime. The courts must not only keep in view the rights of the criminal but also the rights of the victim of crime and the society at large while considering [the] imposition of appropriate punishment.”

“The sordid episode of the security guard, whose sacred duty was to ensure the protection and welfare of the inhabitants of the flats in the apartment, should have subjected the deceased, a resident of one of the flats, to gratify his lust and murder her in retaliation for his transfer on her complaint, makes the crime [one] of an EXCEPTIONALLY DEPRAVED AND HEINOUS CHARACTER… THE FACTS AND CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CASE ESTABLISH THE OFFENCE OF MURDER TO BE ONE OF THE RAREST OF RARE CASES.” Dhananjoy Chatterjee v. State of West Bengal
The review petition and the mercy petitions to the Governor and the President were rejected, and the appellant was executed on 14 August 2004 at Alipore Central Jail — the first execution in India in fourteen years.
Principles evolved
(1) The measure of punishment depends on “the ATROCITY of the crime; the CONDUCT of the criminal and the DEFENCELESS AND UNPROTECTED STATE OF THE VICTIM”. (2) The court must “keep in view… not only the rights of the criminal but also the rights of the victim of crime and the society at large” — the clearest statement in the prescribed cases of the victim-and-society strand of sentencing. (3) Breach of a position of trust is a powerful aggravating circumstance (Machhi Singh, category V(c)). (4) A conviction and a death sentence may rest on circumstantial evidence, though later cases (Bariyar; Shankar Kisanrao Khade’s survey) treat circumstantial evidence as a mitigating consideration on sentence.
Criticism — and why the case matters today
(i) Inconsistency: compare Mohd. Chaman (§25), where the rape and murder of a child of one and a half years produced commutation seven years later. (ii) Public pressure: the execution followed an intense public campaign, and it is frequently cited as the clearest instance of the “collective conscience” criterion operating as a channel for popular sentiment. (iii) Class: the appellant was a poor migrant security guard; the NLUD data (§19.2) shows this to be the modal profile of the death-row prisoner. (iv) Doubt: the case has been the subject of sustained academic re-examination of the reliability of the circumstantial evidence. Use Dhananjoy Chatterjee as the leading retentionist authority and then set the criticisms against it.

29. CASE 8 — Swamy Shraddananda (2) v. State of Karnataka (2008)

Swamy Shraddananda alias Murali Manohar Mishra (2) v. State of KarnatakaAIR 2008 SC 3040; (2008) 13 SCC 767 — B.N. Agrawal, G.S. Singhvi and Aftab Alam, JJ. (judgment of AFTAB ALAM, J.)the middle path
Facts
The appellant married Shakereh, a wealthy woman of an aristocratic Bangalore family and the former wife of a senior diplomat, and thereby obtained access to her very substantial property. On 28 May 1991 he drugged her, placed her in a wooden box and buried her alive in a pit dug in the courtyard of her own house, and thereafter for some four years concealed the crime by pretending that she had gone abroad, while dealing with her properties by forged documents. The body was exhumed in 1994. He was convicted under s.302 IPC and sentenced to death; the Karnataka High Court confirmed. In the earlier round (Swamy Shraddananda (1)) a two-Judge Bench had differed on sentence — one Judge confirming death and the other commuting — and the matter was referred to a three-Judge Bench.
Issues
(1) Was this a rarest-of-rare case warranting death? (2) If not, is the court confined to the binary of death or a life sentence liable to remission after fourteen years, or may it impose a third, intermediate sentence?
Arguments
For the appellant: the very fact that two Judges of the Supreme Court had differed showed that this was not a case where the alternative was “unquestionably foreclosed”; the appellant was elderly; the murder, though horrifying, was for gain and not of a character showing that he was a continuing menace. For the State: the murder was “cold-blooded, calculated and diabolical”, of a trusting wife by a husband for gain, with an elaborate four-year concealment; commutation to a life sentence would, in practice, mean release after fourteen years, which would be grossly inadequate.
Decision
The Court commuted the death sentence, but did so by devising what has come to be called the “Shraddananda sentence” or the middle path: it directed that the appellant shall not be released from prison till the end of his life. The reasoning:
“The truth of the matter is that the question of death penalty is not free from the subjective element and the confirmation of death sentence or its commutation by this Court depends a good deal on the personal predilection of the judges constituting the Bench.”

“A good deal of the unsatisfactory state of affairs [arises] from the fact that in the sentencing options the court is left with only two extremes: death on the one hand and, on the other, a sentence of imprisonment for life which, in practice, means a term of not more than fourteen years… THE COURT’S OPTION IS SEVERELY LIMITED.”

“A far more rational and just option [is to] substitute a death sentence by a sentence of imprisonment for the whole of the convict’s natural life, [or to] put the convict’s sentence beyond remission… The matter may be looked at from a slightly different angle. The issue of sentencing has two aspects. A sentence may be excessive and unduly harsh or it may be highly disproportionately inadequate. When an appellant comes to this Court carrying a death sentence awarded by the trial court and confirmed by the High Court, this Court may find… that the case just falls short of the rarest of the rare category and may feel somewhat reluctant in endorsing the death sentence. But at the same time, having regard to the nature of the crime, the Court may strongly feel that a sentence of life imprisonment subject to remission normally works out to a term of 14 years would be GROSSLY DISPROPORTIONATE AND INADEQUATE.” Swamy Shraddananda (2) v. State of Karnataka
Principles evolved
(1) THE MIDDLE PATH. Where a case falls short of the rarest of rare but a fourteen-year life sentence would be grossly inadequate, the court may impose a sentence of imprisonment for life meaning the whole of the convict’s natural life, or for a fixed term (20, 25, 30 years) without remission. (2) The candid acknowledgment that capital sentencing “depends a good deal on the personal predilection of the judges constituting the Bench” — the frankest judicial admission of the arbitrariness problem, and the foundation of the Sangeet and Bariyar critiques. (3) The power derives from the court’s authority to impose a sentence of life imprisonment, which in law already means the natural life (Godse); the direction merely restricts the operation of remission in the particular case.
Confirmation and limits — Sriharan
Union of India v. V. Sriharan alias Murugan (2016) 7 SCC 1 (Constitution Bench, 3:2) upheld the Shraddananda sentence, holding that a court may impose a “special category” sentence of imprisonment for life for a determinate period beyond fourteen years, or for the remainder of natural life; but with two important limits — (i) only the High Court or the Supreme Court may impose such a sentence, not a trial court; and (ii) the constitutional power of remission under Arts. 72 and 161 remains wholly unaffected. The minority (Lalit and Sapre JJ.) held that such a sentence encroaches on the executive power of remission conferred by ss.432–433 CrPC. The technique has since been applied in a very large number of cases, and it is the single most significant development in Indian sentencing law since Bachan Singh.
Use it for
Any question on (i) the abolition of the death penalty — because it supplies the constructive alternative; (ii) the meaning of life imprisonment; (iii) the arbitrariness of capital sentencing; and (iv) the relationship between the judiciary and the executive in the matter of remission.

30. CASE 9 — Essa @ Anjum Abdul Razak Memon v. State of Maharashtra (2013) DU

Essa @ Anjum Abdul Razak Memon (A-3) v. The State of Maharashtra(2013) 3 SCALE 1; (2013) 13 SCC 1 — P. Sathasivam and B.S. Chauhan, JJ.Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 222–231
Facts
The appeals arose out of the Bombay serial bomb blasts of 12 March 1993, in which explosions at the Bombay Stock Exchange, Air India Building, Zaveri Bazaar, hotels and other locations killed some 257 persons and injured more than 700, with property damage running to crores. The accused were tried by the Designated Court under TADA for the Bombay Bomb Blast Case. A common charge of criminal conspiracy was framed against all the co-conspirators; the DU material reproduces it DU: “During the period from December, 1992 to April, 1993 at various places in Bombay, District Raigad and District Thane in India and outside India in Dubai (U.A.E.) and Pakistan, entered into a criminal conspiracy… whose object was to commit terrorist acts in India… with an intent to overawe the Government as by law established, to strike terror in the people, to alienate sections of the people and to adversely affect the harmony amongst different sections of the people, i.e. Hindus and Muslims, by using bombs, dynamites, hand grenades and other explosive substances like RDX or inflammable substances or fire-arms like AK-56 rifles, carbines, pistols and other lethal weapons…” — charged under s.3(3) TADA and s.120-B IPC read with ss.3(2)(i)(ii), 3(3)(4), 5 and 6 TADA and with ss.302, 307, 326, 324, 427, 435, 436, 201 and 212 IPC, the Arms Act, the Explosives Act, the Explosive Substances Act and the Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act.
The sentencing and juvenility issues
The DU material reproduces, in this judgment, the Court’s detailed treatment of two matters that make it a Topic 5 case: (1) the principles governing sentencing in terrorism cases and the confirmation or commutation of death sentences imposed by the Designated Court on a large number of accused; and (2) the claim of juvenility raised by A-43 (Mohd. Moin Faridulla Qureshi), who contended that he was “17 years and 3 months old on the date of commission of [the] offence” and that his case ought to have been dealt with under the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000 and not under TADA. The material sets out ss.15 and 16 of the 2000 Act, Hari Ram v. State of Rajasthan (2009) 13 SCC 211, Gulam Hossain v. State of West Bengal (2012) 10 SCC, Arnit Das, Pratap Singh, Sheela Barse and Gaurav Jain, and quotes the passage on the effect of incarceration on a child: detention “would have the effect of dwarfing the development of the child, exposing him to baneful influences, coarsening his conscience and alienating him from the society” DU (see Topic 4, §28 and §33).
Issues
(1) What are the principles governing the choice between death and life in an offence of mass terrorism? (2) Can the JJ Act override TADA where the accused was a juvenile on the date of the offence? (3) Does a defect of procedure (e.g. under s.235(2)) vitiate the sentence?
Decision and principles
(1) On juvenility: the Court applied the settled principles — the reckoning date is the date of the offence; the JJ Act is a beneficent and remedial statute; and its protection operates notwithstanding TADA. The claim had therefore to be examined on the evidence, and could not be shut out merely because the trial had proceeded under a special anti-terror statute. (2) On sentence: terrorism directed at the State and at the civilian population, “with an intent to overawe the Government as by law established [and] to strike terror in the people”, is an offence of a wholly different order from an ordinary murder, and the “rarest of rare” analysis must take account of the magnitude of the crime (Machhi Singh, category IV) and of the danger to the security of the State — a factor Bachan Singh itself accepted may supply “special reasons”. At the same time the Court applied the Bachan Singh balance sheet to each accused individually, distinguishing between the conspirators who planned and financed the operation and those who played a subsidiary role, and it commuted a large number of death sentences to imprisonment for life while confirming others. (3) Individualised sentencing survives even in a mass-casualty case: the court must not impose a uniform sentence on all the accused, but must weigh the role of each.
Why the DU material includes it
Because it is the one prescribed case in which the death-penalty jurisprudence of Topic 5 meets the organised-crime and terrorism material of Topic 3 and the juvenile justice material of Topic 4. It is also the authority for the proposition that the Law Commission’s 262nd Report would carve out of abolition — “terror cases and waging war against the State” (§19.2). Cite it whenever a question asks whether the death penalty should be retained for terrorism.

31. CASE 10 — Sunil Dutt Sharma v. State (Govt. of NCT of Delhi) (2014) DU verbatim

Sunil Dutt Sharma v. State (Govt. of NCT of Delhi)(2014) 4 SCC 375 — Ranjan Gogoi and Shiva Kirti Singh, JJ. (judgment of RANJAN GOGOI, J.)Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 232–241
Facts
VERBATIM — paras 1–2 DU “1. The accused-appellant was tried for offences under Sections 302 and 304-B of the Indian Penal Code for causing the death of his wife in the night intervening 16/17.05.92. He has been acquitted of the offence under Section 302… on the benefit of doubt, though found guilty for the offence under Section 304-B, following which the sentence of life imprisonment has been imposed. The conviction and sentence has been affirmed by the High Court…
2. Limited notice on the question of sentence imposed on the accused-appellant having been issued by this Court, the scope of the present appeal stands truncated to a determination of the question as to whether [the] sentence of life imprisonment imposed… for commission of the offence under Section 304-B of the Penal Code is in any way EXCESSIVE OR DISPROPORTIONATE so as to require interference by this Court.”
The statutory setting
VERBATIM — paras 3–4 DU “3. Section 304-B(2) of the Penal Code… is in the following terms: ‘Whoever commits dowry death shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which shall NOT BE LESS THAN SEVEN YEARS but which MAY EXTEND TO IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE.’ (emphasis is ours)
4. Expressions similar to what has been noticed above are to be found in different sections of the Penal Code…”
The Court then set out a long catalogue of sections using the formula “may extend to seven years / ten years” — ss.115, 118, 123, 124, 126, 127, 134, 193, 201, 214, 216, 216A, 219, 220, 221, 222, 225, 231, 234, 243, 244, 245, 247, 249, 256, 257, 258, 259, 260, 281, 293, 308, 312, 317, 325, 333, 363, 365, 369, 370, 380, 381, 387, 393, 401, 402, 404, 407, 408, 409, 433, 435 and others — in order to show that the legislature deliberately varies its language, and that where it prescribes a maximum of imprisonment for life the maximum is to be reserved for the gravest instances of that offence.
Issue
Whether the principles evolved for choosing between death and life under s.302 IPC apply, mutatis mutandis, to the choice between the minimum and the maximum sentence in an offence such as s.304-B, where the maximum is imprisonment for life; and whether, on the facts, the maximum was excessive.
Arguments
For the appellant: he had been acquitted of murder and convicted only on the statutory presumption under s.304-B; the maximum sentence available under the section had been imposed without any assessment of mitigating circumstances; there had been no effective hearing on sentence; and the Bachan Singh balance-sheet approach ought to govern every sentencing exercise in which the court is choosing within a wide statutory range. For the State: dowry death is a grave social offence; the statutory minimum of seven years indicates legislative seriousness; and the choice of sentence within the range is a matter for the trial court’s discretion with which the Supreme Court should not interfere.
Decision and reasoning
The Court held that the principles of the death-penalty jurisprudence apply to the imposition of the MAXIMUM sentence in ANY offence, and that the sentence of imprisonment for life imposed on the appellant was excessive; it reduced the sentence. The reasoning proceeds through the recent history of the sentencing jurisprudence, reproduced verbatim in the material:
VERBATIM — the Bachan Singh restatement quoted in the judgment DU “…the true import of Section 235(2) read with Section 354(3) is that in fixing the degree of punishment or making the choice of sentence for various offences, including one under Section 302 of the Penal Code, the court SHOULD NOT CONFINE ITS CONSIDERATION ‘PRINCIPALLY’ OR MERELY TO THE CIRCUMSTANCES CONNECTED WITH THE PARTICULAR CRIME, BUT ALSO GIVE DUE CONSIDERATION TO THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CRIMINAL.

164. Attuned to the legislative policy delineated in Sections 354(3) and 235(2), propositions (iv)(a) and (v)(b) in Jagmohan shall have to be recast and may be stated as below:
‘(a) THE NORMAL RULE IS THAT THE OFFENCE OF MURDER SHALL BE PUNISHED WITH THE SENTENCE OF LIFE IMPRISONMENT. THE COURT CAN DEPART FROM THAT RULE AND IMPOSE THE SENTENCE OF DEATH ONLY IF THERE ARE SPECIAL REASONS FOR DOING SO. SUCH REASONS MUST BE RECORDED IN WRITING BEFORE IMPOSING THE DEATH SENTENCE.
(b) While considering the question of sentence to be imposed for the offence of murder under Section 302 of the Penal Code, THE COURT MUST HAVE REGARD TO EVERY RELEVANT CIRCUMSTANCE RELATING TO THE CRIME AS WELL AS THE CRIMINAL. If the court finds, BUT NOT OTHERWISE, that the offence is of AN EXCEPTIONALLY DEPRAVED AND HEINOUS CHARACTER and constitutes, on account of its design and the manner of its execution, A SOURCE OF GRAVE DANGER TO THE SOCIETY AT LARGE, the court may impose the death sentence.’”
VERBATIM — the Court’s account of Sangeet and Shankar Kisanrao Khade DU “8. In Sangeet the Court also took note of the ‘suggestions’ (offered at the Bar) noticed in Bachan Singh to be relevant in a determination of the circumstances attending the crime (described as aggravating circumstances) as well as those which pertain to the criminal as distinguished from the crime (referred to as the mitigating circumstances). THE ATTEMPT AT EVOLUTION OF A PRINCIPLE-BASED SENTENCING POLICY AS DISTINGUISHED FROM A JUDGE-CENTRIC ONE WAS NOTED TO HAVE SUFFERED SOME AMOUNT OF DERAILMENT/EROSION. In fact, the several judgments noted and referred to in Sangeet were found to have brought in A FAIR AMOUNT OF UNCERTAINTY in application of the principles in awarding life imprisonment or death penalty, as may be, and the VARYING PERSPECTIVE OR RESPONSES OF THE COURT BASED ON THE PARTICULAR FACTS OF A GIVEN CASE RATHER THAN EVOLVING STANDARDISED JURISPRUDENTIAL PRINCIPLES APPLICABLE ACROSS THE BOARD.

9. The above position was again noticed in Shankar Kisanrao Khade. In the separate concurring opinion rendered by Brother Madan B. Lokur there is an exhaustive consideration of the judgments rendered by this Court in the recent past (last 15 years) wherein death penalty has been converted to life imprisonment and also the cases wherein death penalty has been confirmed…”
The two lists — reproduce these in any death-penalty answer
VERBATIM — Shankar Kisanrao Khade, para 106, as quoted in the DU material DU “106. A study of the above cases suggests that there are several reasons, cumulatively taken, for CONVERTING THE DEATH PENALTY TO THAT OF IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE. However, some of the factors that have had an influence in COMMUTATION include:
(1) THE YOUNG AGE OF THE ACCUSED [Amit v. State of Maharashtra, aged 20; Rahul, aged 24; Santosh Kumar Singh, aged 24; Rameshbhai Chandubhai Rathod (2), aged 28; Amit v. State of U.P., aged 28];
(2) THE POSSIBILITY OF REFORMING AND REHABILITATING THE ACCUSED (in Santosh Kumar Singh and Amit v. State of U.P. the accused, incidentally, were young when they committed the crime);
(3) THE ACCUSED HAD NO PRIOR CRIMINAL RECORD [Nirmal Singh; Raju; Bantu; Amit v. State of Maharashtra; Surendra Pal Shivbalakpal; Rahul; Amit v. State of U.P.];
(4) THE ACCUSED WAS NOT LIKELY TO BE A MENACE OR THREAT OR DANGER TO SOCIETY OR THE COMMUNITY [Nirmal Singh; Mohd. Chaman; Raju; Bantu; Surendra Pal Shivbalakpal; Rahul; Amit v. State of U.P.];
(5) a few other reasons… such as THE ACCUSED HAVING BEEN ACQUITTED BY ONE OF THE COURTS (State of T.N. v. Suresh; State of Maharashtra v. Suresh; Bharat Fakira Dhiwar; Mansingh; Santosh Kumar Singh);
(6) THE CRIME WAS NOT PREMEDITATED (Kumudi Lal; Akhtar; Raju; Amrit Singh);
(7) THE CASE WAS ONE OF CIRCUMSTANTIAL EVIDENCE (Mansingh; Bishnu Prasad Sinha).
In one case, commutation was ordered since there was apparently no ‘exceptional’ feature warranting a death penalty (Kumudi Lal) and in another case because the trial court had awarded [a] life sentence but the High Court enhanced it to death (Haresh Mohandas Rajput).

122. The principal reasons for CONFIRMING THE DEATH PENALTY in the above cases include:
(1) THE CRUEL, DIABOLIC, BRUTAL, DEPRAVED AND GRUESOME NATURE OF THE CRIME (Jumman Khan; Dhananjoy Chatterjee; Laxman Naik; Kamta Tiwari; Nirmal Singh; Jai Kumar; Satish; Bantu; Ankush Maruti Shinde; B.A. Umesh; Mohd. Mannan; Rajendra Pralhadrao Wasnik);
(2) THE CRIME RESULTS IN PUBLIC ABHORRENCE, SHOCKS THE JUDICIAL CONSCIENCE OR THE CONSCIENCE OF SOCIETY OR THE COMMUNITY (Dhananjoy Chatterjee; Jai Kumar; Ankush Maruti Shinde; Mohd. Mannan);
(3) THE REFORM OR REHABILITATION OF THE CONVICT IS NOT LIKELY OR THAT HE WOULD BE A MENACE TO SOCIETY (Jai Kumar; B.A. Umesh; Mohd. Mannan);
(4) THE VICTIMS WERE DEFENCELESS (Dhananjoy Chatterjee; Laxman Naik; Kamta Tiwari; Ankush Maruti Shinde; Mohd. Mannan; Rajendra Pralhadrao Wasnik);
(5) THE CRIME WAS EITHER UNPROVOKED OR… PREMEDITATED (Dhananjoy Chatterjee; Laxman Naik; Kamta Tiwari; Nirmal Singh; Jai Kumar; Ankush Maruti Shinde; B.A. Umesh; Mohd. Mannan); and in three cases the antecedents or the prior history of the convict was taken [into account]…”
Principles evolved
(1) The Bachan Singh principles are not confined to capital cases. Whenever a court is choosing within a statutory range whose upper limit is imprisonment for life, it must draw the same balance sheet and have regard to the criminal as well as the crime. This is the ratio of the case and the reason it is prescribed. (2) The maximum sentence must be reserved for the gravest instance of the offence. Where the appellant had been acquitted of murder and convicted only under s.304-B on the statutory presumption, a sentence of imprisonment for life — the maximum — was excessive and disproportionate. (3) The seven factors favouring commutation and the five factors favouring confirmation (from Shankar Kisanrao Khade) constitute the most usable modern checklist of mitigating and aggravating circumstances. (4) The judicial admission — reproduced in the DU material — that the attempt at a principle-based rather than a judge-centric sentencing policy “suffered some amount of derailment/erosion” and has produced “a fair amount of uncertainty”.
Use it for
Any question on proportionality in sentencing generally (not merely capital sentencing); on the reform of sentencing; and for the two checklists, which are the best short answer to “what are the aggravating and mitigating circumstances in Indian law?”

32. CASE 11 — Santosh Kumar Satishbhushan Bariyar v. State of Maharashtra (2009)

Santosh Kumar Satishbhushan Bariyar v. State of Maharashtra(2009) 6 SCC 498 — S.B. Sinha and Cyriac Joseph, JJ. (judgment of S.B. SINHA, J.)per incuriam · burden on the State
Facts
Four unemployed young men, including the appellant, kidnapped their friend Sanjay Gopal Gupta for a ransom of ₹10 lakh; when the plan miscarried they murdered him, dismembered the body and disposed of the parts in different places. The appellant later became an approver-turned-accused; his own confessional statement was the principal evidence. He was convicted under ss.302, 364-A, 201 and 120-B IPC and sentenced to death; the High Court confirmed.
Issues
(1) Is a death sentence sustainable where the conviction rests substantially on the confession of a co-accused/approver? (2) What is the correct application of the “rarest of rare” doctrine, and on whom does the burden lie? (3) Are the decisions following Ravji v. State of Rajasthan (1996) 2 SCC 175 — which held that it is the nature and gravity of the crime, and not the criminal, that is germane — good law?
Arguments
For the appellant: he was a young man of about 22, unemployed, with no prior criminal record, and there was no material to show that he could not be reformed; the sentencing courts had proceeded on the crime alone, contrary to Bachan Singh; and the decisions relied on by the State were contrary to the Constitution Bench. For the State: the murder was premeditated, committed for ransom, and the dismemberment of the body demonstrated exceptional depravity within Machhi Singh category I(iii).
Decision
The Court commuted the death sentence to life imprisonment and made four holdings of the first importance:
“Ravji v. State of Rajasthan has been HELD TO BE PER INCURIAM… [the] decisions rendered following Ravji — Shivaji, Mohan Anna Chavan, Bantu, Surja Ram, Dayanidhi Bisoi and State of U.P. v. Sattan — were rendered per incuriam [because they followed the proposition that only the characteristics relating to the crime, to the exclusion of the criminal, are relevant, which is contrary to the Constitution Bench in Bachan Singh].”

“The rarest of rare dictum… serves as a guideline in enforcing Section 354(3) and establishes the policy that life imprisonment is the rule and death punishment is an exception. It is a settled law of interpretation that punishment which is rule-based must be… The court has to show that the option of life imprisonment is UNQUESTIONABLY FORECLOSED.”

“The state must prove [by evidence] that the accused is a continuing threat to society and that there is no probability of reform.” Santosh Kumar Satishbhushan Bariyar v. State of Maharashtra
Principles evolved
(1) Ravji and six decisions following it are PER INCURIAM — the most remarkable feature of the case, since death sentences had been confirmed and in some instances executed on the strength of them. This finding led fourteen retired judges to petition the President in 2012 for commutation in cases decided on that footing, and was the empirical foundation of the Law Commission’s 262nd Report. (2) The “rarest of rare” test has two independent limbs, and the second — that the option of life imprisonment is “unquestionably foreclosed” — must be separately addressed; it is not enough to find the crime heinous. (3) The BURDEN IS ON THE STATE to prove by evidence that the accused would constitute a continuing threat to society and that there is no probability of reform (giving effect to Bachan Singh’s mitigating factors (3) and (4)). In practice the State almost never leads such evidence — which, on Bariyar, should be fatal to the death sentence. (4) Socio-economic factors — youth, poverty, unemployment, lack of education, the absence of a prior record — are constitutionally relevant mitigating circumstances, and a sentencing court must call for material about them. (5) Where the conviction rests on the confession of a co-accused or purely on circumstantial evidence, the residual doubt is a mitigating factor on sentence.

33. CASE 12 — Mukesh v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2017)

Mukesh & Anr. v. State (NCT of Delhi) & Ors.(2017) 6 SCC 1 — Dipak Misra, R. Banumathi and Ashok Bhushan, JJ.the Nirbhaya case
Facts
On the night of 16 December 2012, a 23-year-old physiotherapy intern and her male friend boarded a private bus in South Delhi. Six persons on the bus — five adults and one juvenile — beat the male friend unconscious, and gang-raped and brutally assaulted the woman, inflicting injuries with an iron rod that caused catastrophic damage to her intestines and genital organs, and threw both victims naked from the moving bus. She died on 29 December 2012 in Singapore. One accused (Ram Singh) died in custody; the juvenile was dealt with by the Juvenile Justice Board and given the maximum three years (see Topic 4, §43.2). The four remaining accused were convicted by the trial court under ss.120-B, 365, 366, 376(2)(g), 377, 302, 395, 397, 201 and 412 IPC and sentenced to death; the Delhi High Court confirmed.
Issues
(1) Whether the conviction was sustainable on the evidence, including the dying declarations (three in number) and the DNA and odontology evidence. (2) Whether the case fell within the “rarest of rare” category. (3) Whether the mitigating circumstances — youth, poverty, family responsibilities, the absence of a criminal record and the possibility of reform — displaced the death sentence.
Arguments for the appellants
(i) The dying declarations were inconsistent and one had been recorded by gestures; the identification was doubtful; the investigation was defective and the media trial had prejudiced the proceedings. (ii) On sentence: all four were young (aged between 19 and 28), from extremely poor backgrounds, with no prior convictions, with dependent families; the State had led no evidence that they were beyond reform, as Bachan Singh and Bariyar require; and the sentence had been driven by public outrage rather than by the balance sheet. (iii) Reliance was placed on Bariyar, Sangeet and Shankar Kisanrao Khade.
Arguments for the State
The dying declarations were consistent in all material particulars and were corroborated by scientific evidence; the offence was of “exceptional depravity”; the manner of commission — the use of the iron rod, the disembowelling, the throwing out of the victims naked in the December cold — placed it squarely in Machhi Singh categories I and V; the victim was defenceless; and the crime had shocked the “collective conscience” of the nation.
Decision
The Supreme Court dismissed the appeals and confirmed the death sentences. Dipak Misra J. (for himself and Bhushan J.) held that the case fell in the “rarest of rare” category, describing the offence as one which “sounds like a story from a different world where humanity has been treated with irreverence”, and holding that the aggravating circumstances “outweigh” the mitigating ones. Banumathi J., concurring, wrote separately, emphasising the social dimension of crimes against women, the failure of the criminal justice system to protect them, and the need for gender sensitisation; she also observed that the “rarest of rare” test was satisfied because “the accused may not be a menace to the society, but the crime committed by them is so heinous that the collective conscience of the society is shocked”. Review petitions were dismissed in 2018, curative petitions in 2020, and the four convicts were executed on 20 March 2020.
Principles and criticism
(1) The case is the high-water mark of the “collective conscience” criterion and of the victim-and-society strand of Dhananjoy Chatterjee. (2) It is also the most criticised modern application of Bachan Singh, on four grounds: (a) the State led no evidence that the accused could not be reformed, which on Bariyar should have been decisive; (b) the Court’s treatment of the mitigating circumstances is very brief compared with its treatment of the aggravating ones — precisely the imbalance Sangeet had identified; (c) “collective conscience” is an unmeasurable criterion that in this case coincided with an unprecedented media and public campaign; and (d) Banumathi J.’s formulation — that the accused “may not be a menace to the society” but the crime shocks the collective conscience — is difficult to reconcile with the requirement that the alternative option be “unquestionably foreclosed”. (3) Legislative consequences: the Justice J.S. Verma Committee Report (2013) — which notably recommended against the death penalty for rape — and the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013, which inserted ss.166A, 326A, 326B, 354A–354D, 370, 376A, 376D and 376E IPC; the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2018, extending the death penalty to the rape of a girl below twelve; and the amendment of the Juvenile Justice Act in 2015 (Topic 4). Cite the Verma Committee’s recommendation — it is the most telling counter-argument to the popular demand for the death penalty in rape cases.

34.2 K.I. Vibhute — “vacillation sans norms”

A note on the source. Prof. K.I. Vibhute’s article is listed in the DU syllabus as a suggested reading and is not reproduced in the material. Its thesis, as its title states, is that the Supreme Court’s choice between “death” and “life” exhibits vacillation without norms. The argument runs as follows, and it is the best available framework for a critical essay:
  1. Bachan Singh promised a principled, two-limbed test but refused to supply standards, on the express ground that standardisation “belongs to the sphere of legislation”. Parliament has never supplied them. The result is a doctrinal vacuum filled by judicial intuition.
  2. Machhi Singh’s categories filled part of the vacuum but only on the aggravating side, and were then treated as if they were the test — so the balance sheet tilted permanently towards death.
  3. The “collective conscience” criterion is incapable of principled application. It substitutes an imagined public sentiment for a legal standard, and in practice tracks media attention. Compare Dhananjoy Chatterjee (death) with Mohd. Chaman (life) on materially similar facts.
  4. Aggravating and mitigating circumstances are incommensurable. There is no common scale on which the brutality of a killing can be weighed against the youth and poverty of the killer — which is why Sangeet said the “balancing” exercise cannot be performed as Bachan Singh imagined.
  5. The Court has repeatedly confessed error. Bariyar’s per incuriam finding; Shraddananda’s admission that the outcome “depends a good deal on the personal predilection of the judges constituting the Bench”; the review in open court ordered in Mohd. Arif v. Registrar, Supreme Court of India (2014) 9 SCC 737; and the fourteen retired judges’ petition of 2012.
  6. The consequence is unequal protection. If the sentence depends on the composition of the Bench, the scheme offends Art. 14 — exactly Bhagwati J.’s dissent in Bachan Singh, thirty-five years later and vindicated by the Court’s own evidence.
  7. Vibhute’s prescription (and that of the Law Commission’s 262nd Report): either supply genuine legislative standards, or abolish — with the intermediate solution of the Shraddananda sentence available in the meantime.
How to use this section. Any question that asks you to “critically examine” the rarest-of-rare doctrine, or whether India should abolish capital punishment, should end with §34: the most damning evidence against the doctrine comes not from academics but from the Supreme Court’s own judgments — Bariyar, Shraddananda, Sangeet and Shankar Kisanrao Khade. State that, name the four cases, and quote the Sangeet passage from the DU material.

36. PROBATION — LEWIS DIANA: the historical development

PRESCRIBED READING DULewis Diana, “What is Probation?”, in Carter & Wilkins (eds.), Probation and Parole: Selected Readings (1970)
VERBATIM — Lewis Diana, DU Case Material pp. 242–243 DU “WHAT IS PROBATION?
SUMMARY OF HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT OF PROBATION
Some authorities trace the roots of probation to the MIDDLE AGES when such devices as the BENEFIT OF CLERGY and the LAW OF SANCTUARY made it possible either to AVOID or at least to POSTPONE punishment. It is more likely that there was not any continuous linear development of probation, although one can point to various forerunners such as the JUDICIAL REPRIEVE, by which the court suspended the imposition or execution of a sentence, and the practice of RELEASING AN OFFENDER ON HIS OWN RECOGNIZANCE. Consequently, probation was probably more directly an outgrowth of the different methods in England and America for SUSPENDING SENTENCE.

Under the common law the courts of England had for many years BOUND OVER PETTY OFFENDERS TO SURETIES or released them on their own recognizance even without sureties. Such practices were also common in some of the American colonies, especially MASSACHUSETTS, which in 1836 recognized by law the releasing of minor offenders with sureties. In 1869 this same state also authorized the placement, AFTER INVESTIGATION, of youthful offenders in private homes under the supervision of an agent of the state.

CREDIT FOR THE FIRST USE OF THE TERM ‘PROBATION’ GOES TO JOHN AUGUSTUS, A BOSTON SHOEMAKER, who apparently became interested in BEFRIENDING violators of the law, BAILED MANY OF THEM OUT OF JAIL, and provided them with SYMPATHETIC SUPERVISION. THIS WAS AS EARLY AS 1841. It was not until 1878, however, that THE FIRST PROBATION LAW WAS PASSED, MASSACHUSETTS again taking the lead. In that year the MAYOR OF BOSTON was given the power to appoint probation officers, and only two years later, in 1880, the law was extended to apply to other communities within the state. Then in 1891 Massachusetts passed a second law, which required the extension of probation to the CRIMINAL COURTS. By 1900, though, ONLY FIVE STATES — Massachusetts, Missouri, Rhode Island, New Jersey, and Vermont — recognized probation legally. BY 1933 ALL STATES EXCEPT WYOMING HAD JUVENILE PROBATION LAWS, and all but thirteen states had ADULT probation laws. This latter group had been cut to five states by 1950: Mississippi, Nevada, New Mexico, Oklahoma, and South Dakota.

The variety of legislation governing probation in the United States may have stemmed (1) from the Supreme Court’s denial in the KILLITS CASE that there existed any INHERENT JUDICIAL POWER to suspend sentence or any other process in the administration of the criminal code, and (2) from the DIFFERENT POINTS OF VIEW which developed concerning the practice of probation. The result, in the United States at any rate, has been to give to the courts A FAIRLY WIDE DISCRETION in the use of probation.

It remains to be said that WITH THE CREATION OF THE COOK COUNTY JUVENILE COURT IN 1899, PROBATION AS A PRINCIPLE AND AS A PRACTICE RECEIVED GREAT MOMENTUM. GREAT HOPES HAVE SINCE BEEN PINNED UPON IT.”
The five dates to remember from this passage. 1836 — Massachusetts recognises the release of minor offenders with sureties. 1841 — JOHN AUGUSTUS, a Boston shoemaker, coins the term and begins the practice. 1869 — Massachusetts authorises placement of youthful offenders after investigation (the origin of the pre-sentence report). 1878 — the first probation law, in Massachusetts. 1899 — the Cook County Juvenile Court in Chicago, the world’s first juvenile court, gives probation “great momentum”. Add the English precursors Diana names: benefit of clergy, the law of sanctuary, the judicial reprieve, and binding over on recognizance.
The Indian parallel history — add thiss.562 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898 (the predecessor of s.360 of the 1973 Code) was the first general Indian provision for release on probation of good conduct. The Children Act, 1908 and the provincial Children Acts, the Indian Jails Committee Report (1919–20), and the Central Legislature’s Probation of Offenders Bill, 1931 (which lapsed) followed. Several provinces enacted their own laws — the Bombay Probation of Offenders Act, 1938, and the U.P., Madras and Bengal statutes. After Independence the Criminal Procedure Code (Amendment) Act, 1955 widened s.562, and finally the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 (Act 20 of 1958) created a uniform, all-India regime, which Rattan Lal v. State of Punjab AIR 1965 SC 444 described — in the passage the DU material quotes in Abdul Qayum DU — as “a milestone in the progress of the modern liberal trend of reform in the field of penology”.

37. LEWIS DIANA: the seven conceptions of probation

VERBATIM — Lewis Diana, “DEFINITIONS OF PROBATION”, DU Case Material pp. 243–246 DU “DEFINITIONS OF PROBATION

① Probation as a LEGAL DISPOSITION Only
One point of view sees probation simply as A SUSPENSION OF SENTENCE by the court. Since sentence is not imposed, the offender remains in the community until the length of the sentence has expired, unless, of course, in the meantime he has engaged in any conduct that would warrant carrying out the sentence. THIS SYSTEM LEAVES EVERYTHING TO THE PROBATIONER AND MAKES OF PROBATION A SIMPLE POLICING PROCEDURE. Therefore, it implies TWO THINGS to the probationer: ANOTHER CHANCE, and THE THREAT OF PUNISHMENT should he fail to improve his behavior.
In point of time this view has been expressed by authors, mostly with a legal background, writing in the first decade of the twentieth century. I have found no references to it after 1908, when Judge McKenzie Cleland put it this way: probation is a plan “of suspending over offenders the maximum sentence permitted by law” and of allowing them “to determine by their subsequent conduct whether they should lose or retain their liberty… with the full knowledge that further delinquency meant… severe punishment.”

② Probation as a MEASURE OF LENIENCY
In a review of the literature I found BUT ONE AUTHOR who took this approach to probation. However, IT PROBABLY BEST REPRESENTS THE GENERAL LAY POINT OF VIEW, AS WELL AS THAT OF MOST PROBATIONERS. This fact presents a basic problem to professional personnel, who view probation as A FORM OF TREATMENT. Many offenders, however, especially among juveniles, feel their acts are UNFORTUNATE SLIPS, and while possibly inexplicable, they are, in the final analysis, CHOICES BETWEEN RIGHT AND WRONG, choices which the offenders feel capable of controlling. Consequently, in their own minds THEY ARE NOT SICK PERSONS or necessarily even the products of undesirable environments, and so certainly IN NO NEED OF TREATMENT.

③ Probation as a PUNITIVE MEASURE
This again represents a view which has found LITTLE ACCEPTANCE in the literature, especially during the last fifty years. I discovered ONLY ONE WRITER who made punishment the dominant note in his theory of probation. According to ALMY, PROBATION MUST BE PRESENTED TO THE PROBATIONER AS A FORM OF PUNISHMENT, one which permits him to ESCAPE COMMITMENT AND ITS STIGMA but one which also MAKES OTHER DEMANDS. If these demands are not met, then the probationer can expect to receive the same type of punishment as other offenders. THE ASSUMPTION UNDERLYING SUCH A VIEW IS THAT IT IS THE CERTAINTY OF PUNISHMENT WHICH DETERS.

④ Probation as an ADMINISTRATIVE PROCESS
It is likely that the earlier ideas of reform and rehabilitation attached to probation came about AS A REACTION TO THE VARIOUS ABUSES ASSOCIATED WITH THE IMPRISONMENT OF CHILDREN. As a result, A GREAT DEAL OF SENTIMENT was tied to the concept of probation in its beginnings. [On this view probation consists in the supervision, surveillance and administrative management of the offender in the community — investigation before disposition, the enforcement of conditions, home and office visits, reporting, and the use of community resources.]

⑤ Probation as SOCIAL CASEWORK TREATMENT
[The dominant professional view: probation is the application of the methods of social casework — the diagnostic study of the individual, the formulation of a plan of treatment, and a sustained professional relationship — to the offender in the community.]

⑥ Probation as a COMBINATION OF CASEWORK AND ADMINISTRATION
[The view that probation necessarily involves both a treatment relationship and an administrative or supervisory function, and that the two cannot be separated in practice.]

⑦ [Probation as the SEGAL / composite conception]
[Diana groups the first three — the Segal (legal-disposition), punitive and merciful conceptions — together as the minority views, and treats the remaining conceptions as variants of the treatment idea.]”
How to present the seven. Diana’s classification is the best answer to a short note on “What is probation?”. Present them as three non-treatment conceptions — probation as a legal disposition (suspension of sentence; “a simple policing procedure”), as leniency (“the general lay point of view”), and as punishment (Almy) — and three treatment conceptions — probation as an administrative process, as social casework, and as a combination of the two. Then give Diana’s own finding: “in the literature reviewed ONLY FIVE PER CENT of the writers thought of probation wholly as a legal disposition or as a measure of either punishment or leniency”.

38. LEWIS DIANA: the composite definition, and “Probation in Reality”

VERBATIM — Lewis Diana, DU Case Material pp. 250–253 DU “WHAT IS PROBATION?
With the exception of the first three categories (Segal, punitive, merciful), ALL VIEWS EMPHASIZE THE TREATMENT ASPECTS of probation. In the literature reviewed ONLY FIVE PERCENT of the writers thought of probation wholly as a legal disposition or as a measure of either punishment or leniency. In fact, in the literature of the past thirty years such views receive NO MENTION AT ALL. Therefore, notions of probation as either CASEWORK or ADMINISTRATION, or A COMBINATION OF THE TWO, are prominent. These leading approaches OVERLAP CONSIDERABLY so that their differentiation consists almost solely in their respective POINTS OF EMPHASIS. Thus, all three would agree that PROBATION IS A LEGAL DISPOSITION and that PROBATION IS NOT TO BE THOUGHT OF AS MERE LENIENCY OR AS MERE PUNISHMENT; but in the first instance, it is viewed as basically CASEWORK TREATMENT; in the second, ADMINISTRATIVE SUPERVISION; and in the third, BOTH of these. Each, however, contains elements of the other. So in all cases PROBATION IS SEEN AS A SOCIAL AS WELL AS A LEGAL PROCESS, AS A METHOD OF SUPERVISION AND GUIDANCE IN WHICH ALL AVAILABLE COMMUNITY RESOURCES ARE USED, AND AS [ONE] WHICH SHOULD AIM AT THE TOTAL ADJUSTMENT OF THE OFFENDER. The casework approach overshadows the rest by far, so that in phrasing a composite definition derived from the literature it should receive its obvious prominence.

THE COMPOSITE DEFINITION:
“As culled from the professional literature, then, PROBATION MAY BE THOUGHT OF AS THE APPLICATION OF MODERN, SCIENTIFIC CASEWORK TO SPECIALLY SELECTED OFFENDERS WHO ARE PLACED BY THE COURTS UNDER THE PERSONAL SUPERVISION OF A PROBATION OFFICER, SOMETIMES UNDER CONDITIONAL SUSPENSION OF PUNISHMENT, AND GIVEN TREATMENT AIMED AT THEIR COMPLETE AND PERMANENT SOCIAL REHABILITATION.”

PROBATION IN REALITY
What is depicted in the literature DOES NOT OFTEN REPRESENT A VERY REAL OR ACCURATE REPRESENTATION OF WHAT EXISTS IN REALITY. The result of ABJECT WORSHIP AT THE HOLY SHRINE OF PSYCHOANALYSIS has NOT been the development of scientifically validated techniques for the treatment of offenders on probation. In fact, FEW PROBATION OFFICERS, either in the literature or in the field, GIVE A CLEAR AND SPECIFIC DESCRIPTION OF WHAT THEY MEAN BY TREATMENT, casework or otherwise. Probation officers, whether trained in schools of social work or not, frequently express the opinion that JUST ABOUT ANYTHING THAT IS DONE in the way of investigation of cases… COMES UNDER THE HEADING OF CASEWORK TREATMENT. This would include ANY SERVICE, ADVICE, COUNSELING, OR SURVEILLANCE.

Undoubtedly part of the difficulty lies in the fact that THE FIELD OF SOCIAL WORK SEEMS TO HAVE NO WELL-DEFINED AND CONSISTENT THEORY WHICH IT CAN CALL ITS OWN. Casework can mean anything from ‘working with an offender’ to helping a ‘client’ to ‘grow’ or to achieve insight, helping him to help himself, a form of therapy, or a ‘method which recognizes the individual’s inner capacity as the key to his adjustment, and the necessity of his participating in the process of rehabilitation’. HOW THESE THINGS ARE ACCOMPLISHED, HOWEVER, IS RARELY SPECIFIED except in terms of an administrative process.…

THE EMPIRICAL FINDINGS: “It is interesting… to compare such a description of probation as casework treatment with WHAT PROBATION OFFICERS ACTUALLY DO. At the JUVENILE COURT OF ALLEGHENY COUNTY in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, it was found that MORE THAN HALF THE PROBATION OFFICERS DID ACTIVE WORK WITH ONLY THIRTY TO FORTY PERCENT OF THEIR CASELOAD. Even if telephone conversations and correspondence… are counted as contacts, SIXTY-FOUR PERCENT OF THE STAFF HAD FEWER THAN SIX CONTACTS WITH A CHILD OVER A PERIOD OF ONE YEAR. As a matter of fact, MORE THAN HALF the probation officers considered that THE MOST IMPORTANT PART OF THEIR WORK CONSISTED OF THEIR CONTACTS WITH A CHILD AND OTHERS DURING THE INVESTIGATION PERIOD PRIOR TO THE HEARING.

HALF OF THE PROBATION OFFICERS REPORTED THEY DID NO PLANNING ON ANY OF THEIR CASES, one-fourth indicated that they approached from five to ten percent of their caseload with a plan in mind, and the remaining fourth said this was true in forty to fifty percent of their cases. THIRTY-FIVE PERCENT of the probation staff felt that MANY OF THE CHILDREN UNDER THEIR SUPERVISION AT ANY ONE TIME COULD PROBABLY GET ALONG WITHOUT ANY PROBATION SERVICE AT ALL, and NINETY-FIVE PERCENT felt that SOME of the children under their supervision could adjust without it.

IT IS FAIRLY CERTAIN THAT MOST PROBATION, HOWEVER IT MAY BE CONCEIVED IN THE LITERATURE OR IN THE FIELD, STILL AMOUNTS TO LITTLE MORE THAN ADMINISTRATIVE SUPERVISION.”

42. CASE 13 — Jogi Nayak v. State (1965)

Jogi Nayak v. StateAIR 1965 SC 106 (reported also as a decision of the Orissa High Court, per R.K. Das, J.)Summarised in the DU solved paper, 2022 Answer 3
Facts
VERBATIM — DU solved paper, 2022 Answer 3 PYQ “In this case, a 15 year old person was found guilty under Section 394, IPC, i.e., causing [hurt] during robbery. He was sentenced to imprisonment for one year by the Sessions Court.”
Issue
Whether a person convicted under s.394 IPC — an offence punishable with “imprisonment for life, or with rigorous imprisonment for a term which may extend to ten years, and shall also be liable to fine” — can be released on probation under s.4 or claim the benefit of s.6 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958; that is, whether such an offence is “punishable with… imprisonment for life” within the meaning of those sections.
Arguments
For the appellant: the punishment under s.394 is in the alternative — either imprisonment for life or rigorous imprisonment up to ten years — so that the offence is not “punishable with imprisonment for life” in the exclusionary sense; the appellant was only fifteen; and the Act is a beneficent social reform to be construed liberally. For the State: the section in terms provides for imprisonment for life as one of the available punishments, and the exclusion in ss.4 and 6 is therefore attracted.
Decision
VERBATIM — DU solved paper, 2022 Answer 3 PYQ “The Orissa High Court examined the provisions of the Act and observed as under:
‘Section 4(1) of the Act lays down that when a person is guilty of an offence NOT PUNISHABLE WITH DEATH OR IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE, the Court may release him on probation of good conduct under certain circumstances. Section 6(1) makes it obligatory for the Court NOT TO PASS A SENTENCE OF IMPRISONMENT if an offender is under 21 years of age and has committed an offence punishable with imprisonment BUT NOT WITH IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE. THE OBVIOUS OBJECT OF SECTION 4(1) AND SECTION 6(1) OF THE ACT IS THAT PERSONS FOUND GUILTY OF SERIOUS OFFENCES WILL NOT BE GIVEN THE BENEFITS UNDER THE ACT. THE OFFENCE UNDER SECTION 394, IPC, IS PUNISHABLE WITH IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE OR RIGOROUS IMPRISONMENT FOR 10 YEARS AND FINE.’

Justice R.K. Das interpreted the provision ‘death or imprisonment for life’ in [the] light of the following decisions —
(2) Emperor v. Bakhsha (1934) — In this case, the accused was convicted under Section 394, IPC. The question was whether the accused aged 18 years could be bound under Section 562, CrPC [now Section 360, CrPC 1973]. Justice Shadi Lal observed that the punishment provided under Section 394, IPC IS NOT OF AN ALTERNATIVE NATURE and the offender could be awarded a punishment [of] transportation for life [i.e. imprisonment for life].
(3) State v. Shes Shankar (1956) — In this case, the accused was convicted under Section 409, IPC, where the punishment provided was of identical nature as in Section 394, IPC. The Allahabad High Court held that the sentence provided under Section 409, IPC was NOT of an alternative nature and… probation could not have been granted in such a case.

Therefore, the Orissa High Court held that the appellant COULD NOT DERIVE THE BENEFITS of the provisions of the PO Act, 1958 so as to be released on probation of good conduct. HOWEVER, keeping in mind that he was only 15 years old, and that a longer stay in the prison in [the] company of criminals would only turn him into a hardened criminal, THE HIGH COURT DIRECTED HIS RELEASE while upholding his conviction under Section 394, IPC.”
Principles evolved
(1) The words “punishable with death or imprisonment for life” in ss.4 and 6 refer to the punishment PRESCRIBED BY THE SECTION, not to the punishment actually awarded. Where life imprisonment is one of the punishments prescribed, the Act is excluded, even though the section also permits a lesser sentence. (2) “The obvious object of Section 4(1) and Section 6(1)… is that persons found guilty of SERIOUS OFFENCES will not be given the benefits under the Act.” (3) s.394 IPC is therefore outside the Probation of Offenders Act — the proposition that decides DU PYQ 2022 Q3 (§48.1). (4) But the reformative object may nevertheless be given effect in the quantum of sentence: even while refusing probation, the court released the fifteen-year-old, because “a longer stay in the prison in [the] company of criminals would only turn him into a hardened criminal” — the same reasoning as Sudesh Kumar and s.6 of the Act.
Note the modern position
Under the BNS, s.394 IPC is now s.309(6) BNS — “voluntarily causing hurt in committing or attempting to commit robbery” — punishable with imprisonment for life, or rigorous imprisonment for not less than seven years, and fine. The exclusion therefore continues. And a fifteen-year-old today would not be before a criminal court at all: he would be a child in conflict with law under the JJ Act, 2015, and the maximum institutional disposal available to the Board would be three years in a special home (Topic 4, §17.1).

43. CASE 14 — Abdul Qayum v. The State of Bihar (1972) DU verbatim

Abdul Qayum v. The State of BiharAIR 1972 SC 214; (1972) 1 SCC 103 — P. Jaganmohan Reddy, J.Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 258–260
Facts
VERBATIM — para 1 DU “This Appeal is by Special Leave against the Judgment of the Patna High Court exercising its Revisional jurisdiction by which the benefit of the provisions of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958… was DENIED to the Appellant Qayum. The Appellant was convicted under Section 379 of the Indian Penal Code and sentenced to rigorous imprisonment for six months. The reasons given by the trial court — reproduced verbatim
VERBATIM DU “In spite of his recommendations I do not feel inclined to extend the benefit of the provisions of the Probation of Offenders Act to accused Qayum. APPARENTLY HE IS AN ASSOCIATE OF ACCUSED SHAMIM WHO IS A HARDENED CRIMINAL AND A PERSON OF DOUBTFUL CHARACTER. INCIDENTS OF PICK-POCKETING ARE VERY RAMPANT IN THIS SUBDIVISION AND IT WAS JUST A STRAY CHANCE THAT ACCUSED QAYUM WAS CAUGHT IN THIS CASE. Having regard to these facts and the nature of offence and the circumstances in which accused Qayum was caught, HE DOES NOT DESERVE THE BENEFIT OF SECTION 4 OF THE ACT.”
The appeal was dismissed, and the Patna High Court in revision rejected the plea “because of ‘the association of the petitioner with such a hardened criminal and a pick pocket’”.
Issues
(1) What is the object and the correct approach to the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958? (2) Did the courts below apply their minds to the requirements of s.6? (3) Can the appellant be denied the benefit on the ground of his supposed association with a hardened criminal?
Arguments
For the appellant: he was sixteen at the time of the offence and about eighteen at conviction, so s.6 applied and imposed an injunction against imprisonment unless the court was satisfied to the contrary and recorded reasons; the Probation Officer had recommended release; there was nothing in the report to show that he was an associate of Shamim or a hardened criminal; and the offence was a petty theft of ₹56 which the complainant had himself wished to compound. For the State: the offence of pick-pocketing was rampant in the locality; the appellant had acted in concert with a known hardened pick-pocket; and the discretion under ss.4 and 6 had been exercised by three courts.
Decision and reasoning
VERBATIM — paras 3–5 DU “3. In our view NEITHER the Trial Court, the Appellate Court, NOR the High Court APPLIED THEIR MIND to the requirement of the provision[s] of the Act. As pointed out by this Court in Rattan Lal v. The State of Punjab [AIR 1965 SC 444], ‘THE ACT IS A MILESTONE IN THE PROGRESS OF THE MODERN LIBERAL TREND OF REFORM IN THE FIELD OF PENOLOGY. IT IS THE RESULT OF THE RECOGNITION OF THE DOCTRINE THAT THE OBJECT OF CRIMINAL LAW IS MORE TO REFORM THE INDIVIDUAL OFFENDER THAN TO PUNISH HIM.’ The provisions of the Act must therefore be viewed in the light of this LAUDABLE REFORMATORY OBJECT which the legislature was seeking to achieve by enacting the legislation. The Act differentiate[s] offenders below 21 years of age who are guilty of having committed an offence punishable with death or imprisonment for life and those who are guilty of a lesser offence. It is only in the case of offenders who are below the age of 21 years and guilty of lesser offences than those punishable with death and life imprisonment that AN INJUNCTION IS ISSUED TO THE COURT NOT TO SENTENCE THEM TO IMPRISONMENT UNLESS IT IS SATISFIED that, having regard to the circumstances of the case including the nature of the offence and the character of the offenders, it is not desirable to deal with them under Section 3 and Section 4. It is also provided in Sub-section 2 of Section 6 that the Court SHALL, for the purposes of satisfying itself whether it should give the offender the benefit referred to in Sub-section (1), CALL FOR AND CONSIDER A REPORT FROM A PROBATION OFFICER along with any other information available to it relating to the character, physical and mental condition of the offender.

4. …Section 3 empowers the Court to release an offender AFTER ADMONITION where he has been found guilty of having committed an offence under Sections 379, 380, 381, 404 or 420 or any offence punishable with imprisonment of not more than two years or fine or both… and there is no previous conviction proved against him; while Section 4 empowers it to release an offender ON PROBATION of good [conduct] where it considers it EXPEDIENT to do so instead of sentencing him at once to any punishment. In Rattan Lal case a question had arisen as to whether Section 11(2) of the Act circumscribes the jurisdiction of an Appellate Court to make an order under the Act only in a case where the Trial Court could have made that order… It was held that the phraseology used therein was WIDE ENOUGH to enable the Appellate Court or the High Court to make such an order and that IT WAS PURPOSELY MADE COMPREHENSIVE AS THE ACT WAS MADE TO IMPLEMENT A SOCIAL REFORM…

5. In this case it is true that the Trial Court, the Appellate Court as well as the High Court did consider the question… but in our view THEY HAVE COMPLETELY MISDIRECTED THEMSELVES to the essential requirements of that provision. THE PROBATION OFFICER’S REPORT NOWHERE INDICATES THAT THE ACCUSED IS ‘AN ASSOCIATE OF ACCUSED SHAMIM’. The High Court thought apparently he is an associate of Shamim. EVEN IF SHAMIM WAS A HARDENED CRIMINAL as it appears from the Probation Officer’s report dealing with that accused, THERE WAS NO WARRANT FOR INFERRING THAT THE APPELLANT WAS HIS ASSOCIATE.

[The contents of the report:] “A reference to the report of the Probation Officer dated 7-8-65 would show that the accused was approximately 18 years of age and was PHYSICALLY AND MENTALLY NORMAL. Though he was ILLITERATE he had A VOCATIONAL APTITUDE FOR TAILORING and was working in the Bihar Tailoring Works. He was interested [in] his work as a tailor and BEHAVES PROPERLY WITH HIS FATHER AND BROTHERS and has NORMAL ASSOCIATION WITH FRIENDS. Unfortunately HE LOST HIS MOTHER WHEN HE WAS 10 YEARS OLD, and his family history disclosed… that HE COMES FROM A POOR FAMILY and though he has no landed property he has a house of his own to live in. Both his father and his elder brother are employed. THE ATTITUDE OF THE FAMILY TOWARDS THE OFFENDER… WAS ONE OF SYMPATHY AND AFFECTION and the father exercised reasonable control over him. THE REPORT OF THE NEIGHBOURS IS ALSO IN HIS FAVOUR. In the end the Probation Officer expressed the view that THERE IS NO REPORT AGAINST THE CHARACTER OF THE OFFENDER, no previous conviction has been proved against him prior to this case, and in the circumstances… THE RELEASE ON PROBATION MAY BE A SUITABLE METHOD TO DEAL WITH HIM. He therefore recommended that he be released on probation by getting his father to execute a suitable security.”

[The conclusion — the most quoted sentence in the case:] “This report in our view does not justify the conclusion that the appellant is either a hardened criminal or is associated with hardened criminals for denying him the benefit of the provisions of the Act. TO SENTENCE HIM TO IMPRISONMENT WOULD ITSELF ACHIEVE THE OBJECT OF ASSOCIATING HIM WITH HARDENED CRIMINALS, WHICH ASSOCIATION THE COURTS THOUGHT WAS A GOOD GROUND FOR DENYING HIM THE BENEFIT OF BEING RELEASED ON PROBATION. We have no doubt that IF HE IS RELEASED ON PROBATION OF GOOD CONDUCT THERE IS HOPE OF HIS BEING RECLAIMED AND AFFORDED THE OPPORTUNITY TO LIVE A NORMAL LIFE OF A LAW ABIDING CITIZEN. In this view the Appeal is allowed and the sentence is set aside with the direction that he be RELEASED UNDER SECTION 4 OF THE ACT on his entering into a bond, with his father as a surety in the sum of Rs 500, to appear and receive sentence by the Trial Court whenever called upon to do so WITHIN A PERIOD OF ONE YEAR and during that time TO KEEP THE PEACE AND BE OF GOOD BEHAVIOUR.…”
Principles evolved
(1) The object of the Act, quoting Rattan Lal: “The Act is A MILESTONE IN THE PROGRESS OF THE MODERN LIBERAL TREND OF REFORM IN THE FIELD OF PENOLOGY. It is the result of the recognition of the doctrine that THE OBJECT OF CRIMINAL LAW IS MORE TO REFORM THE INDIVIDUAL OFFENDER THAN TO PUNISH HIM.” Every probation answer should open with this sentence. (2) s.6 is an INJUNCTION, not a discretion, in the case of an offender under 21 guilty of an offence not punishable with death or life imprisonment. (3) s.6(2) obliges the court to CALL FOR AND CONSIDER the Probation Officer’s report, together with other information about the offender’s character and physical and mental condition. (4) A court may not go BEYOND the report and invent adverse material. There was “no warrant for inferring” that the appellant was an associate of a hardened criminal when the report said nothing of the kind. (5) THE PARADOX AT THE HEART OF THE CASE: “To sentence him to imprisonment would itself achieve the object of associating him with hardened criminals, which association the courts thought was a good ground for denying him the benefit of being released on probation.” This is differential association (Topic 2) deployed as a rule of sentencing law, and it is the single most quotable line in the probation material. (6) Under s.11, an appellate court or the High Court — and the Supreme Court — may itself make an order under the Act, “as the Act was made to implement a social reform”.
The DU solved paper’s summary
VERBATIM — DU solved paper, 2022 PYQ “The 16 year old appellant was convicted under Section 379, IPC and sentenced to 6 months rigorous imprisonment as he was found to be an associate of a hardened criminal. However, the Apex Court recommended his release on probation and observed that the object of the criminal law is [more] to reform the individual offender than to punish him. Therefore, the provisions of the PO Act must be construed in the light of the said object so that the offender is given an opportunity to redeem himself and lead a normal life as a law abiding citizen.”

44. CASE 15 — State v. Bhola (2003)

State of Maharashtra v. Bhola (also reported as State v. Bhola)(2003) 3 SCC 1gravity of offence and probation
Facts and issue
The respondent had been convicted of an offence, and the High Court, in exercise of its powers, had extended to him the benefit of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958. The State appealed, contending that in view of the nature and gravity of the offence and the circumstances in which it had been committed, the benefit ought not to have been given. The question was therefore the scope of the word “expedient” in s.4(1) and the extent to which an appellate court will interfere with the exercise of the discretion.
Arguments
For the State: s.4 requires the court to have regard to “the circumstances of the case including the nature of the offence and the character of the offender”; the discretion is not to be exercised mechanically or as a matter of sympathy; and in offences that are grave, or that affect the public at large, the benefit must be refused. For the respondent: the Act is a beneficent social legislation to be liberally construed (Rattan Lal; Abdul Qayum); the respondent had no previous conviction; and the discretion having been exercised by the High Court, the Supreme Court should not interfere.
Decision and principle
The Supreme Court held that the benefit of the Probation of Offenders Act is not to be extended as a matter of course. The discretion under s.4 must be exercised judicially, having regard to the nature of the offence, the circumstances in which it was committed and the character and antecedents of the offender; the court must form a positive opinion that release is “expedient”; and the Probation Officer’s report must be considered. Where the offence is grave, or where the offender’s conduct or antecedents do not warrant it, the benefit must be refused, and the court must say why. The Court accordingly interfered with the order below.
How to use it
As the counterweight to Abdul Qayum. Together the two cases state the whole law of judicial discretion under s.4: Abdul Qayum — the court must not refuse the benefit on material that does not exist, and must remember that the object of the Act is reform; State v. Bhola — the court must not grant it mechanically, and the nature of the offence is a relevant and sometimes decisive consideration. Read both with Dalbir Singh (§45), which applies the Bhola principle to a specific class of offence, and with the DU 2017 answer PYQ: “the benefit must not be given in view of the NATURE OF THE OFFENCE and [the] DEPRAVITY OF [THE] OFFENDER. As the offence is SOCIO-ECONOMIC in nature, the benefit must not be given.” Add Phul Singh v. State of Haryana (1979) 4 SCC 413, cited in the DU solved paper: “the provisions of Section 4 of the PO Act should not be liberally applied in undeserving cases where a person in his early 20s commits rape” PYQ.

45. CASE 16 — Dalbir Singh v. State of Haryana (2000) DU verbatim

Dalbir Singh v. State of Haryana(2000) 5 SCC 82 — K.T. Thomas and R.P. Sethi, JJ. (judgment of THOMAS, J.)Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 261–263
The opening — the most quoted passage on deterrence in the prescribed cases
VERBATIM — para 1 DU “WHEN AUTOMOBILES HAVE BECOME DEATH TRAPS, ANY LENIENCY SHOWN TO DRIVERS WHO ARE FOUND GUILTY OF RASH DRIVING WOULD BE AT THE RISK OF FURTHER ESCALATION OF ROAD ACCIDENTS. ALL THOSE WHO ARE MANNING THE STEERING OF AUTOMOBILES, PARTICULARLY PROFESSIONAL DRIVERS, MUST BE KEPT UNDER CONSTANT REMINDERS OF THEIR DUTY TO ADOPT UTMOST CARE AND ALSO OF THE CONSEQUENCES BEFALLING THEM IN CASES OF DERELICTION. ONE OF THE MOST EFFECTIVE WAYS OF KEEPING SUCH DRIVERS UNDER MENTAL VIGIL IS TO MAINTAIN A DETERRENT ELEMENT IN THE SENTENCING SPHERE. ANY LATITUDE SHOWN TO THEM IN THAT SPHERE WOULD TEMPT THEM TO MAKE DRIVING FRIVOLOUS AND A FROLIC.”
Facts
“A man who drove a stage-carriage knocked down a cyclist who succumbed to his injuries.” The appellant was convicted under s.304-A IPC (causing death by a rash or negligent act not amounting to culpable homicide) and sentenced to imprisonment; his conviction was upheld through the appellate hierarchy. Before the Supreme Court he did not press the conviction but sought the benefit of s.4 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958.
Issue
Whether it is “expedient” within the meaning of s.4(1) of the Probation of Offenders Act to release on probation a person convicted under s.304-A IPC of causing death by rash or negligent driving.
Arguments
For the appellant: s.304-A is punishable with imprisonment of either description for up to two years, or fine, or both — well within the scope of s.4 (and indeed of s.3); the appellant had no previous conviction; the offence involved no intention; and the Act is a beneficent statute to be liberally construed. For the State: the word “expedient” requires the court to have regard to “the circumstances of the case including the nature of the offence”; road accidents have assumed alarming proportions; and professional drivers must be deterred.
Decision and reasoning
VERBATIM — paras 9–14 DU [On the meaning of “expedient”:] “…in another shade, it means a device ‘characterised by mere utility rather than principle, conducive to special advantage rather than to what is universally right’ (see Webster’s New International Dictionary).
10. It was then held that the court must construe the said word in keeping with the CONTEXT AND OBJECT of the provision in its widest amplitude. Here the word ‘EXPEDIENT’ is used in Section 4 of the PO Act in the context of casting a duty on the court to take into account ‘THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CASE INCLUDING THE NATURE OF THE OFFENCE…’. This means SECTION 4 CAN BE RESORTED TO WHEN THE COURT CONSIDERS THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CASE, PARTICULARLY THE NATURE OF THE OFFENCE, AND THE COURT FORMS ITS OPINION THAT IT IS SUITABLE AND APPROPRIATE FOR ACCOMPLISHING A SPECIFIED OBJECT THAT THE OFFENDER CAN BE RELEASED ON PROBATION OF GOOD CONDUCT.

11. Courts must bear in mind that when any plea is made based on Section 4 of the PO Act for application to a convicted person under Section 304-A IPC, THAT ROAD ACCIDENTS HAVE PROLIFERATED TO AN ALARMING EXTENT AND THE TOLL IS GALLOPING DAY BY DAY IN INDIA, and that NO SOLUTION IS IN SIGHT nor suggested by any quarter to bring them down. When this Court lamented two decades ago that ‘MORE PEOPLE DIE OF ROAD ACCIDENTS THAN BY MOST DISEASES, SO MUCH SO THE INDIAN HIGHWAYS ARE AMONG THE TOP KILLERS OF THE COUNTRY’, the saturation of accident[s] toll was not even half of what it is today. So V.R. Krishna Iyer, J. has suggested in the said decision thus: ‘Rashness and negligence are RELATIVE CONCEPTS, NOT ABSOLUTE ABSTRACTIONS. In our current conditions, the law under Section 304-A IPC and under the rubric of negligence, must have DUE REGARD TO THE FATAL FREQUENCY OF RASH DRIVING OF HEAVY DUTY VEHICLES AND OF SPEEDING MENACES.’

12. In State of Karnataka v. Krishna [(2000) 4 SCC 75] this Court did not allow a sentence of fine, imposed on a driver who was convicted under Section 304-A IPC, to remain in force, although the High Court too had confirmed the said sentence when an accused was convicted of the offence of driving a bus callously and causing the death of a human being. In that case this Court enhanced the sentence to rigorous imprisonment for six months besides imposing a fine.

13. Bearing in mind the galloping trend in road accidents in India and the devastating consequences visiting the victims and their families, CRIMINAL COURTS CANNOT TREAT THE NATURE OF THE OFFENCE UNDER SECTION 304-A IPC AS ATTRACTING THE BENEVOLENT PROVISIONS OF SECTION 4 OF THE PO ACT. While considering the quantum of sentence to be imposed for the offence of causing death by rash or negligent driving of automobiles, ONE OF THE PRIME CONSIDERATIONS SHOULD BE DETERRENCE. A professional driver pedals the accelerator of the automobile ALMOST THROUGHOUT HIS WORKING HOURS. HE MUST CONSTANTLY INFORM HIMSELF THAT HE CANNOT AFFORD TO HAVE A SINGLE MOMENT OF LAXITY OR INATTENTIVENESS WHEN HIS LEG IS ON THE PEDAL OF A VEHICLE IN LOCOMOTION. HE CANNOT AND SHOULD NOT TAKE A CHANCE THINKING THAT A RASH DRIVING NEED NOT NECESSARILY CAUSE ANY ACCIDENT; OR EVEN IF ANY ACCIDENT OCCURS IT NEED NOT NECESSARILY RESULT IN THE DEATH OF ANY HUMAN BEING; OR EVEN IF SUCH DEATH ENSUES HE MIGHT NOT BE CONVICTED OF THE OFFENCE; AND LASTLY, THAT EVEN IF HE IS CONVICTED HE WOULD BE DEALT WITH LENIENTLY BY THE COURT. HE MUST ALWAYS KEEP IN HIS MIND THE FEAR PSYCHE THAT IF HE IS CONVICTED OF THE OFFENCE FOR CAUSING DEATH OF A HUMAN BEING DUE TO HIS CALLOUS DRIVING OF THE VEHICLE HE CANNOT ESCAPE FROM A JAIL SENTENCE. THIS IS THE ROLE WHICH THE COURTS CAN PLAY, PARTICULARLY AT THE LEVEL OF TRIAL COURTS, FOR LESSENING THE HIGH RATE OF MOTOR ACCIDENTS DUE TO CALLOUS DRIVING OF AUTOMOBILES.

14. Thus, bestowing our serious consideration on the arguments addressed by the learned counsel for the appellant, WE EXPRESS OUR INABILITY TO LEAN TOWARDS THE BENEVOLENT PROVISION IN SECTION 4 OF THE PO ACT. THE APPEAL IS ACCORDINGLY DISMISSED.”
Principles evolved
(1) “Expedient” in s.4(1) is to be construed in the context and object of the provision, which casts a duty on the court to have regard to the circumstances of the case “including the nature of the offence”. (2) An offence under s.304-A IPC arising from rash or negligent driving does NOT attract the benefit of s.4. This is a rule of general application, stated as a class rule and not merely on the facts — the most significant judicial carve-out from the Probation Act. (3) In sentencing for causing death by rash or negligent driving, “one of the prime considerations should be DETERRENCE”; and the driver “must always keep in his mind THE FEAR PSYCHE that if he is convicted… HE CANNOT ESCAPE FROM A JAIL SENTENCE”. (4) “Rashness and negligence are RELATIVE concepts, NOT ABSOLUTE ABSTRACTIONS” — the standard of care is to be judged against contemporary conditions (Krishna Iyer J.). (5) The role is one “which the courts can play, PARTICULARLY AT THE LEVEL OF TRIAL COURTS” — a direction addressed to the sentencing practice of the subordinate judiciary.
Why the case is the perfect foil to Abdul Qayum
Both are probation cases, both were decided under s.4, and they reach opposite results for opposite reasons. Abdul Qayum is the reformative pole — the object of criminal law is “more to reform the individual offender than to punish him”. Dalbir Singh is the deterrent pole — “one of the most effective ways of keeping such drivers under mental vigil is to maintain a deterrent element in the sentencing sphere”. Together they demonstrate that the Probation Act is not a charter of leniency but a statutory discretion whose exercise depends on which theory of punishment the offence calls for. That is the ideal conclusion to a question on probation.
The modern context
India records roughly 1.5 lakh road-accident deaths a year — among the highest in the world. s.304-A IPC is now s.106(1) BNS, and the maximum has been raised from two years to FIVE; and the new s.106(2) BNS punishes a driver who causes death by rash or negligent driving and escapes without reporting with up to ten years. The Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019 enhanced the penalties under ss.183–199 substantially. Dalbir Singh therefore reads as a judicial anticipation of the legislative response. Cite it with State of Punjab v. Saurabh Bakshi (2015) 5 SCC 182, in which the Supreme Court again deprecated the practice of imposing token sentences under s.304-A and urged the legislature to enhance the punishment.

46. CASE 17 — M.C.D. v. State of Delhi (2005) DU verbatim

Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. State of Delhi & Anr.(2005) 4 SCC 605 — Ashok Bhan and A.R. Lakshmanan, JJ.Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 264–270
Facts
VERBATIM — paras 2–7 DU “2. The Municipal Corporation of Delhi, aggrieved against the judgment… of the High Court of Delhi in Criminal Revision Petition No. 185 of 2004, by which order the High Court gave the benefit of probation under Section 4 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958… to the second respondent Gurcharan Singh but maintained the conviction, preferred the above appeal.
3. …One Mr M.K. Verma (PW 4), Junior Engineer, Civil Line Zone, visited 189, Prem Gali, Punja Sharif, Mori Gate where he found unauthorised construction going on at the first floor… FIR was prepared… [and notice issued] under Sections 343/344 of the Delhi Municipal Corporation Act, 1957… Subsequently, the second respondent along with Kuldeep Singh were prosecuted for commission of offences under Sections 332 and 461 of the DMC Act before the Designated Municipal Court.
4. The trial court… convicted the second respondent… and sentenced him to SIX MONTHS’ SIMPLE IMPRISONMENT and imposed a fine of Rs 5000.
5. …the Sessions Court… dismissed the appeal…
6. Against the judgment… the accused filed Criminal Revision Petition No. 185 of 2004 before the High Court… At the time of arguments, the advocate for the accused submitted… that the accused did not wish to challenge the conviction on merits and stated it [to be] a fit case… to be admitted to the benefit of the POB Act on the ground that the accused faced trial for 12 YEARS in the lower courts and remained in jail for THREE DAYS.
7. The High Court… held that the accused suffered the agony of trial lasting for 12 years… there is no allegation that the petitioner-accused is a previous convict, and… the accused deserved the benefit of probation under Section 4 of the POB Act, and while maintaining the conviction, the sentence of imprisonment and fine as awarded to him was set aside.”
The critical fact that emerged before the Supreme Court: the second respondent had, in fact, been convicted by a Magistrate on an earlier occasion (Criminal Case No. 202 of 1997), and had suppressed that fact from the High Court.
Issues
(1) Is the calling for and consideration of the Probation Officer’s report under s.4(2) mandatory? (2) Can a previous convict be released under s.4? (3) What is the effect of the accused’s suppression of a material fact?
Arguments
For the appellant Corporation (Mr Ashwani Kumar, Senior Counsel): the High Court had disposed of the revision without giving the Corporation an opportunity to file a counter-affidavit; it had granted probation without calling for the Probation Officer’s report, which s.4(2) makes mandatory; and the accused was a previous convict and had deliberately suppressed that fact. For the respondent (Mr Jaspal Singh, Senior Counsel): the conviction had not been challenged; the accused had suffered a trial of twelve years and some custody; the offence was a municipal-law offence of unauthorised construction; and the grant of probation was an exercise of discretion with which the Supreme Court ought not to interfere.
Decision and reasoning
VERBATIM — paras 21–25 DU [On the suppression:] “…If he withholds a vital document in order to gain advantage on the other side then he would be GUILTY OF PLAYING FRAUD ON THE COURT AS WELL AS ON THE OPPOSITE PARTY. The second respondent… was not justified in suppressing the material fact that he was convicted by the Magistrate on an earlier occasion. Since the second respondent DELIBERATELY SUPPRESSED THE CRUCIAL AND IMPORTANT FACT, WE DISAPPROVE STRONGLY… the conduct of the second respondent, and by reason of such conduct, THE SECOND RESPONDENT DISENTITLED HIMSELF FROM GETTING ANY RELIEF OR ASSISTANCE FROM THIS COURT. We, however, part with this case with a heavy heart expressing our strong disapproval of the conduct and behaviour, but direct that the second respondent [is] to pay a sum of Rs 10,000 by way of cost to the appellant…

22. We have already reproduced Section 4 of the POB Act. IT APPLIES TO ALL KINDS OF OFFENDERS WHETHER UNDER OR ABOVE 21 YEARS OF AGE. THIS SECTION IS INTENDED TO ATTEMPT POSSIBLE REFORMATION OF AN OFFENDER INSTEAD OF INFLICTING ON HIM THE NORMAL PUNISHMENT OF HIS CRIME. THE ONLY LIMITATION IMPOSED BY SECTION 6 IS THAT IN THE FIRST INSTANCE AN OFFENDER UNDER TWENTY-ONE YEARS OF AGE WILL NOT BE SENTENCED TO IMPRISONMENT. While extending [the] benefit of this [section], THE DISCRETION OF THE COURT HAS TO BE EXERCISED HAVING REGARD TO THE CIRCUMSTANCES IN WHICH THE CRIME WAS COMMITTED, THE AGE, CHARACTER AND ANTECEDENTS OF THE OFFENDER. SUCH EXERCISE OF DISCRETION NEEDS A SENSE OF RESPONSIBILITY. The offender can only be released on probation of good conduct under this section when the court forms an opinion, having considered the circumstances of the case, the nature of the offence and the character of the offender, that in a particular case, the offender should be released… The section itself is clear that BEFORE APPLYING THE SECTION, THE MAGISTRATE SHOULD CAREFULLY TAKE INTO CONSIDERATION THE ATTENDANT CIRCUMSTANCES.

THE SECOND RESPONDENT IS A PREVIOUS CONVICT AS PER THE RECORDS PLACED BEFORE US. SUCH A PREVIOUS CONVICT CANNOT BE RELEASED IN VIEW OF SECTION 4 OF THE POB ACT. THE COURT IS BOUND TO CALL FOR A REPORT AS PER SECTION 4 OF THE POB ACT, BUT THE HIGH COURT HAS FAILED TO DO SO — ALTHOUGH THE COURT IS NOT BOUND BY THE REPORT OF THE PROBATION OFFICER, IT MUST CALL FOR SUCH A REPORT BEFORE THE CASE COMES TO ITS CONCLUSION. THE WORD ‘SHALL’ IN SUB-SECTION (2) OF SECTION 4 IS MANDATORY, AND THE CONSIDERATION OF THE REPORT OF THE PROBATION OFFICER IS A CONDITION PRECEDENT TO THE RELEASE OF THE ACCUSED, AND A RELEASE WITHOUT SUCH A REPORT WOULD, THEREFORE, BE ILLEGAL.

23. In Ram Singh v. State of Haryana [(1971) 3 SCC 914] a Bench of two Judges of this Court… observed: ‘Counsel for the appellants invoked the application of the Probation of Offenders Act. Sections 4 and 6 of the Act indicate the procedure requiring the court TO CALL FOR A REPORT from the probation officer and CONSIDERATION of the report and any other information available relating to the character and physical and mental condition of the offender. THESE FACTS ARE OF PRIMARY IMPORTANCE before the court can pass an order under the Probation of Offenders Act.’

24. In R. Mahalingam v. G. Padmavathi [(1979) Cri LJ (NOC) 20 (Mad)] the Court observed: ‘If any report is filed by the probation officer, the court is BOUND to consider it. OBTAINING SUCH A REPORT OF THE PROBATION OFFICER IS MANDATORY, since sub-section (1) of Section 4 says that the court SHALL consider the report of the probation officer. WORDS “IF ANY” DO NOT MEAN THAT THE COURT NEED NOT CALL FOR A REPORT from the probation officer. The words “if any” would only cover a case where, notwithstanding such requisition, the probation officer for one reason or other HAS NOT SUBMITTED A REPORT. Before deciding to act under Section 4(1), IT IS MANDATORY ON THE PART OF THE COURT TO CALL FOR A REPORT from the probation officer, and if such a report is received, IT IS MANDATORY ON THE PART OF THE COURT TO CONSIDER THE REPORT. But if for one reason or the other such a report is not forthcoming, the court has to decide the matter on other materials available to it… In the instant case, the Magistrate passed [an] order releasing the accused on probation WITHOUT TAKING INTO CONSIDERATION THEIR CHARACTER. HELD, THE REQUIREMENT OF SECTION 4(1) WAS NOT FULFILLED and therefore the case [was] remanded.’

25. Since the High Court has disposed of the criminal revision without giving an opportunity of filing [a] counter-affidavit to the counsel for MCD and… the respondent did not disclose the fact… that he has also been convicted in another Criminal Case No. 202 of 1997, the judgment impugned in this appeal cannot be allowed to stand. We, therefore, have no hesitation in setting aside the order impugned and remit[ting] the matter to the High Court for fresh disposal strictly in accordance with law.

26. The appeal is, accordingly, allowed with costs of Rs 10,000 to be paid by the second respondent to the appellant.”
Principles evolved
(1) s.4 applies to ALL offenders, whether above or below 21. The only age-based limitation is in s.6, which protects offenders under 21 from imprisonment in the first instance. (2) THE WORD “SHALL” IN s.4(2) IS MANDATORY. The court is bound to call for the Probation Officer’s report, and consideration of that report is a CONDITION PRECEDENT to release. A release without such a report is ILLEGAL. (3) The court is not bound by the report, but it must call for it and consider it — the two propositions must be stated together. (4) The words “if any” in s.4(2) do not dispense with the requirement of calling for a report; they cover only the case where the officer, having been required, fails to submit one. (5) A previous convict ordinarily cannot be released under s.4; “such a previous convict cannot be released in view of Section 4”. (Strictly, s.4 contains no express bar on previous convictions — unlike s.3 — so the proposition is best understood as a very strong indication against the exercise of the discretion, since character and antecedents are expressly relevant.) (6) Suppression of a material fact is a fraud on the court and disentitles the party to discretionary relief. (7) The discretion “needs a sense of responsibility” and must be exercised having regard to the circumstances of the crime and the age, character and antecedents of the offender.
Practical importance
This is the case that makes Lewis Diana’s critique operative in Indian law. Diana found that probation in practice “amounts to little more than administrative supervision” because the investigation is perfunctory; M.C.D. makes the investigation itself a condition precedent. Cite it in every probation problem: the third question the court must ask (Figure 6) is whether the report has been called for.

47. CASE 18 — Chhanni v. State of U.P. (2006) DU verbatim

Chhanni v. State of Uttar Pradesh(2006) 5 SCC 396 — Arijit Pasayat and S.H. Kapadia, JJ. (judgment of ARIJIT PASAYAT, J.)Reproduced in the DU Case Material, pp. 271–275
Facts
VERBATIM — paras 2–4 DU “2. The appellant calls in question [the] legality of the order passed by a learned Single Judge of the Allahabad High Court, Lucknow Bench, by which three appeals were disposed of rejecting the prayer made for MODIFICATION of the judgment… Appellant Chhanni was convicted for the offences punishable under Sections 304 Part II, 323/149 and 147 IPC. He was sentenced to five years’ RI on the first count and six months’ RI and a fine of Rs 250 on the second count and one year’s RI on the third count. …So far as the appeal filed by the present appellant is concerned, the same was partly allowed. His conviction under Section 304 Part II IPC and the sentence thereunder was SET ASIDE, but he was convicted under Section 323 IPC and sentenced to undergo ONE YEAR’S RI.…
3. An application was filed by the appellant before the High Court… for modification of the judgment… The prayer was that he should be directed to be released on probation under Section 4 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958… or in the alternative under Section 360 of the Code… The High Court noted that there was NO PROVISION FOR PERMITTING MODIFICATION OF AN ORDER and in fact the plea which was pressed into service was NOT URGED before the High Court when the criminal appeal was heard. 4. Learned counsel for the appellant submitted that when the matter was called before the High Court, the appellant’s counsel was NOT PRESENT. But considering the fact that the appeal was pending for more than a decade, the High Court heard the learned counsel for the State and passed a judgment the modification of which was sought for. Because of genuine difficulties the appellant’s counsel could not be present. In any event the High Court had set aside the conviction in terms of Section 304 Part II IPC.
5. There is no appearance on behalf of the State of U.P. in spite of notice.”
Issues
(1) What is the relationship between s.360 CrPC and the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958? Can they operate simultaneously in the same area? (2) Should the appellant be given an opportunity to be considered for probation, although the plea was not urged when the appeal was heard?
Decision — the central holding
VERBATIM — paras 6–9 DU “6. WHERE THE PROVISIONS OF THE PROBATION ACT ARE APPLICABLE, THE EMPLOYMENT OF SECTION 360 OF THE CODE IS NOT TO BE MADE. In cases of such application, IT WOULD BE AN ILLEGALITY RESULTING IN HIGHLY UNDESIRABLE CONSEQUENCES, which the legislature, that gave birth to the Probation Act and the Code, wanted to obviate. Yet the legislature in its wisdom HAS OBLIGED THE COURT UNDER SECTION 361 OF THE CODE TO APPLY ONE OR THE OTHER BENEFICIAL PROVISION; be it Section 360 of the Code or the provisions of the Probation Act. IT IS ONLY BY PROVIDING SPECIAL REASONS THAT THEIR APPLICABILITY CAN BE WITHHELD BY THE COURT. The comparative elevation of the provisions of the Probation Act [is] further noticed in SUB-SECTION (10) OF SECTION 360 of the Code, which makes it clear that NOTHING IN THE SAID SECTION SHALL AFFECT THE PROVISIONS OF THE PROBATION ACT. THOSE PROVISIONS HAVE A PARAMOUNTCY OF THEIR OWN IN THE RESPECTIVE AREAS WHERE THEY ARE APPLICABLE.

7. SECTION 360 OF THE CODE relates ONLY to persons NOT UNDER 21 YEARS OF AGE convicted for an offence punishable with FINE ONLY OR WITH IMPRISONMENT FOR A TERM OF SEVEN YEARS OR LESS, [and] to any person UNDER 21 YEARS OF AGE OR ANY WOMAN convicted of an offence NOT PUNISHABLE WITH SENTENCE OF DEATH OR IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE. THE SCOPE OF SECTION 4 OF THE PROBATION ACT IS MUCH WIDER. IT APPLIES TO ANY PERSON FOUND GUILTY OF HAVING COMMITTED AN OFFENCE NOT PUNISHABLE WITH DEATH OR IMPRISONMENT FOR LIFE. SECTION 360 OF THE CODE DOES NOT PROVIDE FOR ANY ROLE FOR PROBATION OFFICERS in assisting the courts in relation to supervision and other matters, WHILE THE PROBATION ACT DOES make such a provision. WHILE SECTION 12 OF THE PROBATION ACT STATES THAT THE PERSON FOUND GUILTY OF AN OFFENCE AND DEALT WITH UNDER SECTION 3 OR 4 OF THE PROBATION ACT SHALL NOT SUFFER DISQUALIFICATION, IF ANY, ATTACHED TO CONVICTION OF AN OFFENCE UNDER ANY LAW, THE CODE DOES NOT CONTAIN [A] PARALLEL PROVISION. TWO STATUTES WITH SUCH SIGNIFICANT DIFFERENCES COULD NOT BE INTENDED TO COEXIST AT THE SAME TIME IN THE SAME AREA. SUCH COEXISTENCE WOULD LEAD TO ANOMALOUS RESULTS. The intention to retain the provisions of Section 360 of the Code and the provisions of the Probation Act as applicable at the same time in a given area cannot be gathered from the provisions of Section 360 or any other provision of the Code. THEREFORE, BY VIRTUE OF SECTION 8(1) OF THE GENERAL CLAUSES ACT, WHERE THE PROVISIONS OF THE ACT HAVE BEEN BROUGHT INTO FORCE, THE PROVISIONS OF SECTION 360 OF THE CODE ARE WHOLLY INAPPLICABLE.

8. ENFORCEMENT OF THE PROBATION ACT IN SOME PARTICULAR AREA EXCLUDES THE APPLICABILITY OF THE PROVISIONS OF SECTIONS 360 AND 361 OF THE CODE IN THAT AREA.

9. The High Court is justified in its view that there is no provision for modification of the judgment. BUT CONSIDERING THE PECULIAR CIRCUMSTANCES WE DIRECT THE HIGH COURT TO CONSIDER THE APPLICATION UNDER THE PROBATION ACT OR SECTION 360 OF THE CODE, AS THE CASE MAY BE, so far as the appellant is concerned, AND PASS THE APPROPRIATE ORDER WITHIN THREE MONTHS from the receipt of this order. We make it clear that WE HAVE NOT EXPRESSED ANY OPINION AS REGARDS THE MERITS.
10. The appeal is allowed.”
Principles evolved
(1) THE TWO REGIMES CANNOT COEXIST. “Where the provisions of the Probation Act are applicable, the employment of Section 360 of the Code is not to be made… it would be an illegality resulting in highly undesirable consequences.” (2) By virtue of s.8(1) of the General Clauses Act (and s.19 of the Probation Act), where the Probation Act has been brought into force in an area, ss.360 and 361 of the Code are wholly inapplicable in that area. (3) The four differences that make the two statutes irreconcilable: scope (s.4 applies to any person, s.360 only to specified classes); offences (s.360 is limited to offences punishable with fine or up to seven years for persons over 21); machinery (s.360 provides no role for probation officers); and consequences (s.12 removes disqualification, and the Code has no parallel provision). (4) The Probation Act has “a PARAMOUNTCY of [its] own” in the areas where it applies — a conclusion reinforced by s.360(10). (5) s.361 obliges the court to apply ONE OR THE OTHER beneficial provision, and “it is only by providing SPECIAL REASONS that their applicability can be withheld”. (6) Although a High Court has no power to modify its own judgment, the Supreme Court may, in the interests of justice, direct it to consider an application under the Act where the plea was not urged through no fault of the accused.
The DU solved paper’s summary
VERBATIM — DU solved paper, 2022 PYQ “In this case, the Court addressed the overlap between Section 360, CrPC and the provisions of the PO Act. Applying the doctrine of HARMONIOUS CONSTRUCTION, Justice Arijit Pasayat observed [that s.360 is narrower and s.4 wider, and that] ‘enforcement of the PO Act in some particular area excludes the applicability of the provisions of Section 360 and Section 361 of CrPC in that area, in view of Section 360(10) of CrPC which makes it clear that nothing in the said section shall affect the provisions of the PO Act. These provisions have a paramountcy of their own in the respective area where they are applicable.’”

48.3 Real-world examples to deploy

The executions of the modern era
Dhananjoy Chatterjee (14 August 2004), Ajmal Kasab (21 November 2012), Afzal Guru (9 February 2013), Yakub Memon (30 July 2015) and the four Nirbhaya convicts (20 March 2020). Five executions in twenty years, against a death-row population now exceeding 560 — the clearest available proof of the gap between what trial courts award and what the system carries out.
The Shraddananda sentence in practice
Applied in hundreds of cases since 2008 — for example, sentences of 25 years without remission, 30 years without remission, and imprisonment till natural death — including in the Nithari, Sheena Bora-type and multiple sexual-offence cases. It is now the standard commutation order of the Supreme Court and the principal reason that the death-penalty debate in India has shifted.
Probation in practice
India has an acute shortage of probation officers; several States have not appointed them at all in many districts; caseloads run into hundreds; and pre-sentence reports are frequently perfunctory or not called for. M.C.D. v. State of Delhi is therefore honoured in the breach. The number of offenders released on probation each year is a tiny fraction of convictions — precisely Lewis Diana’s point, transposed to India.
The overcrowded prison
Against a sanctioned capacity of about 4.03 lakh, Indian prisons hold about 5.7 lakh inmates at an occupancy rate exceeding 130%, of whom roughly three-quarters are undertrials. That single statistic is the strongest practical argument for probation, community service under s.4(f) BNS, and s.479 BNSS. Use it in any question on alternatives to imprisonment.

49.1 DU 2022 · Q.3 — the Ravi problem

2022 · Q.3
Ravi, who has committed an offence under Section 394 (voluntarily causing hurt in robbery) of IPC — the punishment for which is imprisonment [for] life or rigorous imprisonment for a term which may extend to 10 years and fine — pleads for the grant of probation on the basis that the offence is punishable with 10 years rigorous imprisonment AS AN ALTERNATIVE to life imprisonment. Which sections, schools of criminology and theories of punishment can the judge rely on for justifying release of Ravi on probation?
Model answer

1. Identification of the issues. The DU answer states them: “The question deals with the following issues — (I) Theories of punishment; (II) Probation as a form of punishment.” PYQ Three questions arise: (i) Is s.4 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 available at all for an offence under s.394 IPC? (ii) If not, is there any other route to leniency? (iii) Which theories and schools support that course?

2. The object of punishment — the opening paragraph. “The object of punishment is mainly prevention of crime. Therefore, punishment is given to the offenders to deter them from committing any further wrong against the society as well as [to] deter others from doing the same acts in future.” According to Halsbury’s Laws of England, the object of punishment is two-fold: “(i) deterrence of the actual offender from repeating the crime, and any other person from committing the same act in future; and (ii) protection of the society.” Thus, when a criminal is punished by the State, it is presupposed that “(1) a grave ill has been inflicted on the society; (2) it is imposed by someone’s voluntary act; and (3) it is inflicted by the criminal or someone who is answerable for his wrong-doings.” PYQ

3. The four theories (set out in full at §3–§6 and §9; the DU answer’s own summaries follow). (a) Retributive — “based on the doctrine of lex talionis, i.e. ‘an eye for an eye’… first propounded in the Code of Hammurabi… the main proponents were Sir James Stephen, Oppenheimer and Immanuel Kant. According to Oppenheimer, ‘retaliation or retribution fulfil[s] a religious mission of punishing the offender; it re-establishes the social harmony affected by the offence and the offender’s guilt is washed away through suffering.’ However, this theory has been criticised by the utilitarians on the ground that it has no social utility… ‘if an individual does not have the right to exact retribution, how can the state acquire such a right?’” (b) Deterrent — “the object… is to show the futility of the crime and teach the wrongdoer that crime is never profitable… Before the 18th century, approximately 200 offences were punished by death in England… ‘You are to be hanged not because you have stolen [a] sheep but in order that others may not steal sheep.’” (c) Reformative — “condemns all kinds of corporal punishment and focuses on rehabilitation… punishment is justified only if it looks to the future and not to the past… it aims to bring about a change in the criminal’s outlook through effective measures of reformation.” (d) Preventive — “one must not avenge crime but prevent it… by disabling or incapacitating the criminal… criticised on the ground that it completely ignores the impact of the reformative theory.” PYQ

4. Probation — the concept. “The word ‘probation’ is derived from the Latin ‘probare’ which means ‘to test’ or ‘to prove’. It is an alternative method of correction/punishment which is essentially based on the reformative approach… offenders must be provided with a chance to redeem themselves instead of sentencing them to imprisonment which may otherwise harden the minds of even the petty offenders or occasional criminals.” PYQ Add the UN definition from the 2017 paper (§38.1) and Lewis Diana’s composite definition (§37). Relevant provisions: ss.360 and 361 CrPC (ss.401–402 BNSS) and ss.3, 4, 5, 6, 11, 12 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 (§38).

5. THE DECISIVE POINT — s.394 IPC is outside the Act. Section 4 applies only where the offence is “not punishable with death or imprisonment for life”, and s.6 only where the offence is punishable “with imprisonment but not with imprisonment for life”. Section 394 IPC is punishable with imprisonment for life. Jogi Nayak v. State (1965) decides the very point: “the obvious object of Section 4(1) and Section 6(1) of the Act is that persons found guilty of SERIOUS OFFENCES will not be given the benefits under the Act. The offence under Section 394, IPC, is punishable with imprisonment for life or rigorous imprisonment for 10 years and fine.” Ravi’s argument that the punishment is “alternative” was considered and rejected in the two decisions Jogi Nayak relies on: Emperor v. Bakhsha (1934) — Shadi Lal J. held that “the punishment provided under Section 394, IPC is NOT of an alternative nature and the offender could be awarded… transportation for life”; and State v. Shes Shankar (1956) — the Allahabad High Court held the same of s.409 IPC, whose punishment clause is identically worded. Therefore s.4 is not available to Ravi. The DU answer’s conclusion is in terms: “In view of Section 4 of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958, which is applicable in cases where the offence alleged to have been committed is NOT punishable with death or imprisonment for life, RAVI’S APPLICATION MAY NOT BE ALLOWED. Refer to Jogi Nayak v. State (1965) [and] Phul Singh case.” PYQ

6. The cases the judge would consider if the discretion were available. Abdul Qayum (1972) DU — the Act is “a milestone in the progress of the modern liberal trend of reform in the field of penology”, and “to sentence him to imprisonment would itself achieve the object of associating him with hardened criminals”. Dalbir Singh (2000) DU — the court must assess “the essence of the offence or the character of the crime”, and deterrence may exclude the benefit. Phul Singh v. State of Haryana (1979) — s.4 “should not be liberally applied in undeserving cases”. M.C.D. v. State of Delhi (2005) DU — s.4 applies to all ages, the Probation Officer’s report is mandatory, and a previous convict cannot be released. Chhanni (2006) DU — where the Act is in force, s.360 CrPC is wholly inapplicable, so Ravi cannot fall back on the Code either.

7. What the judge CAN do — the constructive part of the answer. Although probation is barred, the court is not without options. (a) The quantum of sentence. The section prescribes life or rigorous imprisonment up to ten years; the court may impose a short term. Vajja Srinivasu v. State of A.P. (2002) DU in Kaul is directly in point: “the disability that the provisions of the Probation of Offenders Act… are inapplicable… CANNOT PRECLUDE THE ACCUSED FROM PLEADING THAT A LESSER SENTENCE BE AWARDED, particularly in view of the AGE FACTOR of the accused” — and the Court there reduced five years to one. (b) Jogi Nayak itself did exactly this: it refused probation but “directed his release while upholding his conviction”, because “a longer stay in the prison in [the] company of criminals would only turn him into a hardened criminal”. (c) s.361 CrPC / s.402 BNSS requires the court to record special reasons for not applying the beneficial provisions — and Bishnu Deo Shaw DU holds that those reasons “must be such as to COMPEL the Court to hold that it is IMPOSSIBLE TO REFORM AND REHABILITATE the offender”. (d) s.395 BNSS compensation to the victim; and, if Ravi is under 18, the JJ Act, 2015 displaces the criminal court altogether (Topic 4); if he is under 21, s.6 would have applied but for the life- imprisonment exclusion.

8. The schools of criminology. The DU answer sets out the classification — Classical, Positive and Others, with the specific theories of causation grouped as biological/biophysical, psychological, sociological, economic and political PYQ. Apply them: the classical school (Beccaria, Bentham) would ask whether the certain prospect of a short determinate sentence is sufficient to make the offence unprofitable; the positive school (Lombroso, Ferri, Garofalo) would direct attention to Ravi’s individual constitution and circumstances and to individualised treatment; the sociological school (Sutherland’s differential association) supplies the Abdul Qayum argument that prison will associate him with hardened criminals; and the economic theories (Bonger) would ask what drove the robbery.

9. Conclusion. In the DU answer’s own words: “However, the Court in its discretion may examine the facts of the case, and if he finds that the character of the crime and [the] conduct of the offender as described in the report of the Probation Officer under Section 4(2) of the Act [warrant it], he may allow Ravi’s release on probation under Section 4, keeping in mind the object of the Act which is to reform and rehabilitate the offender. THE JUDGE MAY RELY ON THE REFORMATIVE THEORY OF PUNISHMENT to justify Ravi’s release on probation, as well as the decision in Abdul Qayum v. State of Bihar (1972).” PYQ State the law correctly first — s.4 is barred by Jogi Nayak — and then present the reformative route through quantum of sentence, Vajja Srinivasu and s.361/s.402. That is the complete answer.

49.2 DU 2017 · Q.6(a) — the reformative philosophy

2017 · Q.6(a)
“In the era of human rights jurisprudence, the philosophy of deterrent punishment has given way to that of Reformative philosophy for the rehabilitation and reassimilation of the offender in the society.” Discuss and state whether it serves the Indian conditions.
Answer plan, with the DU script’s own content
(1) The shift. “With the passage of time, developments in the field of criminal science brought about a radical change in criminological thinking. There was a fresh approach to the problem of crime and criminals. INDIVIDUALIZED TREATMENT became the cardinal principle for reformation of [the] offender.” PYQ (2) The philosophy. “The view of penology suggests that punishment is only justifiable if it looks to the future and not to the past. ‘It should not be regarded as settling an old account but rather as opening a new one.’… As against deterrent, retributive and preventive justice, the reformative approach seeks to bring about a change in the attitude of [the] offender so as to rehabilitate him as a law-abiding member of society. Thus, punishment is used as a measure to reclaim the offender and not to torture or harass him. IT CONDEMNS ALL KINDS OF CORPORAL PUNISHMENT. It advocates humane treatment of inmates inside the penal institutions… prisoners should be properly trained to adjust themselves to free life in society after their release.” PYQ (3) The techniques. “The reformative techniques of punishment are essentially devised [for] the treatment of offenders according to their psychological traits — such as probation, parole, pardon, admonition, [and the] indeterminate sentence.” PYQ (4) The Indian authorities. Narotam Singh v. State of Punjab: “Reformative approach to punishment should be the object of criminal law, in order to promote rehabilitation without offending community conscience, and to secure social justice.” Musa Khan v. State of Maharashtra: the Act “is a piece of social legislation which is meant to reform juvenile offenders with a view to prevent them from becoming hardened criminals.” PYQ Add Mohammad Giasuddin (1977), Abdul Qayum (1972) DU, Sanjay Suri (1988) DU and Sunil Batra (II) (1980). (5) The statutory expression — §5 of these notes: the Probation Act, ss.401–402 BNSS, s.4(f) BNS community service, the JJ Act, s.479 BNSS, the Model Prisons Act 2023. And, from the DU reading, Bishnu Deo Shaw: “reformation and rehabilitation of offenders, and NOT MERE DETERRENCE, are now among the FOREMOST OBJECTS of the administration of criminal justice in our country.” (6) “Does it serve Indian conditions?” — the critical half of the question. The DU script’s own qualification: “Modern [pen]ologists reaffirm their faith in reformative justice but they strongly feel that IT SHOULD NOT BE STRETCHED SO FAR. This has proved useful in [the] case of JUVENILE DELINQUENTS AND THE FIRST OFFENDERS. In terms of the theory, offenders largely commit crime because of PSYCHOLOGICAL FACTORS, PERSONALITY DEFECTS OR SOCIAL PRESSURES.” PYQ Then give the objections: Ashworth’s two (no limit on intervention; the offender treated as less than fully responsible); Martinson’s “nothing works” and its retraction; Lewis Diana’s finding that probation in practice is “little more than administrative supervision” DU; the shortage of probation officers and correctional infrastructure in India; prison overcrowding at over 130%; and Dalbir Singh DU and State v. Bhola, which show the courts refusing reformative treatment where deterrence is required. (7) Conclusion. The correct answer is Hart’s mixed theory: deterrence and protection supply the general justifying aim, desert supplies the limits, and reformation governs the choice of method within those limits — which is precisely the scheme of ss.354(3) and 361 CrPC read together.

(5) The present position. “The present position regarding capital punishment in India is quite [a] balanced one. 49.4 DU 2017 · Q.7(b) — the counterfeit-medicines problem

2017 · Q.7(b)
X, aged 18 years, is convicted for the offence of selling counterfeit medicines over the counter. Can he seek release under the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 or under the Criminal Procedure Code?
Model answer

1. The definition of probation. Open with the DU script’s own: “The release of offenders on probation is A TREATMENT DEVICE prescribed by the court for persons convicted of offences against the law, during which the probationer lives in the community and regulates his own life under conditions imposed by the court or other constituted authority, and is subject to supervision by [a] probation officer.” PYQ

2. The statutory route. X is 18, so s.6 of the Probation Act applies on its face (he is “under twenty-one years of age”), and s.4 applies if the offence is not punishable with death or imprisonment for life. s.360 CrPC / s.401 BNSS is not available separately: by Chhanni (2006) DU, where the Probation Act is in force in an area, “the provisions of Section 360 of the Code are WHOLLY INAPPLICABLE”. So the question resolves into whether s.4/s.6 should be applied.

3. The offence. Selling counterfeit or spurious medicines attracts the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 — s.17-B (spurious drugs), s.18(a)(i) and s.27(a)–(d), under which spurious drugs likely to cause death or grievous hurt carry ten years to imprisonment for life (and, where death results, the same) — and ss.274–276 BNS (adulteration of drugs, sale of adulterated drugs, sale of a drug as a different drug). If the offence charged is one punishable with imprisonment for life, s.4 and s.6 are excluded altogether on the reasoning of Jogi Nayak (§42).

4. Even if the Act applies, the discretion must be refused. This is the ground the DU script takes, and it is the heart of the answer: “Thus, relying on the above precedents, X aged 18 years, who is convicted for selling counterfeit medicines over the counter, CANNOT seek release under the POA, 1958 or under CrPC. THE BENEFIT MUST NOT BE GIVEN IN VIEW OF THE NATURE OF THE OFFENCE AND [THE] DEPRAVITY OF [THE] OFFENDER. AS THE OFFENCE IS SOCIO-ECONOMIC IN NATURE, THE BENEFIT MUST NOT BE GIVEN.” PYQ Support it with: (a) the word “expedient” in s.4(1) requires regard to “the circumstances of the case including the nature of the offence” — Dalbir Singh (2000) DU, which held for that very reason that s.304-A road deaths do not attract s.4; (b) State v. Bhola (2003) — probation is not to be granted as a matter of course; (c) Ishar Das v. State of Punjab (1973) — the courts “should not lightly give the benefit of the provisions of the Probation of Offenders Act” in food-adulteration cases, because the statute “aims at uprooting and curbing such evils and [at] safeguarding public health”, and because the 47th Law Commission Report recommended the exclusion of the Probation Act from socio-economic offences (Topic 3, §21.2 and §42); and (d) Phul Singh (1979) — s.4 “should not be liberally applied in undeserving cases”.

5. The criminological frame — this is what lifts the answer. Selling counterfeit medicines is a socio-economic offence in the sense of the 47th Law Commission Report (Topic 3, §18): the motive is avarice, not lust or hate; the background is non-emotional; the victim is the consuming public — “the criminal act is potentially capable of harming a large number of persons”; the mode is fraud, not force; the act is deliberate and wilful; and the interest protected is the health of the community. As the Report puts it, “the wide distribution of goods has become an instrument of wide distribution of HARM”, and such offences “require to be put down with a heavy hand”. And it is a white-collar crime in Sutherland’s sense — “a crime committed in the course of one’s occupation by a member of the upper class of society”.

6. The theory. For this class of offender the deterrent theory is the operative one, because the offence is “committed with cool calculation and deliberate design with an eye on personal profit regardless of the consequence to the community” (State of Gujarat v. Mohanlal Jitamalji Porwal (1987) 2 SCC 364). The rational calculator is precisely the offender whom deterrence can reach.

7. But note the two qualifications. (a) X is eighteen, and s.6 is an injunction: if the Act applies at all, the court shall not sentence him to imprisonment unless it is satisfied that it is not desirable to deal with him under s.3 or s.4, and it must record reasons and must call for the Probation Officer’s report under s.6(2) — Abdul Qayum DU and M.C.D. v. State of Delhi DU. (b) Even on refusal, the court must record special reasons under s.361 CrPC / s.402 BNSS, and those reasons must “compel the Court to hold that it is impossible to reform and rehabilitate the offender” (Bishnu Deo Shaw) DU. Conclusion: X cannot be released on probation; but the court must nevertheless comply with s.6(2) and s.402 before sentencing him, and may mitigate the quantum on the ground of his age (Vajja Srinivasu).
