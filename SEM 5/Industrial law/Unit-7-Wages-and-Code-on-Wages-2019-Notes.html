0.1 The two most useful “insider” points in this unit

① “Chapter 2, 3 & 7” means the chapters of the Code, not of a book

The syllabus line “The Labour Wage Code, 2019 : …” is followed by the reading reference “Chapter 2, 3 & 7”. Read against the arrangement of sections of the Code, those are Chapter II — Minimum Wages (§§5–14), Chapter III — Payment of Wages (§§15–25) and Chapter VII — Inspector-cum-Facilitator (§51). Every item in the syllabus list maps onto one of those three chapters, with the definition of “wages” sitting in Chapter I, §2(y).

② Say the statute’s status correctly — it changed on 21 November 2025

For five years the standard caveat was “the Code is enacted but not in force”. That is no longer true. All four labour codes — the Code on Wages, 2019, the Industrial Relations Code, 2020, the Code on Social Security, 2020 and the OSH Code, 2020 — were brought into force with effect from 21 November 2025, repealing 29 central labour laws; the Code on Wages (Central) Rules, 2026 followed on 8 May 2026. The Minimum Wages Act, 1948, the Payment of Wages Act, 1936, the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965 and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 are therefore repealed law, referred to in the Code’s own §69 (repeal and savings). Cite the Code as the governing statute and the old Acts as its source and background.

Caution box — three traps that go with Point ②
  1. The floor wage is in the statute but not yet notified. As on 28 September 2026 no statutory floor wage has been fixed under §9. What circulates as the “national floor wage” is the older, advisory National Floor Level Minimum Wage (₹176 a day with effect from 2017; ₹178 a day is also commonly reported after 2019), which is not binding on the States. Do not merge the two figures.
  2. The old Acts still govern the facts of your cases. Crown Aluminium (1958), Greaves Cotton (1964) and PUDR (1982) were decided under the Minimum Wages Act, 1948 and the Payment of Wages Act, 1936. Those Acts have now been repealed, but the adjudicative principles they generated (capacity to pay, industry-cum-region, no downward revision at the floor) survive as the working law of §§5–9 of the Code.
  3. The Central Rules do not yet prescribe the calculation norms. The Code leaves the “manner of calculating” and the “norms of fixation” to be prescribed (§6(5), §6(6)(c), §9(1)); the draft Rules circulated in 2025–26 had proposed three consumption units per household, 2,700 calories per consumption unit, 10% for rent, 20% for fuel/lighting/miscellaneous and 25% for education, medical requirements, recreation and contingencies — the 1957 tripartite norms plus the sixth component of Reptakos Brett — but the norms were still to be specified by order. Frame this as “expected / proposed”, not as notified law.

The unit in one sentence

Wages in Indian industrial law run on a three-tier ladder — the minimum wage, which is an absolute and non-reducible floor enforceable as a fundamental right; the fair wage, which is fixed by comparing the industry and the region and can move up or down with the employer’s capacity to pay; and the living wage, which is the constitutional destination of Article 43 — and the Code on Wages, 2019 has now converted that ladder into a statute with a universal minimum wage, a central floor wage, a codified mode and time of payment, and an Inspector-cum-Facilitator instead of an inspector-raj.

AConcept and Kinds of Wages — Minimum, Fair and Living Wage

A.1 What is a “wage”? — the common-law starting point and its abandonment

A wage is, in economic terms, the price paid for labour; in legal terms, it is the remuneration payable by an employer to an employee under the terms of employment, express or implied, for work done. In the classic common law of master and servant a wage was understood as nothing more than the object of an individual bargain — a price at which one person agreed to sell and another to buy so many hours of work. The whole of Indian wages law is the story of the law refusing to leave the wage at that.

Gajendragadkar J. put the transition in terms that examiners love to see quoted, in Crown Aluminium Works v. Workmen (Case 24):

“It is well known”, observes Sir Frank Tillyard, “that English common law still regards the wage bargain as a contract between an individual employer and an individual worker, and that the general policy of the law has been and is to leave to the two contracting parties a general liberty of bargaining, so long as there are no terms against public policy. In India as well as in England and other democratic welfare States great inroad has been made on this view of the common law by labour welfare legislation such as the Minimum Wages Act and the Industrial Disputes Act. With the emergence of the concept of a welfare state, collective bargaining between trade unions and capital has come into its own and has received statutory recognition; the state is no longer content to play the part of a passive onlooker in an industrial dispute. The old principle of the absolute freedom of contract and the doctrine of laissez faire have yielded place to new principles of social welfare and common good.” — Crown Aluminium Works v. Workmen, AIR 1958 SC 130 (Case 24)

Two further passages from the same judgment supply the unit’s vocabulary. First, the function a wage structure is expected to perform — “a bulwark against the dangers of a depression, safeguard against unfair methods of competition between employers and a guaranty of wages necessary for the minimum requirements of employees”. Secondly, the objective of industrial adjudication in a wage dispute:

“Though social and economic justice is the ultimate ideal of industrial adjudication, its immediate objective in an industrial dispute as to the wage structure is to settle the dispute by constituting such a wage structure as would do justice to the interests of both labour and capital, would establish harmony between them and lead to their genuine and wholehearted cooperation in the task of production. … In achieving this immediate objective, industrial adjudication takes into account several principles such as, for instance, the principle of comparable wages, productivity of the trade or industry, cost of living and ability of the industry to pay. The application of these and other relevant principles leads to the constitution of different categories of wage structures. These categories are sometimes described as living wage, fair wage and minimum wage.” — Crown Aluminium Works v. Workmen (Case 24)
Why the law intervenes at all — four reasons that can be listed in any answer
  1. Unequal bargaining power. The individual worker, in Bhagwati J.’s phrase in PUDR (Case 27), is often faced with a “Hobson’s choice” — “either to starve or to submit to the exploitative terms dictated by the powerful employer”. A wage left to contract is therefore not a freely negotiated price at all.
  2. Exploitation and the floor. Because the market can always produce a wage that keeps a worker merely alive, the legislature fixes a statutory floor below which no employer may go, whatever the contract says.
  3. Industrial peace. Wage disputes are the single largest source of industrial disputes; adjudicated wage structures are designed to “restore peace and goodwill in the industry”.
  4. The welfare-State and constitutional mandate. Articles 38, 39(a), 41, 42 and 43 of the Constitution direct the State towards a living wage, adequate means of livelihood and just conditions of work; Article 23 prohibits forced labour; Article 21 protects dignity. Wages law is where these directives become money.

A.2A The international human rights anchor — Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR, 1948)

Wages in modern industrial jurisprudence are not treated as mere contractual price-tags dictated by unbridled market forces; they are recognized as substantive human rights rooted in human dignity. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on 10 December 1948 — the exact same year India enacted the historic Minimum Wages Act, 1948 — furnished the international philosophical foundation that directly shaped Part III (Fundamental Rights) and Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy) of the Indian Constitution, as well as the Supreme Court’s wage jurisprudence.

A.3 The three-tier classification — where it comes from

The classification of wage structures into minimum, fair and living wages was the work of the Committee on Fair Wages (1948), appointed following the recommendation of the Royal Commission on Labour (1931). The Committee’s Report, published by the Ministry of Labour in 1949, was “broadly approved” by the Supreme Court in Express Newspapers (P) Ltd. v. Union of India, AIR 1958 SC 578 and Standard Vacuum Refining Co. of India v. Its Workmen, AIR 1961 SC 895 — a fact recorded by the Court in Reptakos Brett (Case 26). The Court itself, in Crown Aluminium, added the important qualification that the three categories “cannot and do not mean the same thing in all countries nor even in different industries in the same country”; their content expands with the economy.

The Committee’s own definitions (verbatim, as quoted by the Supreme Court)

Living wage — Committee on Fair Wages (1949)

“(T)he living wage should enable the male earner to provide for himself and his family not merely the bare essentials of food, clothing and shelter but a measure of frugal comfort including education for the children, protection against ill-health, requirements of essential social needs, and a measure of insurance against the more important misfortunes including old age.”

Minimum wage — Committee on Fair Wages (1949)

“(T)he minimum wage must provide not merely for the bare sustenance of life but for the preservation of the efficiency of the worker. For this purpose the minimum wage must also provide for some measure of education, medical requirements and amenities.”

Note the sting inside the minimum-wage definition

The Committee’s minimum wage is not a starvation wage. It is a need-based wage that already contains three social items — education, medical requirements and amenities — because a worker who is under-fed, unwell or illiterate is not an “efficient” worker. This is why the Supreme Court could later say that the whole idea of “minimum” is dynamic: what was fair wage forty years ago may be today’s minimum (Crown Aluminium). And it is why Reptakos Brett could add a sixth, expressly social, component without doing violence to the concept.

A.4 Minimum wage — the absolute floor

(a) The core propositions

  • The minimum wage is the statutory floor below which no employer may pay, whatever the contract or the employer’s circumstances. Under the Code on Wages, 2019 it is defined simply as “the wage fixed under section 6” (§2(s)).
  • Capacity to pay is irrelevant. This is the sharpest rule in the unit. In Crown Aluminium the Court held: “There is … one principle which admits of no exceptions. No industry has a right to exist unless it is able to pay its workmen at least a bare minimum wage. … If an employer cannot maintain his enterprise without cutting down the wages of his employees below even a bare subsistence or minimum wage, he would have no right to conduct his enterprise on such terms.” In Reptakos Brett: “An employer who cannot pay the minimum wage has no right to engage labour and no justification to run the industry.”
  • It is payable at all times and under all circumstances. In Unichoyi v. State of Kerala the Court rejected the plea that minimum wage fixation could be struck down because the employer could not bear the burden: the fixation of minimum wages is in the nature of a legislative determination for which capacity to pay is not a consideration.
  • Below it, the labour becomes “forced labour”. PUDR (Case 27) — a fundamental-rights consequence that no other tier carries.

(c) The 1957 five norms — the “need-based minimum wage”

The 15th Session of the Indian Labour Conference (1957), a tripartite body of employers, workers and Government, laid down five norms for computing a need-based minimum wage. They were quoted with approval in Standard Vacuum Refining Co. (1961) and reproduced by the Supreme Court again in Reptakos Brett (Case 26), from which the following is verbatim:

The five norms (Indian Labour Conference, 1957)

“(i) In calculating the minimum wage, the standard working class family should be taken to consist of 3 consumption units for one earner; the earnings of women, children and adolescents should be disregarded.
(ii) Minimum food requirement should be calculated on the basis of a net intake of calories, as recommended by Dr Aykroyd for an average Indian adult of moderate activity.
(iii) Clothing requirements should be estimated at per capita consumption of 18 yards per annum which would give the average workers’ family of four, a total of 72 yards.
(iv) In respect of housing, the rent corresponding to the minimum area provided for under Government’s Industrial Housing Scheme, should be taken into consideration in fixing the minimum wage.
(v) Fuel, lighting and other ‘miscellaneous’ items of expenditure should constitute 20 per cent of the total minimum wage.”

(d) The sixth component — Reptakos Brett (1992)

The sixth component (verbatim)

“(vi) children’s education, medical requirement, minimum recreation including festivals/ceremonies and provision for old age, marriages, etc. should further constitute 25 per cent of the total minimum wage.”

— added as a guide for fixing the minimum wage in the industry, in Workmen v. The Management of Reptakos Brett & Co. Ltd., (1992) 1 SCC 290 (Case 26), because “the concept of ‘minimum wage’ is no longer the same as it was in 1936. Even 1957 is way behind.”

(e) How the norms are to be worked — an illustrative computation

Illustration (method only — not a notified rate)

Suppose a State is working out a need-based minimum wage for an unskilled worker in an urban area. Using the six components it would proceed in columns: (1) family = 3 consumption units for one earner; (2) food = cost of the prescribed calorie intake per consumption unit per day × 3 units × 30 days; (3) clothing = 18 yards per capita per annum for four persons, converted into a monthly money value; (4) housing = the rent of the minimum area under the Industrial Housing Scheme; (5) fuel, lighting and miscellaneous = 20% added to the total of the preceding items; and (6) social and dignitary needs — children’s education, medical requirement, minimum recreation, festivals and ceremonies, old-age and marriage provision — = 25% of the total minimum wage arrived at. In practice the appropriate Government then assigns the computed figure to skill categories (unskilled, semi-skilled, skilled, highly skilled), to zones and to industries, and indexes the cost of living element so that it is revised periodically (in several States, twice a year, on the movement of the CPI-IW).

How this connects to the Code and to the current position (2026)

The Code on Wages gives the norms a statutory home: §6(5) leaves the manner of calculating to be prescribed, and §6(6)(c) leaves the norms of fixation to be prescribed, with §6(6)(a) requiring the appropriate Government primarily to take into account the skill of workers (unskilled, skilled, semi-skilled and highly-skilled) and/or the geographical area, and §6(6)(b) permitting it to take account of arduousness or hazardousness of the work. The draft Rules circulated in December 2025 proposed the criteria — 3 consumption units, 2,700 calories per consumption unit, 10% for rent, 20% for fuel, electricity and miscellaneous, and 25% for education, medical requirements, recreation and contingencies — which is precisely the 1957 norm set plus the Reptakos sixth component; but the final position is that these criteria are to be specified by a special or general order of the Central Government. So a strong answer says: the Code codifies the concept of a prescribed need-based minimum wage, while the numbers are still being worked out.

A.5 Fair wage — the middle tier

The fair wage is the wage a worker ought to receive in the particular industry, given the industry’s capacity. It is neither the floor (which must be paid regardless of capacity) nor the aspirational living wage. Its range is bounded from below by the minimum wage and from above by the capacity of the industry to pay. The Supreme Court in Crown Aluminium described the principles that operate in fixing it — “the principle of comparable wages, productivity of the trade or industry, cost of living and ability of the industry to pay” — and in Reptakos Brett summarised the tier as “a little above” the minimum wage.

Fixed by whom?

Industrial tribunals in adjudication; wage boards; collective bargaining/settlements; and, for many industries historically, statutory wage boards (for example, the Central Wage Boards for the textile, sugar and jute industries, and the Wage Boards for working journalists).

By what method?

The industry-cum-region formula — comparison with comparable concerns in the same industry and in the same region, weighing the total pay packet and the employer’s financial capacity. Fully worked out in Greaves Cotton (Case 25), Part C of these notes.

Can it fall?

Yes — “if a fair wage structure is constructed by industrial adjudication, and in course of time, experience shows that the employer cannot bear the burden of such wage structure, industrial adjudication can, and in a proper case should, revise the wage structure, though such revision may result in the reduction of the wages” (Ahmedabad Mills Owners’ Association, approved in Reptakos). But never below the minimum.

Two further propositions complete the tier. First, fair wage is comparative: the Tribunal is not inventing a number but locating the establishment on a map of comparable establishments. Secondly, fair wage is dynamic in both directions: upward on a rise in the cost of living or where the employer’s capacity has improved; downward where genuine incapacity is proved on the merits, but “sparingly” and after considering alternatives such as retrenchment already effected (Crown Aluminium).

A.6 Living wage — the constitutional ceiling

The living wage is the standard described in the Fair Wages Committee’s definition reproduced in §A.3: not merely food, clothing and shelter, but “a measure of frugal comfort” — children’s education, protection against ill-health, essential social needs, and “a measure of insurance against the more important misfortunes including old age”. It is the wage Article 43 directs the State to endeavour to secure. The Supreme Court’s treatment of it in Reptakos Brett is unusually candid and makes an excellent closing paragraph:

“A living wage has been promised to the workers under the Constitution. A ‘socialist’ framework to enable the working people a decent standard of life, has further been promised by the 42nd Amendment. The workers are hopefully looking forward to achieve the said ideal. The promises are piling up but the day of fulfilment is nowhere in sight. Industrial wage — looked at as a whole — has not yet risen higher than the level of minimum wage.” — Workmen v. Management of Reptakos Brett & Co. Ltd. (1992) 1 SCC 290 (Case 26)

The Court added the observation that makes the three tiers a hierarchy rather than a division: “In any case there is a very long way between the need based wage and the living wage.” In other words, in India today the practical legal work is done by the minimum wage and the fair wage; the living wage functions as a direction of travel — but it is not a dead letter, because it is the standard against which the adequacy of every minimum wage is eventually judged, and because a wage below the statutory minimum produces, as PUDR holds, a breach of Article 23 rather than merely a statutory default.

A.8 Dearness allowance — the inflation-proofing layer

Why DA exists. Wages are fixed in money but consumed at prices. The dearness allowance (also called the cost of living allowance or special allowance) is the component that protects the real wage against the erosion of the nominal wage by a rise in the cost of living. It was introduced in Indian industry during the Second World War and is now a statutory element of the minimum wage itself: §7(1)(a) of the Code on Wages requires the minimum rate to consist of “a basic rate of wages and an allowance at a rate to be adjusted, at such intervals and in such manner as the appropriate Government may direct, to accord as nearly as practicable with the variation in the cost of living index number applicable to such workers”. And under §2(y)(ii), dearness allowance is one of the three included components of “wages” — with basic pay and retaining allowance.

Fixed DA and variable DA

Fixed DA is a flat sum (₹X per month) that does not move with prices: simple to administer, predictable for budgeting, but it silently loses value in inflationary periods. Variable DA (VDA) is linked to a price index — usually the CPI-IW — and is revised periodically (often half-yearly, on the basis of the index notified by the Labour Bureau); it protects real wages automatically but makes the wage bill unpredictable. Most adjudicated and statutory structures use both: a fixed slab plus a variable slab that is neutralised to a stated percentage.

BCase 24 — Crown Aluminium Works v. Workmen, AIR 1958 SC 130

M/s Crown Aluminium Works, Belur (represented by Jeewanlal (1929) Ltd.) v. Their Workmen

1958 SCR 651 : AIR 1958 SC 130 · Before P.B. Gajendragadkar, J. · Appeal by special leave under Article 136 against the award of the Labour Appellate Tribunal · LB-503 case material, pp. 167–179
Three-tier wage structure Downward revision of wages Concessional payments / bounty vs wages Capacity to pay Section 10, IDA
Context — the problem the case solves

The case is the Supreme Court’s first sustained analysis of the three categories of wage structures and of the question that has haunted Indian wage adjudication ever since: may a wage structure once fixed be cut back? Its answer — an absolute “no” at the level of the minimum wage, and a qualified “yes” above it — is the doctrinal foundation for everything that follows in this unit, including the legislator’s floor wage and the constitutional holding in PUDR.

Facts — a wages dispute inside a collapsing market
  1. The parties and the reference. The appellant, M/s Crown Aluminium Works of Belur, represented by Jeewanlal (1929) Ltd., and its workmen represented by the Bengal Aluminium Workers’ Union. By order dated 31 July 1952 the Government of West Bengal referred thirteen matters for adjudication to Shri S.K. Niyogi, constituting the Sixth Industrial Tribunal, under §10 of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947.
  2. The earlier wage history. In 1947 the First Omnibus Engineering Tribunal for the West Bengal engineering industry, to which the appellant was a party, awarded a comprehensive wage structure published on 30 June 1948, covering basic wages, dearness allowance and leave. A second Tribunal’s award, published on 21 September 1950, increased the dearness allowance on account of the rise in the cost of living index and revised leave rules in the light of the Factories Act, 1948. The appellant, having from time to time revised its “facility bonus” to keep pace with the cost of living, found itself with a wage packet that gave its workmen no cause for complaint; it was therefore not a party to the second arbitration.
  3. The Palit arbitration (1950). A minor dispute about an increment was referred to the arbitration of Shri G. Palit. The company resisted the increment by arguing that its workmen were, on the whole, “handsomely remunerated” when special allowance, bonus, dearness allowance and standard wages were added up. Shri Palit was unimpressed; he observed that the Managing Director “chose to hold the key in his own hands so that he can manipulate the quantum of benefit under this head and could adjust it to the output in the factory”, allowed one anna per day as increment, and — prophetically — advised the company that “it will be prudent for the company to have a hide bound wage structure instead of having so many flexible component parts of the wage which merely will create unrest”.
  4. The economy measures of 1952. On 28 March 1952 the company gave notice proposing (i) reduction of factory hours from 47 to 40, (ii) reduction of the facility bonus by 3 annas per day, and (iii) reduction of temporary dearness allowance for salaried workers by 10% — all attributed to a financial set-back and to take effect from 1 June 1952. A further notice of 30 May 1952 proposed additional cuts: a further 6 annas per day off the facility bonus, withdrawal of the two hours’ concession of special bonus, and discharge of the workmen of the rolling mills department.
  5. Resistance, conciliation and retrenchment. The Union agreed only to a reduction of working hours from 47 to 42½ per week. Joint consultations on 2 and 26 June 1952 failed. On 27 June 1952 the company informed the workers that the reductions would operate from 1 June 1952, that the two hours’ concession would be withdrawn from 1 July 1952 and that the rolling mills workers would be discharged from 1 August 1952. A joint conference before the Labour Commissioner on 4 July 1952 failed. The company then discharged the 52 rolling-mills workmen with fourteen days’ notice pay and retrenched a further 227 workmen of various categories with effect from 26 July 1952. Conciliation having failed, the dispute was referred to the Sixth Industrial Tribunal.
  6. The two awards. The Sixth Industrial Tribunal (Shri Niyogi) investigated the company’s finances, reconstituted the wage structure and dearness allowance in the light of the Omnibus Engineering Awards of 1948 and 1950, and held that the two hours’ concession, the facility bonus and the food concession were bounty gratuitously paid, liable to be withdrawn at pleasure; having properly revised the wage structure, the company was given liberty to abolish them. On 11 July 1955 the Labour Appellate Tribunal, disposing of cross-appeals by a consolidated order, disagreed: the so-called concessions had been enjoyed by the workmen for a long time as of right and as part of their basic wages and dearness allowance, and had become a term of the conditions of their service; moreover, “it has been the convention with Industrial Tribunals not to reduce the existing emoluments of the workmen to their prejudice”. It therefore modified the wage structure so that (1) the existing basic wage of a time-rated worker was the total of his basic wage and the two hours’ concession immediately before 1 June 1952; (2) his existing dearness allowance was the total of temporary DA, facility bonus and food concession available before 1 June 1952, even if a part had since been curtailed; and (3) the three concessions ceased to have any separate existence from that date. Both Tribunals agreed that existing basic wages and total emoluments were not to be reduced.
  7. The financial findings. The unit, originally started by Americans and taken over by the appellant on 9 August 1951, manufactured household utensils from aluminium circles. Circles had been imported until the war; a rolling mills department set up during the war from scrap produced inferior quality, and once better circles became available demand collapsed and the rolling mills were closed in February 1952. Both Tribunals examined the accounts from 1947 to September 1952 and found the economic position “none too bright”, with manufacturing cost in 1952 (as in some earlier years) exceeding the sale price. The original Tribunal saw no prospect of improvement; the Appellate Tribunal thought that, as a result of the substantial retrenchment, “financial position of the relevant unit of the aluminium industry appears to have improved”.
Issues
  1. Whether a wage structure fixed by industrial adjudication can ever be revised to the prejudice of the workmen — in particular where the employer’s financial position has deteriorated.
  2. Whether the Labour Appellate Tribunal was right in holding that there is a convention of industrial adjudication against reducing existing emoluments.
  3. Whether the two hours’ concession, the facility bonus and the food concession were bounty (revocable at will) or integral components of the wage structure.
  4. Incidentally, whether the creation of two wage scales — one for existing workmen and another for new entrants — was permissible.
Arguments
For the appellant (Mr Sen) — the management
  1. The Labour Appellate Tribunal erred in assuming that there is a convention in industrial adjudication never to reduce existing emoluments. Just as a rise in the cost of living justifies revision in favour of the workmen, a deterioration in the financial position of the employer must justify revision in favour of the employer.
  2. The company’s financial position had genuinely deteriorated — declining sales, manufacturing cost exceeding sale price, and a product (utensils) that loses lustre and glaze if stored, adding to the loss.
  3. The two hours’ concession, the facility bonus and the food concession were concessional payments, i.e. bounty; the workmen had no right to them, and the original Tribunal had so found, and had given liberty to abolish them once the wage structure was properly revised.
  4. The result of the Appellate Tribunal’s order is the creation of two wage structures — one for existing workmen and a lower one for new entrants — which is irrational and unjustifiable.
  5. Mr Sen expressly clarified that his main target was the validity of the assumption of an absolute convention, rather than the correctness of the actual modifications made.
For the respondents (the workmen)
  1. The so-called concessions had been paid consistently for years and had become part of the conditions of service; they were as of right, not bounty.
  2. Industrial Tribunals have consistently followed the practice of protecting existing emoluments; each of the Tribunals that dealt with this very dispute had done so.
  3. The company’s own past conduct — its pleadings before Shri Palit and its statement before the Tribunal that its pay structure consisted of five items — showed that it treated these allowances as part of wages.
  4. If there was financial difficulty, it had already been addressed by the retrenchment of 227 workmen plus the closure of the rolling mills; a further cut in wages was neither necessary nor just.
  5. The modification was in any event confined to existing workmen; the wage structure itself, being near the minimum/fair wage level, was not reducible at all.
Decision (Holding)
  • The appeal was dismissed. Both of the appellant’s contentions substantially failed, and the modifications made by the Labour Appellate Tribunal in favour of existing workmen were upheld.
  • On the general question, the Court answered in favour of the appellant in the abstract — there is no rigid or inexorable convention that a wage structure can never be revised to the prejudice of workmen — but subject to a decisive limitation at the level of the minimum wage.
  • On the facts, the Appellate Tribunal’s decision was not based solely or chiefly on the alleged convention: it also rested on the finding that the retrenchment had improved the company’s position, on the absence of any reference on the fall in the cost of living index, and on the finding that the wages were “the irreducible minimum or may at best be in the region of fair wages with a small margin over the minimum wage”.
  • On the character of the three payments, the Appellate Tribunal’s conclusion that they were “in essence and in substance” part of the basic wage and dearness allowance was upheld, and the wage structure as revised was left undisturbed.
  • The question of two wage scales (existing workmen versus new entrants) was expressly left undecided, since it had become unnecessary.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. Absolute floor rule. No wage structure can or should be revised to the prejudice of workmen if it falls in the category of the bare subsistence or minimum wage — “even theoretically”, and regardless of the employer’s financial position. This admits of no exception.
  2. Qualified flexibility above the floor. Where the wage structure is above the minimum (a fair wage or higher), the employer may seek revision to the prejudice of the workmen, provided a case is made out on the merits to the satisfaction of the Tribunal.
  3. The Tribunal’s checklist. In deciding such a claim the Tribunal may enquire — (a) whether the employer’s financial difficulties could not have been adequately met by the retrenchment already effected and sanctioned; (b) whether the difficulty is of short duration or likely to continue for a fairly long time; and (c) what the consequences will be for industrial peace, weighing the discontent caused by a cut against the unemployment that follows if an industry is burdened beyond its capacity. The satisfaction of the Tribunal is the condition precedent, and it may impose conditions as to time or otherwise.
  4. Substance over label. Payments made over a long period, which the employer itself has treated as part of the wage structure in its pleadings and evidence, are wages and not bounty, whatever they are called.
Principles and observations (obiter and contextual)
  • The philosophy of wage adjudication. Industrial adjudication in a welfare State “attempts, gradually and by stages though it may be, to attain the principal objective of a welfare state, to secure ‘to all citizens justice social and economic’”; the immediate objective is a wage structure which does justice to both labour and capital and establishes harmony for production.
  • The elasticity of the three tiers. The three categories — and their variants (comfort or decency level, subsistence level, poverty or floor level) — do not mean the same thing in all countries or in all industries; their content expands with the economy. “What may be a fair wage in a given industry today may cease to be fair and may border on the minimum wage in future.” This observation is the direct ancestor of the sixth component added in Reptakos Brett thirty-four years later.
  • Starvation wages cannot be encouraged. In under-developed countries with large-scale unemployment, unorganised labour may be available on starvation wages, but “the employment of labour on starvation wages cannot be encouraged or favoured in a modern democratic welfare state”.
  • Practical wisdom for Tribunals. A substantial reduction in wages is likely to lead to discontent and disharmony, but an industry burdened beyond its capacity may itself be destroyed, leading to unemployment; both consequences must be weighed.
  • Judicial restraint on appeal. Normally the Supreme Court would be “slow to entertain an objection that some of the considerations which have weighed with the Appellate Tribunal in reaching its final decision are either invalid or are not borne out by sufficient evidence on record”.
The two-line statement of the ratio (memorise this)

(1) At or near the level of the minimum or bare subsistence wage, a wage structure can never be reduced, not even for the employer’s financial stringency. (2) Above that level, it may be reduced if the employer establishes a genuine case on the merits before the Tribunal — which will consider alternatives such as retrenchment already effected, the duration of the difficulty, and the industrial-peace consequences, and may impose conditions.

How to use this case in an answer
  • For “kinds of wages” (essay): open with the three-tier classification, quote the FWC definitions and the Tillyard passage, then use Crown Aluminium for the proposition that the categories are elastic in content.
  • For “can wages be reduced?”: the case is the answer — the two-tier rule plus the Tribunal’s checklist; and Reptakos Brett is its reinforcement a generation later.
  • For “bounty vs wages”: the substance-over-label principle — conduct, pleadings and duration decide, not nomenclature.
  • For PUDR-type questions: use the sentence “No industry has a right to exist unless it is able to pay its workmen at least a bare minimum wage” to link the statutory floor to the constitutional holding.
Four precision guards (avoid these slips)
  1. The Court did not hold that the appellant’s financial difficulty was unreal; it held that the Appellate Tribunal’s protective modification was not shown to be wrong and was not based merely on a supposed convention.
  2. The case does not lay down a rule that the wage level of an establishment may be reduced merely because comparable concerns pay less; capacity to pay is a distinct enquiry (Reptakos Brett makes this explicit).
  3. The two-scale point (existing workmen versus new entrants) was left open. Do not cite the case as authority either way on that question.
  4. The reference was under §10 of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947; the wage-fixation principles applied by the Tribunal were the Omnibus Engineering Awards, not the Minimum Wages Act — the Act enters this case as background, and through Article 43-type reasoning, not as the source of the rate.

CCase 25 — Greaves Cotton & Co. Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1964 SC 689 — and the Industry-cum-Region Formula

C.1 The formula in one paragraph

The industry-cum-region formula is the working method by which industrial tribunals fix a fair wage (and dearness allowance). Since the fair wage is a comparative standard, the Tribunal does not invent a figure; it builds one out of two coordinates — the industry coordinate (what comparable concerns engaged in the same line of business pay) and the region coordinate (what comparable establishments in the same geographical area pay). The wage of the establishment under adjudication must be located within the band formed by these comparators, adjusted for its own capacity to pay, and then converted into scales (minimum, maximum, annual increment, and the span of years in which the maximum is reached), with “adjustment” increments to fit existing employees into the new scale.

Two question have always troubled the formula: which coordinate gets the greater weight? and what is a “comparable” concern? Both were answered in Greaves Cotton, which is why the case is prescribed.

C.2 The two predecessor decisions

Workmen of Hindusthan Motors v. Hindusthan Motors, (1962) 2 LLJ 352

The Court observed that it is “ordinarily desirable to have as much uniformity as possible in the wage-scales of different concerns of the same industry working in the same region, as this puts similar industries more or less on an equal footing in their production struggle”, and applied to Hindusthan Motors the wage scales awarded by the Third Major Engineering Tribunal in Bengal. This is the industry-emphasis strand: where many concerns of the same industry operate in the same region, wage parity protects competition.

French Motor Car Co. Ltd. v. Workmen, (1962) 2 LLJ 744

So far as clerical and subordinate staff are concerned, the Court held that it may be possible to take into account even concerns engaged in different lines of business, “for the work of clerical and subordinate staff is more or less the same in all kinds of concerns”. This is the region-emphasis strand, and it also supplied the law on adjustment increments: adjustments are generally granted when scales are fixed for the first time, but where scales already existed they may still be granted — “sparingly”, and on the facts of each case.

The two decisions were cited at the bar in Greaves Cotton as being in conflict. The Court held that they are not: they are two applications of one principle, and which coordinate predominates depends on a single variable — the density of comparable concerns of the same industry in the region.

Greaves Cotton & Co. Ltd. and three other companies v. Their Workmen

(1964) 5 SCR 362 : AIR 1964 SC 689 (the LB-503 case list cites it as AIR 1964 SC 639) · Before K.N. Wanchoo, J. · Nine appeals by special leave from the awards of the Industrial Tribunal, Bombay · LB-503 case material, pp. 180–192
Industry-cum-region formula “Density test” Total wage packet Adjustment increments Uniform DA Gratuity ceiling
Context — why the case matters

This is the case that converted a slogan (“industry-cum-region”) into a usable method. It tells a tribunal when to look sideways at competitors and when to look around at the neighbourhood; it insists that the comparison be made on the total pay packet and not component by component; it prohibits the invention of categories that exist nowhere in the comparators; and it explains when “adjustment” increments are justified.

Facts
  1. The four companies. (i) Greaves Cotton & Co. Ltd. — the first company, whose main activity was to invest money in manufacturing concerns; (ii) Greaves Cotton and Crompton Parkinson Private Ltd. — distribution of the products of Crompton Parkinson (Works) India Ltd., with servicing and repair at its workshop; (iii) Kenyon Greaves Private Ltd. — manufacture of high-grade interstranded ropes for the textile industry; and (iv) Ruston and Hornsby (India) Private Ltd. — manufacture of oil engines and pumps. The last three were controlled, one way or another, by the first.
  2. The references. Disputes between the four companies and their workmen were referred to the Industrial Tribunal, Bombay by nine reference orders between April and December 1959 — two references for each of the first three companies and three for Ruston and Hornsby. The main dispute was wages, dearness allowance and gratuity; nine awards were made, the principal award on wages and DA being common.
  3. Stagnation. Wages and DA in the four companies had continued unchanged since 1950, when the last award between the parties was made; there was no serious dispute about the companies’ financial capacity, and because the first company controlled the others the scales for clerical and subordinate staff were the same across the group.
  4. What the Tribunal did. It dealt with clerical and subordinate staff separately from factory workmen. For the clerical and subordinate staff it revised wages and DA after comparison with “comparable concerns”, provided for fitting the staff into the new scales with one to three extra increments depending on length of service between 1950 and 1959, and made the award effective from 1 April 1959 — a week before the first reference. For factory workmen it prescribed rates without comparing with comparable concerns, introduced a new seventh category of “higher unskilled”, gave them the same DA as the clerical and subordinate staff, and directed adjustments on the same basis. It also framed a gratuity scheme with a maximum of 20 months’ wages and a provision that where an employee was dismissed or discharged for misconduct causing financial loss, gratuity to the extent of that loss alone would be withheld.
Issues
  1. Whether the Tribunal went wrong in its application of the industry-cum-region formula, and in particular in leaning on the region aspect rather than the industry aspect for clerical and subordinate staff.
  2. Whether the Tribunal was carried away by the recommendations of the tripartite conference on need-based minimum wages, and thereby pitched the scales too high.
  3. Whether the comparison was made with concerns that were in truth comparable.
  4. Whether the Tribunal ought to have considered the total wage packet (basic wage plus DA) rather than the components separately.
  5. Whether the creation of the category “higher unskilled”, and the fixation of factory-workmen’s wages without comparison, were valid.
  6. Whether adjustment increments and the retrospective effect from 1 April 1959 were justified, and whether the gratuity scheme (20 months; loss-linked deduction) was proper.
Arguments
For the appellants (the companies)
  1. The Tribunal failed to apply the industry-cum-region formula properly: it was “carried away” by the tripartite conference’s need-based minimum wage recommendations, and the wages awarded were higher than those prevalent in any comparable concern.
  2. It leaned on the region aspect rather than the industry aspect for clerical and subordinate staff, contrary to Hindusthan Motors, which required wage parity with comparable concerns in the same industry so that all concerns compete on equal production costs.
  3. Comparisons were made with concerns that were not comparable.
  4. The Tribunal failed to consider the total effect of the increase in basic wage and DA taken together, so that the total pay packet could not be seen against the total pay packet of comparable concerns.
  5. The Tribunal invented a category of “higher unskilled” workmen which no party had demanded and which did not exist in any comparable concern.
  6. For factory workmen, no attempt was even made to compare with the wages prevalent in concerns the Tribunal itself considered comparable.
  7. The adjustments, the retrospective operation from 1 April 1959, and the raised gratuity ceiling of 20 months were all unjustified.
For the respondents (the workmen)
  1. The need-based minimum wage norms of the tripartite conference provided the correct standard for low-paid categories, and the Tribunal’s reference to them was legitimate.
  2. Since the four companies did not belong to a single, well-defined industry with numerous direct competitors, the region comparison was appropriate — particularly for clerical and subordinate staff, whose work is broadly the same in all concerns.
  3. The comparators were genuinely comparable: both sides had filed scales, and some of the concerns cited by the two sides were the same.
  4. The financial capacity of the companies was not disputed, and there had been no revision for nine years; scales pitched nearer the highest in the comparators were therefore justified.
  5. Adjustments were justified because the companies’ historical increment rates were low and the increments lasted unusually long; the facts were distinguishable from French Motor Car Co.
  6. Uniform DA across clerical, subordinate and factory staff removes an anomaly, since the pressure of prices is the same on everyone earning the same wage.
Decision (Holding)
  1. Tripartite norms: although the Tribunal discussed the conference recommendations at length (because the workmen had based their claims on them), it did not in fact follow them; its final decision rested on comparisons with comparable concerns. The mere reference to those recommendations did not vitiate the award.
  2. Clerical and subordinate staff: the Tribunal was not wrong in emphasising the region aspect for these categories, for two reasons — the four companies were not engaged in the same line of industry, and it was not shown (as it had been in Hindusthan Motors) that there were a large number of comparable concerns in the same region. The main company was in substance an investment and financial concern; the Tribunal was right to take for comparison such companies as would stand comparison with it. The wage scales fixed were upheld.
  3. Total wage packet: the Tribunal should have specifically examined the total wage packet (basic + DA) — it had not — but since the scales fixed were less than the highest and more than the lowest in comparable concerns, and the DA fixed was also between those extremes, the total packet could not be said to be the highest in the region, and the omission did not vitiate the award.
  4. Factory workmen: the appeals were allowed on this limb. The Tribunal had “paid scant regard” to the exemplars filed before it and had made no comparison for factory workmen as it had for clerical staff; and its new category of “higher unskilled” was unjustified — “there cannot be degrees of want of skill among the unskilled class”. The wage scales for factory workmen were set aside and the matter remanded for fresh fixation within the six pre-existing categories (unskilled, semi-skilled I, semi-skilled II, skilled I, skilled II, skilled III), after taking into account the wages prevalent in comparable concerns, with liberty to the parties to lead further evidence. The DA award for factory workmen was set aside consequentially, since the Tribunal must compare the total pay packet in fixing both.
  5. Dearness allowance: for clerical staff, upheld — the new scale was broadly in line with scales recently fixed in the region, with only a slight improvement on the second hundred. For subordinate staff, the Tribunal was right to place them on the same DA scale as clerical staff: DA is meant to neutralise the rise in the cost of living, which is felt uniformly; the need for uniformity was supported by the trend in the region; and, the Court added, subordinate staff and factory workmen “these days are as keen to educate their children as clerical staff”.
  6. Adjustments: upheld. The question is always one of fact and circumstance; the companies’ first rate of increment (for example, ₹5 for junior clerks) had lasted far longer than in comparable concerns (13 years as against three or four, or at most eight), and the scales were on the low side — so the grant of adjustment increments could not be said to be wrong. French Motor Car Co. was distinguished because there the pre-existing scales were already the highest in the region.
  7. Retrospective effect: fixing 1 April 1959 was not “really retrospective”, being practically the date of the first reference (8 April 1959) in the case of the main company; the order was not interfered with.
  8. Gratuity: upheld. The loss-linked deduction for misconduct was the usual provision in the region; and as tribunals had begun to award higher ceilings (in one concern, Mackinnon Mackenzie, as much as thirty months by agreement), a ceiling of 20 months called for no interference.

Result: the appeals were dismissed as to retrospective effect, adjustments, and the wages and DA of clerical and subordinate staff; allowed as to factory workmen, with a remand; and dismissed as to gratuity. The new award on remand would also operate from 1 April 1959. Parties to bear their own costs; two months allowed for payment of arrears.

Ratio Decidendi — the density test
The principle of the two decisions, as stated by Wanchoo J.

“In applying the industry-cum-region formula for fixing wage-scales the Tribunal should lay stress on the industry part of the formula if there are large number of concerns in the same region carrying on the same industry; in such a case in order that production cost may not be unequal and there may be equal competition, wages should generally be fixed on the basis of the comparable industries, namely, industries of the same kind. But where the number of industries of the same kind in a particular region is small it is the region part of the industry-cum-region formula which assumes importance, particularly in the case of clerical and subordinate staff, for … there is not much difference in the work of this class of employees in different industries.”

Principles and observations
  • Comparisons must be made, and made honestly. Where a tribunal compares nothing, or ignores the exemplars on record, its award cannot stand — hence the remand for factory workmen.
  • The unit of comparison is the total pay packet. Basic wage and DA interact; a moderate basic with a generous DA may produce an excessive total. The Tribunal must compare totals, though a failure to do so is not automatically fatal if the resulting figures are independently within the range.
  • Artificial categories are impermissible. A wage category must correspond to something real in the industry; “higher unskilled” did not, because want of skill admits of no degrees.
  • DA should be uniform for equal wages. “Time has now come when employees getting same wages should get the same dearness allowance irrespective of whether they are working as clerks, or members of subordinate staff or factory-workmen”, because “the pressure of high prices is the same on these various kinds of employees”.
  • Adjustment is discretionary, not automatic. Extra increments to fit existing employees into a revised scale may be given even a second time, but sparingly, where the earlier increments were low and long-drawn; where the earlier scales were already the highest in the region, adjustments will be refused.
  • Effective dates should track the reference. A date a week before the first reference is, practically, the date of the reference and is not objectionable.
  • Gratuity is a wage-related benefit that a tribunal may mould — a ceiling may rise with the trend in the region, and a deduction for misconduct may legitimately be confined to the financial loss caused.
Answer framework — “Explain the industry-cum-region formula” (short note or sub-question)
  1. Define: the method of fixing a fair wage by comparing the establishment with (a) comparable concerns in the same industry and (b) comparable concerns in the same region, and then adjusting for capacity to pay.
  2. Purpose: fair wage is a comparative, not an absolute, standard; comparisons secure (i) equal production costs and fair competition, and (ii) wages that match local living costs.
  3. The two precedents: Hindusthan Motors (industry emphasis), French Motor Car Co. (region emphasis, especially clerical and subordinate staff).
  4. The reconciling case: Greaves Cotton — the density test (large numbers of same-industry concerns → industry; few → region) — and the case’s own answer on its facts (region emphasis upheld because the four companies were differently placed and the main company was an investment/finance concern).
  5. The safeguards: total pay packet; no invented categories; adjustments sparingly; DA uniformity for equal wages; comparators genuinely comparable.
  6. Conclusion: the formula is a flexible method, not a rigid rule; it is the practical content of the fair wage tier, and it is applied “in the light of all relevant considerations”, including capacity to pay.
Worked example — applying the formula to a real-world pattern

Suppose a Kolkata-based auto-components manufacturer of 400 workmen, in a region where twelve comparable auto-component units (of similar size) operate, claims that the wages it must pay should be fixed by reference to West Bengal industry as a whole. Apply Greaves Cotton: (i) for factory workmen, because there are many same-industry concerns in the region, the industry coordinate governs — parity of production costs matters, and the comparator set should be the auto-component units, not, say, tea plantations; (ii) for clerical and subordinate staff, the Tribunal may legitimately use a wider set of regional comparators of comparable standing and turnover, because the nature of their work does not vary much between industries; (iii) in either case the comparison must be of the total pay packet (basic + DA, and DA being uniform across categories earning the same wage) and the resulting scales must lie between the highest and lowest comparators, adjusted for the employer’s capacity, with adjustment increments if the establishment’s own historical increments were low and long-drawn. If the Tribunal were to invent a category such as “senior-unskilled” that exists nowhere in the region, the award on that head would fail, exactly as “higher unskilled” failed in 1964.

Three limits of the formula (useful for “critical analysis” marks)
  1. It fixes fair wages, not minimum wages. The statutory minimum is an absolute floor: a State or Central notification under §§5–9 of the Code does not depend on comparators, and Unichoyi refuses to test it against capacity to pay.
  2. It is vulnerable to the employer’s choice of comparators. Much of the litigation is about the adequacy of the comparator set; the safeguard is that both parties file schedules and the Tribunal must show that it has considered them.
  3. It cannot cure an artificially low base. If the entire region pays badly, the formula will reproduce the badness — which is precisely why the statutory minimum wage and now the floor wage exist as correctives outside the formula.

DCase 26 — Workmen v. The Management of Reptakos Brett & Co. Ltd., (1992) 1 SCC 290 : AIR 1992 SC 504

The Workmen v. The Management of Reptakos Brett & Co. Ltd.

(1992) 1 SCC 290 : AIR 1992 SC 504 · Before Kuldip Singh, J. · Appeal by special leave against the award of the Industrial Tribunal, Madras (14 October 1987) as upheld by the Madras High Court (Single Judge and Division Bench) · LB-503 case material, pp. 193–205
Slab system of DA Over-neutralisation Sixth component Burden of proof 30-year settlement history
Context — the modern turning point

Reptakos Brett is the case in which the Supreme Court (i) updated the concept of the minimum wage for the 1990s by adding a sixth, social component, and (ii) re-affirmed Crown Aluminium’s protection against downward revision with a much more demanding burden of proof on the employer. It is also the case that supplies the definitional passages on living wage and minimum wage that students use in every “concept of wages” essay — because the judgment reproduces the Fair Wages Committee’s definitions and the 1957 norms verbatim.

Facts
  1. The company and the scheme. Reptakos Brett & Co. Ltd. manufactures pharmaceutical and dietetic speciality products and has three units — two at Bombay and one at Madras. The Madras factory, set up in 1959, is the concern in the case. From the beginning the company had, on its own, provided a slab system of dearness allowance: DA linked both to the cost of living index and to the basic wage — a double-linked DA.
  2. Thirty years of settlements. The first settlement was on 11 August 1964, which accepted the double-linked DA while providing variable DA limited to the cost of living index up to 5.41–5.50; the 18 July 1969 settlement removed that limit; DA rates were revised on 7 August 1971; and further settlements followed on 4 July 1974 and 4 January 1979. Slab system with variable DA thus continued as “the basic constituent of the wage structure in the Company from its inception” and was “consciously accepted by the parties” over almost thirty years.
  3. The 1983 references. The workmen raised demands which were referred for adjudication to the Industrial Tribunal, Madras; the company made counter-demands, also referred. One issue was framed as: “Whether the demand of the management for restructuring of the dearness allowance scheme is justified, if so, to frame a scheme?”
  4. The tribunal’s award. By its award dated 14 October 1987 the Tribunal accepted the company’s case, abolished the slab system and directed that in future DA be linked only to the cost of living index at 33 paise per point over 100 points of the Madras City Cost of Living Index (1936 base). The company’s case, as recorded by the Tribunal, was that the pre-war (1936) wage of a worker in the Madras region was ₹26, evidenced by Buckingham and Carnatic Mills Ltd. and Good Pastor Press; that ₹26 equivalent to 100% neutralisation meant 26 paise per point; that at 2780 points in December 1984 the fully neutralised wage would be ₹722.80 (₹26 basic + ₹696.80 DA); and that since a lower-grade workman was in fact getting ₹1,394 (basic + DA + house rent allowance), the rate of neutralisation was 192% — the workmen were living on a “high-wage island”.
  5. The High Court. Both sides filed writ petitions; while they were pending the parties filed a joint memorandum dated 13 June 1988 recording a settlement of 13 May 1988 and stating that they were not pressing their petitions except on the DA-restructuring issue. The learned Single Judge upheld the Tribunal; the Division Bench dismissed the workmen’s writ appeal by judgment dated 14 September 1989. The workmen appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave.
Issues
  1. Whether the company was entitled to restructure (in substance, reduce) the DA scheme by abolishing the slab system, on the ground that the slab system had resulted in over-neutralisation and landed the workmen on a “high-wage island”.
  2. Whether the computation of “192% neutralisation”, based on a pre-war wage of ₹26, was sound.
  3. Whether a DA scheme voluntarily introduced and reiterated in settlements over thirty years could be altered to the prejudice of the workmen.
  4. What is the content of the “minimum wage” at the end of the twentieth century — in particular whether the 1957 five-norm formula remains adequate.
Arguments
For the appellant workmen (Mr M.K. Ramamurthi)
  1. The arithmetic is wrong. The Tribunal and the High Court “grossly erred” in taking ₹26 as the pre-war wage of a worker in the Madras region and, on that basis, reaching 192% neutralisation for December 1984.
  2. Principle: even assuming over-neutralisation, unless the pay structure is within the concept of a living wage and it is further proved that the company is financially unable to bear the burden, the existing pay structure or DA scheme cannot be revised to the prejudice of the workmen (Crown Aluminium; Ahmedabad Mills Owners’ Association).
  3. Voluntariness and settlement history: a DA scheme voluntarily introduced by the company and reiterated in various settlements cannot be altered to the detriment of the workmen.
  4. Reliance on Indian Hume Pipe Co. — an employer cannot abolish a DA scheme which has worked smoothly for almost thirty years merely on the plea that it is more beneficial than the schemes of other industries in the region.
  5. No evidence of incapacity: the Tribunal’s finding of financial inability was given in a different reference (on the workmen’s demands for bonus and other benefits) and not on the company’s own demand for restructuring DA.
For the respondent company (Mr F.S. Nariman)
  1. Comparable concerns in the region paid lower and simpler DA; the company’s workmen were on a high-wage island.
  2. An existing DA scheme can be revised even to the prejudice of the workmen, for which Crown Aluminium is authority.
  3. Reliance on Killick Nixon Ltd. v. Killick & Allied Companies Employees’ Union, (1975) 2 SCC 260 — where a ceiling on an over-generous DA had been permitted — and on Monthly Rated Workmen at the Wadala factory of the Indian Hume Pipe Co., (1986) 2 SCR 484, where over-generous DA was said to be restructurable where genuinely unsustainable.
  4. The Tribunal had found, in the bonus reference, that the company was not in a financial position to bear an additional burden (para 20 of the award, Ex. M-13).
  5. Under Article 136 the Court should not interfere where there is “some basis, some material to validate the award” (Shaw Wallace & Co. v. Workmen; Statesman Ltd. v. Workmen).
Decision (Holding)
  1. The appeal was allowed. The award of the Tribunal and the judgments of the Single Judge and the Division Bench were set aside; the company’s reference on the issue of restructuring the dearness allowance was declined and rejected; the workmen were awarded costs throughout, assessed at ₹25,000.
  2. The “192% neutralisation” computation collapsed. The ₹26 figure rested on a misreading of the Textile Labour Enquiry Committee report: that Committee had excluded a 25% dietary addition on the ground that it belonged to the “living wage” standard, whereas the Supreme Court had held in Standard Vacuum that the Committee in fact did not proceed beyond the minimum level and that ₹50–55 was the need-based minimum wage in 1940. It was therefore not possible to accept ₹26/28 as the minimum wage in the Madras region in 1936. The Good Pastor Press case had never decided the point at all — the Tribunal’s reliance on it was “wholly misplaced”.
  3. The “present value” method: “the purchasing power of today’s wage cannot be judged by making calculations which are solely based on 30/40 years old wage structure. The only reasonable way to determine the category of wage structure is to evaluate each component of the category concerned in the light of the prevailing prices.”
  4. The burden of proof: “It is … for the management which is seeking restructuring of DA scheme to the disadvantage of the workmen to prove to the satisfaction of the tribunal that the wage structure in the industry concerned is well above minimum level and the management is financially not in a position to bear the burden of the existing wage structure.” The company had proved neither.
  5. The thirty-year scheme was protected: “the DA scheme — which had stood the test of time for almost thirty years and had been approved by various settlements between the parties — has been unjustifiably abolished by the courts below”. Merely being higher than comparable industries does not justify cutting benefits: what the company had shown was that its DA was “somewhat higher” than others in the region; there was no material to show that it was higher than what the need-based minimum wage would require.
  6. Killick Nixon and Indian Hume Pipe distinguished, not overruled: neither lays down a general rule that slab DA schemes should be abolished to the prejudice of workers; each turned on its own facts.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. The sixth component. The need-based minimum wage of 1957 is outdated; “children’s education, medical requirement, minimum recreation including festivals/ceremonies and provision for old age, marriages, etc. should further constitute 25 per cent of the total minimum wage.”
  2. The absolute floor, restated and strengthened. “In an industry or employment where the wage structure is at the level of minimum wage, no such revision at all, is permissible, not even on the ground of financial stringency.”
  3. The two-limbed burden on the employer who seeks a reduction: (a) prove that the wage structure is well above the minimum level; and (b) prove genuine financial inability to bear the existing burden. Both, and by correct and current figures.
  4. Long-standing, settlement-approved benefits are part of the wage structure and cannot be unilaterally restructured; the employer’s conduct over decades is evidence of the character of the payment (a proposition that links this case directly back to Crown Aluminium’s bounty-versus-wages holding).
  5. Old base years are unreliable. Neutralisation must be computed on prevailing prices, evaluating each component of the wage structure as it stands today.
Principles and observations
  • Wage categories re-stated: “Broadly, the wage structure can be divided into three categories — the basic ‘minimum wage’ which provides bare subsistence and is at poverty line level, a little above is the ‘fair wage’ and finally the ‘living wage’ which comes at a comfort level. It is not possible to demarcate these levels of wage structure with any precision.”
  • Wage is no longer a mere contract: “A worker’s wage is no longer a contract between an employer and an employee. It has the force of collective bargaining under the labour laws. Each category of the wage structure has to be tested at the anvil of social justice which is the live-fibre of our society today.”
  • The purpose of DA: “the only purpose of DA is to enable a worker — in the event of a rise in cost of living — to purchase the same amount of goods of basic necessity as before”, i.e. neutralisation, of which 100% is the maximum purpose; over-neutralisation beyond that is what the employer may legitimately attack — if it can prove it.
  • The constitutional promise and the gap: a living wage has been promised under the Constitution (Article 43) and a socialist framework by the 42nd Amendment; but “the promises are piling up but the day of fulfilment is nowhere in sight”, and industrial wages as a whole “has not yet risen higher than the level of minimum wage”.
  • The floor is an entitlement: “The employees are entitled to the minimum wage at all times and under all circumstances. An employer who cannot pay the minimum wage has no right to engage labour and no justification to run the industry.”
  • No jurisdiction to rewrite a lawful scheme merely because it is generous: even the Article 136 discretion of the Court will be exercised where the tribunal and the High Court have acted “in total oblivion of the legal position” and “manifest injustice has been caused to the workmen”.
Answer framework — “Can an employer reduce DA on the ground of over-neutralisation?”
  1. State the rule: a wage structure above the minimum may be revised downward, but never a minimum-level structure (Crown Aluminium; Ahmedabad Mills Owners’ Association; Reptakos).
  2. State the burden: the employer must prove, to the Tribunal’s satisfaction, (a) that the structure is well above the minimum level and (b) financial inability to bear it — using current figures, not old base years.
  3. Explain over-neutralisation: neutralisation is the extent to which DA compensates the rise in the cost of living; 100% is the purpose; anything above it is over-neutralisation, which an employer may attack — subject to the burden.
  4. Apply Reptakos: the ₹26 base was a misreading of the Textile Committee report; Standard Vacuum had fixed ₹50–55; the scheme had survived thirty years and several settlements; the incapacity finding was from another reference — hence the claim failed.
  5. Distinguish Killick Nixon and Indian Hume Pipe (fact-specific) and note the long-standing-benefit principle connecting the case back to Crown Aluminium’s bounty-versus-wages holding.
  6. Conclude with the sixth component and the constitutional frame: the minimum wage is now explicitly social in content, and the direction of movement is upward.
Precision guards
  1. The sixth component is “25 per cent of the total minimum wage” — do not write 20% (that is the 1957 miscellaneous norm) or 15% (that is the in-kind limit in §2(y) of the Code).
  2. Reptakos did not overrule Killick Nixon or Indian Hume Pipe; it distinguished them. Say “distinguished”, not “overruled”.
  3. The case does not hold that DA can never be restructured. It holds that restructuring requires proof of both limbs, on current figures, and that a long-standing, settlement-accepted scheme will not be disturbed without compelling reason.
  4. Do not confuse the slab system (DA linked to both index and basic wage) with the neutralisation rate; the company’s attack was on the slab system because it produced over-neutralisation.

ECase 27 — People’s Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India, AIR 1982 SC 1473

People’s Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India — the Asiad construction workers’ case

AIR 1982 SC 1473 · Supreme Court of India, writ petition under Article 32 treated as a public interest petition on a letter to Bhagwati, J. · Judgment by P.N. Bhagwati, J. · Order dated 11 May 1982 · LB-503 case material, pp. 206–218
Public interest litigation Article 23 — forced labour Minimum wage as fundamental right Principal employer’s liability Jamadar commission
Context — the constitutionalisation of the minimum wage

Until 1982 the minimum wage was, in the eyes of the law, a statutory entitlement: a rate fixed under the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, enforceable through the Act’s inspectorate and claims machinery. PUDR changed its constitutional status. Holding that labour or service rendered for less than the minimum wage is “forced labour” within Article 23, the Court transformed under-payment from a statutory default into a violation of a fundamental right — enforceable under Article 32, against private employers as well as the State, by the workers themselves or by any public-spirited person on their behalf. It is, in the words of the Cornell notes, the “single most important case connecting labour/wage law to fundamental rights”.

Facts
  1. How the case began. The People’s Union for Democratic Rights (PUDR) — an organisation formed to protect democratic rights — commissioned a team of three social scientists to investigate the conditions under which workmen engaged in the various projects connected with the Asian Games (Asiad) 1982 in Delhi were working. The report was sent to Bhagwati J. by letter. Because the letter was based on a report prepared after personal investigation and study, the Court treated it as a writ petition on the judicial side and issued notice to the Union of India, the Delhi Development Authority and the Delhi Administration.
  2. The projects. India was hosting the Asian Games; to complete the work to international standards the Government had undertaken construction of flyovers, stadia, swimming pools, hotels and the Asian Games village complex, farmed out among the Delhi Administration, the DDA and the New Delhi Municipal Committee. These authorities engaged contractors, who were registered as principal employers under §7 of the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970. The contractors engaged workmen through jamadars, who brought them from Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and Orissa.
  3. The violations alleged. (a) The workers were entitled to a minimum wage of ₹9.25 per day (the rate fixed for construction of roads and building operations) but the jamadars deducted ₹1 per day per worker as commission, so the workers received only ₹8.25; (b) the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 was violated — women workers were paid only ₹7 per day, the balance being misappropriated; (c) children below fourteen were employed in construction work, in violation of Article 24 and the Employment of Children Act, 1938; (d) the Contract Labour Act, 1970 and the Inter-State Migrant Workmen (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act, 1979 were breached, depriving workers of living conditions, medical and other facilities; and (e) proper living, medical and other amenities were denied.
  4. What the respondents conceded. The Union of India was “more frank” than the others: it admitted in its affidavit that ₹1 per worker per day was deducted by the jamadars from the wages payable, with the result that workers did not get the minimum wage of ₹9.25, and that this was a violation of the Minimum Wages Act, 1948 — though it claimed prosecutions had been launched and shortfalls recovered (no particulars, however, were given). The Delhi Administration conceded that jamadars “might be” deducting ₹1 a day; the DDA claimed that its written contracts incorporated “Model Rules for the protection of Health and Sanitary Arrangements for Workers” ensuring minimum wages.
Issues
  1. Locus standi: had the petitioners any standing to maintain the writ petition, the rights said to be violated being those of the workers and not of the petitioners?
  2. Maintainability under Article 32: could a writ petition lie when what was alleged was breach of labour statutes rather than of any fundamental right? And could it lie against the Union of India, the Delhi Administration and the DDA when the workmen were employees of the contractors?
  3. Article 23: is there a breach of Article 23 where a person provides labour or service and is paid less than the minimum wage?
  4. Principal employer’s responsibility: to what extent are the authorities who entrusted the work to contractors liable for the contractors’ defaults?
Arguments
For the petitioners (PUDR)
  1. The traditional rule of standing confines access to the person injured; but the Court has “jettisoned” that rule for a liberalised doctrine under which any bona fide member of the public may move the Court on behalf of persons unable, by reason of poverty, disability or social and economic disadvantage, to approach it — even by a letter.
  2. Payment below the statutory minimum wage, given the total absence of bargaining power of these workers, is forced labour within Article 23, and Article 23 is enforceable against private persons, not merely the State.
  3. Employment of children below fourteen in hazardous construction work directly violates Article 24 (which, like Articles 17 and 23, is enforceable against the whole world).
  4. Non-observance of the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 is “in effect and substance” a breach of the equality principle in Article 14; violations of the Contract Labour Act and the Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act deprive workmen of basic human dignity and so engage Article 21.
  5. The authorities who entrusted the work cannot escape responsibility: §20 of the Contract Labour Act and §§17–18 of the Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act place obligations on the principal employer.
For the respondents (Union of India, Delhi Administration, DDA)
  1. PUDR has no locus standi: the rights alleged to be violated are the workers’, PUDR has suffered no personal legal injury and therefore has no cause of action.
  2. No writ under Article 32 lies because the grievances are breaches of ordinary labour legislation, not breaches of fundamental rights; the remedy, if any, is under those statutes.
  3. In any event the workmen were employees of the contractors, not of the respondents; the cause of action, if any, lies against the contractors.
  4. Whatever violations occurred were isolated; prosecutions have been launched and shortfalls recovered.
Decision (Holding)
  1. Locus standi — objection rejected. The traditional rule confining access to those to whom legal injury is caused has been jettisoned; where persons whose rights are violated are, by reason of poverty, disability or social and economic disadvantage, unable to approach the Court, any member of the public acting bona fide and not out of extraneous motivation may move the Court, even by letter, and the Court will treat the letter as a writ petition. The Asiad workmen were “poor, ignorant, illiterate humans”, and the petitioners were admittedly acting out of a sense of public service; the petition was maintainable.
  2. Article 32 — the substantive routes. (a) Article 24: employment of children below fourteen in construction work — plainly a violation of a fundamental right, enforceable against everyone, including the contractors, and the authorities were under a duty to ensure compliance. (b) Equal Remuneration Act: its non-observance is “in effect and substance” a complaint of breach of the principle of equality before the law under Article 14. (c) Contract Labour Act and Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act: the rights and benefits they confer are intended to ensure basic human dignity to workmen; depriving them of those rights violates Article 21 as expanded in Maneka Gandhi and Francis Coralie Mullin. (d) Minimum wage: non-payment violates Article 23.
  3. Principal employers’ liability. Under §20 of the Contract Labour Act, if any amenity required to be provided under §§16–19 is not provided by the contractor, the obligation rests on the principal employer; under §§17 and 18 of the Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act the principal employer is liable to make payment of wages to migrant workmen and to pay the allowances and provide the facilities specified. The authorities therefore “cannot escape their obligation to the workmen to ensure observance of these labour laws by the contractors”, and could not “fold their hands in despair and become silent spectators of the breach of a constitutional prohibition”.
  4. Article 23 — the central holding. “Where a person provides labour or service to another for remuneration which is less than the minimum wage, the labour or service provided by him clearly falls within the scope and ambit of the words ‘forced labour’ under Article 23.” Conversely, a person paid at least the minimum wage receives what he is lawfully entitled to, and no inference of force arises.
  5. Relief and directions. By its order of 11 May 1982 the Court directed that the minimum wage — or the wage payable if higher — be paid by the contractors to the workmen directly, without the intervention of the jamadars, and that jamadars be not entitled to deduct any amount as commission; that if the Union of India, Delhi Administration or DDA finds that a workman has not received the minimum wage, it shall take legal action against the contractor, by prosecution or by recovery of the shortfall; that future government contracts should contain a suitable provision for direct payment and a prohibition on jamadar deductions; and that three Ombudsmen be appointed to make periodic inspections of the construction sites and report on compliance.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. Payment below the minimum wage is forced labour under Article 23. Article 23 is enforceable against private persons as well as the State, and the remedy may be sought by writ petition under Article 32.
  2. “Force” includes economic compulsion. Force is not confined to physical force or force exerted through a legal provision; it includes “compulsion arising from hunger and poverty, want and destitution”. Any factor depriving a person of a choice of alternatives and compelling a particular course of action is “force”; labour so compelled is forced labour.
  3. “Begar” and “other similar forms of forced labour” must be read expansively. The contention that Article 23 is attracted only where there is no remuneration at all was rejected: the additional words “or other similar forms of forced labour” would be rendered futile, and the Article would become “a mere rope of sand” if a dominant party could escape it by paying “a negligible amount of remuneration”.
  4. A contract of service does not create forced labour unless it is compelled. A person cannot be compelled by law or otherwise to continue to serve; but where, by reason of his economically helpless condition, an employee faces “Hobson’s choice — either to starve or to submit to the exploitative terms dictated by the powerful employer”, the appearance of voluntariness is illusory.
  5. The State’s positive obligation. Whenever a fundamental right enforceable against private individuals (Articles 17, 23, 24) is being violated, it is the constitutional obligation of the State to take the necessary steps to interdict the violation — the more so when the victim belongs to the weaker section of humanity and cannot wage a legal battle against a powerful opponent.
  6. Principal employers are answerable for the observance of labour laws by their contractors.
Principles and observations
  • Comparative and international human rights support: the Court drew on Bailey v. Alabama (1910) 219 US 219 — the Thirteenth Amendment “involuntary servitude” jurisprudence — and on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR, 1948): specifically Article 4 (prohibition of slavery and servitude in all their forms) and Article 23(3) (the universal right to just and favourable remuneration ensuring an existence worthy of human dignity); on ILO Convention No. 29 (1930) and Convention No. 105 (1957) on forced or compulsory labour; also on Article 4 of the European Convention on Human Rights and Article 8 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR). International human rights declarations were thus directly marshalled to illuminate the substantive reach of domestic Fundamental Rights under Articles 21 and 23.
  • Interpretive canon: “when interpreting the provisions of the Constitution conferring fundamental rights, the attempt of the court should be to expand the reach and ambit of the fundamental rights rather than to attenuate their meaning and content” (Maneka Gandhi).
  • The socio-economic reading: the Constitution makers enacted Article 23 in Part III rather than Part IV because bonded and forced labour was a “relic of a feudal exploitative society” incompatible with the egalitarian order; putting it among the Directive Principles would have left it unenforceable.
  • Enforcement duty: Government and public authorities must institute an effective system of periodic inspections with occasional surprise inspections, and must not wait for complaints from workmen — “that is the least which a government … is expected to do in a social welfare State”.
  • Limits of Article 23: payment at or above the minimum wage is not forced labour; and the article is not a guarantee of a fair or living wage — a distinction students must state precisely.
The UDHR Dimension in PUDR — Economic Coercion as Servitude

In PUDR, Bhagwati J. recognized that constitutional guarantees and international covenants are meaningless to impoverished workers if they can be forced by hunger into exploitative terms. Invoking the universal principles embodied in Articles 4 and 23 of the UDHR, the Court established that economic compulsion vitiates genuine consent. Where an employer pays less than the statutory minimum wage to workers who have no real bargaining power, the transaction is tainted by economic coercion; it constitutes modern servitude under UDHR Article 4, violates the right to dignity-bearing remuneration under UDHR Article 23(3), and amounts to unconstitutional forced labour under Article 23 of the Indian Constitution.

Real-world afterlives of the case (use these for “contemporary relevance”)
  • The Asiad case and the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976. PUDR was the Supreme Court’s first great proclamation that Article 23 binds private employers. Two years later, in Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) 3 SCC 161, the Court took the same article into the stone quarries of Haryana, reading “forced labour” together with Article 21 and the 1976 Act and evolving the theory of the “bonded labourer” as a person who is compelled to work for little or no wages.
  • Famine relief and minimum wage: in Sanjit Roy v. State of Rajasthan (1983) 1 SCC 525 the Court applied PUDR to hold that paying famine-relief workers less than the minimum wage — a practice sought to be justified by the poverty of the State and the “charity” character of the work — violates Article 23.
  • Construction labour today. The Asiad fact pattern — principal employers, contractors, labour intermediaries, migrant workmen, wage shortfalls — is now regulated by the Building and Other Construction Workers (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act, 1996, which requires registration of employers and workers, welfare boards funded by a cess, and payment of wages in accordance with the Minimum Wages Act (now Chapter II of the Code on Wages). The jamadar of 1982 has become the “labour supplier”, and the legal question — who is the employer of record, and who bears the wage liability — remains the same question the Court answered in PUDR.
  • Public-interest standing today. The liberalised locus standi that PUDR applied to migrant construction workers is now standard constitutional doctrine; it means that wage violations in large projects can, and do, reach the Supreme Court on a letter.
Answer framework — “Discuss PUDR v. Union of India” (15 marks)
  1. Introduction: the case as the constitutionalisation of the minimum wage — from a statutory entitlement under the Minimum Wages Act, 1948 to a fundamental right enforceable under Article 32.
  2. Facts (short): Asiad construction projects, principal employers and contractors, jamadars, ₹9.25 payable and ₹8.25 received, women at ₹7, children below 14, CLRA and ISMW violations.
  3. Preliminary objections and their rejection: locus standi (liberalised standing on a letter) and Article 32 maintainability (Article 24; Article 14 via the Equal Remuneration Act; Article 21 via the dignity-bearing labour statutes).
  4. The core holding: below-minimum payment is forced labour; “force” includes economic compulsion; “other similar forms of forced labour” must not be read narrowly; Bailey v. Alabama as comparative support.
  5. Consequences: principal employers’ statutory liability; the State’s positive duty to enforce; the relief — direct payment, no jamadar commission, ombudsmen, contract clauses.
  6. Critical analysis / significance: dignity and Article 21; the limits (only below-minimum payment attracts Article 23); the practical difficulties of enforcement; and the line of cases that followed (Sanjit Roy; Bandhua Mukti Morcha; the BOCW Act, 1996).
  7. Conclusion: the case converts the wage floor from a policy into a right, and it is the reason the wage floor in the Code on Wages, 2019 and any future floor wage under §9 must be read as a right and not merely as a statutory rate.
Precision guards
  1. The precise holding is about payment below the statutory minimum wage. Payment below a fair or living wage does not attract Article 23 — do not overstate it.
  2. Article 23 is enforceable against private persons as well as the State (like Articles 17 and 24); do not describe it as a State-only right.
  3. The judgment is by Bhagwati, J. (later Chief Justice) and the impugned wage was ₹9.25 per day for Delhi construction work in 1982 — quote figures accurately as historical facts, not as current rates.
  4. The case concerned the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, not the Code on Wages (which came 37 years later); when writing, note the transition expressly, since examiners reward awareness of the repeal.

FThe Code on Wages, 2019 — Chapters II, III and VII

F.1 The scheme of the Code, and its present status

What it is

An Act “to amend and consolidate the laws relating to wages and bonus and matters connected therewith or incidental thereto” — Act No. 29 of 2019, assented to on 8 August 2019, extending to the whole of India, with 69 sections arranged in nine chapters. It repeals and replaces four statutes: the Payment of Wages Act, 1936, the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965 and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 (§69, repeal and savings).

Whether it is in force

Yes. The four labour codes were brought into force with effect from 21 November 2025, and the Code on Wages (Central) Rules, 2026 were notified on 8 May 2026 for the central sphere. Certain provisions dependent on further notification (including the fixing of the statutory floor wage under §9, and the constitution of the Central Advisory Board in its entirety) are still to be operationalised. In the transitional period, the pre-existing rules, notifications and schemes continue so far as they are not inconsistent with the Code.

F.2 Definition of “wages” — §2(y)

§2(y), Code on Wages, 2019 (verbatim)

“(y) ‘wages’ means all remuneration whether by way of salaries, allowances or otherwise, expressed in terms of money or capable of being so expressed which would, if the terms of employment, express or implied, were fulfilled, be payable to a person employed in respect of his employment or of work done in such employment, and includes,—
(i) basic pay;
(ii) dearness allowance; and
(iii) retaining allowance, if any,
but does not include—
(a) any bonus payable under any law for the time being in force, which does not form part of the remuneration payable under the terms of employment;
(b) the value of any house-accommodation, or of the supply of light, water, medical attendance or other amenity or of any service excluded from the computation of wages by a general or special order of the appropriate Government;
(c) any contribution paid by the employer to any pension or provident fund, and the interest which may have accrued thereon;
(d) any conveyance allowance or the value of any travelling concession;
(e) any sum paid to the employed person to defray special expenses entailed on him by the nature of his employment;
(f) house rent allowance;
(g) remuneration payable under any award or settlement between the parties or order of a court or Tribunal;
(h) any overtime allowance;
(i) any commission payable to the employee;
(j) any gratuity payable on the termination of employment;
(k) any retrenchment compensation or other retirement benefit payable to the employee or any ex gratia payment made to him on the termination of employment:
Provided that, for calculating the wages under this clause, if payments made by the employer to the employee under clauses (a) to (i) exceeds one-half, or such other per cent. as may be notified by the Central Government, of the all remuneration calculated under this clause, the amount which exceeds such one-half, or the per cent. so notified, shall be deemed as remuneration and shall be accordingly added in wages under this clause:
Provided further that for the purpose of equal wages to all genders and for the purpose of payment of wages, the emoluments specified in clauses (d), (f), (g) and (h) shall be taken for computation of wage.
Explanation.—Where an employee is given in lieu of the whole or part of the wages payable to him, any remuneration in kind by his employer, the value of such remuneration in kind which does not exceed fifteen per cent. of the total wages payable to him, shall be deemed to form part of the wages of such employee;”

Decoding the definition — a five-step method

StepWhat the provision doesWhy it matters
1. The core concept“All remuneration … expressed in terms of money or capable of being so expressed … payable to a person employed in respect of his employment or of work done in such employment”Remuneration must be (i) monetary or capable of money valuation, (ii) payable under the terms of employment, express or implied, and (iii) referable to the employment
2. The three inclusionsBasic pay, dearness allowance and retaining allowance (if any)This is the rock-bottom of “wages”. Everything else is either excluded or added back by the provisos
3. The eleven exclusions (a)–(k)Bonus under a law; house accommodation and amenities; employer’s PF/pension contribution and interest; conveyance allowance and travelling concession; sums to defray special employment expenses; house rent allowance; remuneration under an award, settlement or court order; overtime allowance; commission; gratuity; retrenchment compensation, other retirement benefits and ex gratia payments on terminationEach of these heads is excluded because the legislature intends it to be treated separately — some are already protected by their own statutory schemes, others (overtime, commission) would distort monthly wage calculations
4. The 50% add-back provisoIf the payments under clauses (a) to (i) exceed one-half of the total remuneration, the excess shall be deemed to be remuneration and added back to wagesThe anti-avoidance rule. It prevents employers from shrinking “wages” — and therefore PF, gratuity, bonus and leave-encashment bases — by inflating allowances. In substance: basic pay + DA + retaining allowance must be at least 50% of total remuneration
5. The second proviso and the ExplanationFor equal wages and payment of wages, the exclusions in clauses (d), (f), (g) and (h) — conveyance allowance, HRA, award/settlement remuneration and overtime — are taken into computation. Remuneration in kind counts as wages up to 15% of total wagesA single definition is deliberately unflattened for two purposes: pay equality (so that an employer cannot justify a gender gap by burying pay in allowances) and timely payment (so that an employer cannot defeat the mode-and-time rules by calling wages an “allowance”)
Worked example — applying the 50% rule

Take a monthly cost to company of ₹60,000: basic pay ₹20,000, dearness allowance ₹5,000, retaining allowance nil, house rent allowance ₹20,000, conveyance ₹5,000, and other allowances ₹10,000. Clauses (a)–(i) payments = HRA + conveyance + others = ₹35,000; that is more than one-half of ₹60,000 (i.e. ₹30,000). The excess, ₹5,000, is deemed remuneration and added back: wages = ₹20,000 + ₹5,000 + ₹5,000 = ₹30,000. If instead the exclusions had been only ₹25,000 — less than one-half — wages would simply have been ₹25,000 (basic + DA), and no add-back would occur. Reverse the same logic to see the consequence: the base for provident fund, gratuity, statutory bonus, leave encashment and overtime changes with the add-back, which is why the definition is the single most consequential provision in the Code for employers — and the one that affects workers most quietly.

Why “wages” ≠ “remuneration”

The Code answers a question the old statutes answered differently in each context: what is the base for statutory dues? By defining wages narrowly (basic + DA + retaining allowance) yet forcing add-backs above the 50% ceiling, it fixes a single base for provident fund, gratuity, statutory bonus, leave encashment and overtime across the labour codes. The Employees’ Provident Fund threshold itself was raised from ₹15,000 to ₹25,000 per month with effect from 17 September 2026 under the Code on Social Security, 2020 — an illustration of how the wage definition reaches far beyond the wage statute.

The definitional residue

Note what the Code does not unify: the Industrial Disputes Act’s §2(rr) definition still governs “wages” for the purposes of retrenchment compensation under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020, and the Employees’ Compensation Act, 1923 (§2(1)(m)) still has its own definition for compensation. Also, §2(z) “worker” and §2(k) “employee” are separate definitions — “employee” includes supervisory, managerial, administrative and technical personnel, which is what makes the minimum wage universal. A precise answer distinguishes the three terms: employee (all employed persons other than apprentices and members of the armed forces), worker (workmen-type categories, excluding those in managerial/administrative capacity and supervisors above the notified wage), and wages (the remuneration defined by §2(y)).

(2) The cost of living allowance and the cash value of the concessions in respect of supplies of essential commodities at concession rate shall be computed by such authority, as the appropriate Government may by notification, appoint, at such intervals and in accordance with such directions as may be specified or given by the appropriate Government from time to time.”

(2) Every committee appointed by the appropriate Government under clause (a) of sub-section (1) shall consist of persons— (a) representing employers; (b) representing employees which shall be equal in number of the members specified in clause (a); and (c) independent persons, not exceeding one-third of the total members of the committee.
(3) After considering the recommendation of the committee … or … all representations received by it before the date specified in the notification …, the appropriate Government shall by notification fix, or as the case may be, revise the minimum rates of wages and unless such notification otherwise provides, it shall come into force on the expiry of three months from the date of its issue: Provided that where the appropriate Government proposes to revise the minimum rates of wages in the manner specified in clause (b) of sub-section (1), it shall also consult concerned Advisory Board constituted under section 42.
(4) The appropriate Government shall review or revise minimum rates of wages ordinarily at an interval not exceeding five years.”

Common misconceptions about minimum wage fixation — say these correctly
  1. “The minimum wage is fixed only for scheduled employments.” Wrong after the Code. The Schedule of “scheduled employments” belonged to the Minimum Wages Act, 1948. §5 of the Code applies to every employee in every establishment, organised or unorganised — the single biggest expansion in coverage.
  2. “Inability to pay can justify paying below the minimum.” Wrong. That was the law under Crown Aluminium and Unichoyi, and it remains the law: the floor does not bend for capacity.
  3. “The employer can choose the skill category.” No — the categories of unskilled, semi-skilled, skilled and highly skilled, and the zone, are fixed by the Government (§6(6)(a)); the employer only applies the notified rate.
  4. “DA is discretionary.” Once the structure includes a cost of living allowance under §7(1)(a), it must be adjusted “at such intervals and in such manner as the appropriate Government may direct” — it is part of the statutory minimum, not a gift.

F.4 Floor wage — §9, and the power of the Central Government to fix it

§9 — Power of Central Government to fix floor wage (verbatim)

“(1) The Central Government shall fix floor wage taking into account minimum living standards of a worker in such manner as may be prescribed: Provided that different floor wage may be fixed for different geographical areas.
(2) The minimum rates of wages fixed by the appropriate Government under section 6 shall not be less than the floor wage and if the minimum rates of wages fixed by the appropriate Government earlier is more than the floor wage, then, the appropriate Government shall not reduce such minimum rates of wages fixed by it earlier.
(3) The Central Government may, before fixing the floor wage under sub-section (1), obtain the advice of the Central Advisory Board constituted under sub-section (1) of section 42 and consult State Governments in such manner as may be prescribed.”

Analysis — the four things §9 does

  • Creates a two-level structure. The floor wage is national (or geographical-area-specific) and is fixed by the Central Government; the minimum wage remains with the appropriate Government (Central or State) under §6. The floor sits below the minimum wage as a national baseline: §9(2) forbids any minimum rate below the floor.
  • Freezes a one-way ratchet. Where a State (or the Centre, for the central sphere) is already paying above the floor, it cannot reduce the existing minimum rate to the floor. Downward revision is statutorily barred — a legislative echo of Crown Aluminium and Reptakos Brett.
  • Prescribes a standard, not a number: the floor is to take into account the “minimum living standards of a worker” in the manner to be prescribed. The 2025–26 draft Rules would measure that by minimum living standards including food, clothing and housing for a family of three consumption units, with periodic adjustment for cost of living variation and revision ordinarily every five years.
  • Requires consultation: §9(3) requires the advice of the Central Advisory Board (which, under §42(1), is a tripartite body with five State representatives, one-third of the members being women) and consultation with State Governments. Legally the advice is permissive (“may obtain”), but as a matter of administration the exercise cannot be completed without it.
The floor wage in the examination — how to write it up
  • Concept: a national (or area-wise) baseline below which no minimum wage may be fixed — the first time Indian law has created a binding national wage floor.
  • Institutional design: Central Government + Central Advisory Board + consultation with States; different floor wages for different geographical areas recognising regional price differences.
  • Statutory effect: §9(2) — two prohibitions: (i) no minimum rate below the floor; (ii) no reduction of an existing higher rate.
  • Purpose: to end the wide inter-State disparity in minimum wages (in 2025–26 the lowest State unskilled minimum wage was about ₹11,000 a month and the highest about ₹20,000) and to give the Article 43 promise a statutory floor.
  • Present status: in force in the statute, but not yet notified; pending that notification the States’ own notified rates remain the operative bottom.
  • Criticism worth scoring: a floor below all existing State rates changes little in practice unless it is pitched at a genuinely living level; and because it is “in the manner prescribed”, its effectiveness depends on the Rules and the periodic revision cycle.

Real example — the deduction provisions at work

A factory deducts ₹4,000 as “damage to machinery” and ₹2,500 as “disciplinary fine” from a worker’s ₹18,000 monthly wage. Two questions arise. First, has the process been followed? A fine under §19 requires (i) the act or omission to be specified in a notice approved by the appropriate Government and exhibited on the premises, and (ii) an opportunity of showing cause; the fine cannot exceed 3% of the wage for the period — here the ceiling is ₹540, so ₹2,500 fails outright. A damage deduction under §21 requires proof of the actual damage attributable to the employee’s negligence or default, and again an opportunity to show cause. Secondly, is the total within §18(3)? The permitted aggregate deduction in any wage period cannot exceed 50% of the wages. If the two deductions together were, say, ₹11,000 on a wage of ₹18,000, the excess over ₹9,000 is recoverable only in the prescribed manner — and, if the money was meant for a PF or trust account and was not deposited, §18(5) protects the employee from the employer’s default.

F.6 The Inspector-cum-Facilitator and the enforcement architecture — Chapters VI, VII and VIII

§51 — Appointment of Inspector-cum-Facilitators and their powers (verbatim, sub-sections 1–6)

“(1) The appropriate Government may, by notification, appoint Inspector-cum-Facilitators for the purposes of this Code who shall exercise the powers conferred on them under sub-section (4) throughout the State or such geographical limits assigned in relation to one or more establishments situated in such State or geographical limits or in one or more establishments, irrespective of geographical limits, assigned to him by the appropriate Government, as the case may be.

(4) Every Inspector-cum-Facilitator appointed under sub-section (1) shall be deemed to be public servant within the meaning of section 21 of the Indian Penal Code.
(5) The Inspector-cum-Facilitator may— (a) advice to employers and workers relating to compliance with the provisions of this Code; (b) inspect the establishments as assigned to him by the appropriate Government, subject to the instructions or guidelines issued by the appropriate Government from time to time.
(6) Subject to the provisions of sub-section (4), the Inspector-cum-Facilitator may,— (a) examine any person who is found in any premises of the establishment, whom the Inspector-cum-Facilitator has reasonable cause to believe, is a worker of the establishment; (b) require any person to give any information, which is in his power to give with respect to the names and addresses of the persons; (c) search, seize or take copies of such register, record of wages or notices or portions thereof as the Inspector-cum-Facilitator may consider relevant in respect of an offence under this Code and which the Inspector-cum-Facilitator has reason to believe has been committed by the employer; (d) bring to the notice of the appropriate Government defects or abuses not covered by any law for the time being in force; and (e) exercise such other powers as may be prescribed.”

Sub-sections (7) and (8) add that a person required to produce documents or give information is legally bound to do so within the meaning of §§175 and 176 of the Indian Penal Code, and that the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 applies to a search or seizure as it applies to a search under a warrant issued under §94 of that Code.

What is new about the “Inspector-cum-Facilitator”?

From inspector-raj to facilitator

The statutory title and the first listed function — to “advice to employers and workers relating to compliance” — shift the emphasis from prosecution to compliance support. The model returns where the law is technical (wage records, registers, wage slips) and where the employer’s default is inadvertent rather than exploitative.

Web-based and randomised inspection

§51(2) and (3): the inspection scheme may provide for generation of a web-based inspection and electronic calling of information, and for randomised selection of establishments for inspection. The object is to remove the discretion that made inspection vulnerable to influence, and to reduce physical visits.

Compliance before prosecution

Under §54(3) the Inspector-cum-Facilitator must, before initiating prosecution for record-keeping offences and for contraventions of “any other provision”, give the employer a written direction fixing a time for compliance; if the employer complies, no prosecution. The concession is lost if the same violation is repeated within five years.

F.8 Critical appraisal — five strengths, five criticisms, and a conclusion

Strengths

  • Universality. Minimum wage protection is no longer hostage to a scheduled-employment list; every employee is covered.
  • A floor above the market. The §9 floor wage, once notified, will give the country a binding national minimum for the first time.
  • A single wage base. §2(y) ends the definitional chaos, with the 50% add-back preventing artificial restructuring of pay.
  • Enforcement redesign. Web-based, randomised inspection plus the compliance-first duty under §54(3) reduces both corruption and unnecessary prosecution.
  • Effective remedies. Compensation up to ten times the claim, a three-year limitation, single applications for groups, recovery as land revenue and an appeal — the strongest claim regime in Indian wage law.

Criticisms

  • The floor is still unseen. Four years after assent, and nearly a year after commencement, the §9 floor wage has not been notified; the binding baseline remains a promise.
  • Prescribed norms, deferred content. The manner of calculation, the norms of fixation and the floor-wage methodology are all left to rules or orders — flexibility that can become delay.
  • Fifty per cent may be too low to be real. The add-back protects the base only above the half-way mark; employers can still structure pay to keep “wages” near 50% of cost, and the consequence for gratuity and PF arises mainly where allowances dominate.
  • Facilitator vs enforcer. A single officer asked both to advise and to prosecute may, in practice, be captured by the employers he must inspect — the classic critique of amalgamating the two roles.
  • Residual fragmentation. “Wages” continues to mean something different under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 (for retrenchment compensation) and the Employees’ Compensation Act, 1923 — the dream of one definition across labour law is only half-realised.
A closing formulation for the statutory limb

The Code on Wages, 2019 does three things at once. It compresses four statutes into one with a single definition of wages; it expands the reach of the minimum wage from scheduled employments to every employee, and adds a statutory floor wage below which no State may fix a rate; and it replaces the inspector with a facilitator, shifting the first question from “whom shall we prosecute?” to “how shall we make this establishment compliant?”. What it does not do is answer the question the Supreme Court has been asking since 1958: what a minimum wage must actually contain. That answer still comes from the 1957 norms as enlarged by Reptakos Brett — and, where the wage falls below the notified minimum, from Article 23 as explained in PUDR.

The two constitutional lessons to memorise
  1. Article 19(6) answers the employer’s 1948 objection. The freedom to carry on business does not include the freedom to buy labour below the socially determined floor; wage regulation is a permissible and, in developing countries, necessary public-interest restriction.

G.2 The 1957 norms — the “need-based” spine of Indian wage law

The five norms — 15th session of the Indian Labour Conference (July 1957)

The Conference accepted, for the purpose of determining the minimum wage, that the standard working-class family should be assumed to consist of three consumption units for one earner, and that the wage should be computed on five components:
(i) Food: the minimum food requirement of 2,700 calories per adult per day — the standard worked out by Dr W.R. Aykroyd, the Nutrition Adviser to the Government of India, for the initial nutritional requirements of an Indian adult engaged in moderate work;
(ii) Clothing: 18 yards per person per annum — expressed in the norm as 72 yards per annum for the standard family of four;
(iii) Housing: rent corresponding to the minimum area provided under the Government’s Industrial Housing Scheme;
(iv) Fuel, lighting and other miscellaneous items of expenditure: to constitute 20% of the total minimum wage;
(v) the family is to be taken as consisting of three consumption units for one earner, the earnings of women, children and adolescents being disregarded.

The norms were endorsed by the Supreme Court in Standard Vacuum Refining Co. of India v. Workmen, AIR 1961 SC 895 — where the question was whether a minimum wage of ₹50 to ₹55 a month could have been the correct figure in 1940 — and again in Reptakos Brett, which added the sixth component: children’s education, medical requirement, minimum recreation including festivals and ceremonies, and provision for old age, marriage etc., fixed at 25% of the minimum wage.

G.3 The statutes the Code replaced — and what survives of them

Minimum Wages Act, 1948 (repealed by §69)

  • §3: the appropriate Government to fix minimum rates of wages in the scheduled employments listed in the Schedule (and in Part II, in respect of which rates may be fixed for the first time by the Central Government).
  • §4: the components — basic rate with a cost-of-living allowance; or basic rate with or without DA plus the cash value of concessions; or an all-inclusive rate. Substantially reproduced in §7 of the Code.
  • §5: the procedure — committee method or notification method with two months’ notice. Reproduced in §8 of the Code.
  • §12: payment of minimum rates — mirror of §5 of the Code.
  • §13: fixing hours for a normal working day, the weekly rest day and payment for work on a rest day.
  • §14: overtime at twice the ordinary rate — now §14 of the Code.
  • §22C–22F (inserted 2005): the “competent authority” and the requirement of a written application by the employee, a hearing, and a written order with reasons before the employer may deduct — the ancestor of the Code’s §21 safeguards.
  • §20: claims — within six months (extendable), compensation up to ten times the claim. Expanded in §45 of the Code to three years and a single application for any number of employees.
  • Why the repeal matters: the employment Schedule disappears; every employee in every establishment now has a statutory wage floor. The Supreme Court’s old decisions under the 1948 Act — including all four prescribed cases except PUDR’s Article 23 holding — continue to be cited for their reasoning, because the concepts (minimum/fair/living, capacity to pay, industry-cum-region) are carried forward by the Code.

Payment of Wages Act, 1936; Payment of Bonus Act, 1965; Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 (repealed)

  • Payment of Wages Act, 1936: applied to factories, railways, mines and other notified establishments, with a wage ceiling; §4 (wage periods), §5 (time of payment), §7 (deductions), §8 (fines), §15 (claims with compensation up to ten times). The Code universalises the coverage, adds electronic payment, tightens the time limits and keeps the deduction architecture — including the 50% aggregate ceiling and the 3% fine cap.
  • Payment of Bonus Act, 1965: statutory minimum bonus 8⅓% and maximum 20% of “salary or wage”, with the eligibility ceiling notified at ₹21,000 a month and the calculation ceiling at ₹7,000 a month (or the minimum wage, if higher) after the 2015 amendment; disqualifications for fraud, violent behaviour, theft and sexual harassment. Chapter IV of the Code (§§26–41) carries the same architecture. Note the arithmetic: 8⅓% is one-twelfth of a month’s wage, i.e. a “thirteenth-month” payment floor; and the Code fixes ₹100 or 8⅓%, whichever is higher, as the floor for a wage-earner at the bottom.
  • Equal Remuneration Act, 1976: §4 (duty to pay equal remuneration for the same work or work of a similar nature), §5 (no discrimination in recruitment), §6 (duty to maintain a register), with the “same work or work of a similar nature” test defined in §2(h). The Code compresses this into a single §3, extending the prohibition to any discrimination on the ground of gender in recruitment and conditions of service, and pulling HRA, conveyance, award/settlement amounts and overtime into the wage computation for equal-pay purposes through the second proviso to §2(y).

G.6 Three wage-protection instruments beyond the Code

1. Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976

The statutory counterpart of Article 23. It abolishes every bonded labour system, extinguishes the bonded debt and discharges the bonded labourer from the obligation to repay it, and sets up vigilance committees at the district and sub-divisional level. Its connection with PUDR is direct: the Supreme Court in Bandhua Mukti Morcha read Article 23 with Article 21 and this Act to hold that a workman who is compelled to work for a pittance — even without physical coercion — is a bonded labourer entitled to release and rehabilitation. In a wage answer, the Act supplies the criminal-law arm of the same prohibition on which PUDR rests.

2. The MGNREGA, 2005 — the wage floor as an entitlement

The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 gives a household a legal guarantee of at least one hundred days of unskilled manual work in a financial year, with wages payable at the minimum wage notified for agricultural labourers, payment ordinarily within fifteen days, and compensation for delay where payment is delayed (at a rate prescribed by the Central Government). Unemployment allowance is payable when work is not provided. It is the clearest Indian example of a wage floor being administered as a right rather than as a regulatory requirement — the practical meaning that PUDR gave to Article 23 in the relief-phase of the case.

3. ILO wage instruments

Two conventions frame the international standard. Convention No. 95 (Protection of Wages, 1949) requires wages to be paid in legal tender at regular intervals, prohibits deductions except under conditions prescribed by law, and restricts payment in kind — the ancestor of the §2(y) Explanation limiting in-kind wages to 15%. Convention No. 131 (Minimum Wage Fixing, 1970) lists the elements to be taken into account in determining minimum wage levels: the needs of workers and their families (with reference to the general level of wages in the country, the cost of living, social security benefits and comparative living standards of other social groups) and economic factors including the requirements of economic development, levels of productivity and the desirability of attaining and maintaining a high level of employment. The 1957 Indian norms and §6(6) of the Code on Wages are in substance an application of this Convention. India’s ratification status under each convention should be checked before it is asserted in the examination hall.

Where the platform economy fits — a word to add and a caution

The Code on Social Security, 2020 recognises gig workers, platform workers and unorganised workers for the first time, provides for their registration and welfare benefits financed in part by contributions from aggregators (commonly cited at 1–2% of annual turnover, subject to a ceiling of 5% of the amounts paid or payable to gig and platform workers), and leaves the exact rate to rules. What the Code on Wages does not do is settle who is the “employer” of a platform worker, and therefore whether a minimum wage applies at all. The honest position in an examination is this: the Code’s definition of “employee” is broad enough to cover anyone employed for remuneration, but the classification of platform work as employment or as independent contracting is an open question, and the wage floor remains formally real while practically contested. Say that, rather than claiming either that gig workers are covered or that they are excluded.

How the pieces fit — the one-paragraph integration

Article 43 sets the destination (living wage); the 1957 norms and Reptakos Brett give the minimum wage its content; Crown Aluminium and Hydro (Engineers) protect that content against downward revision; Greaves Cotton and its companion cases carry the analysis upward, from the minimum to the fair wage; PUDR converts the floor into a fundamental right; and the Code on Wages, 2019 supplies the statutory machinery — universal coverage, a national floor wage, a single definition of wages and a claims regime with compensation up to ten times the shortfall. A complete Unit 7 answer is, in essence, that chain: constitutional standard → normative content → protection against reduction → fair-wage extension → fundamental-right enforcement → statutory machinery.

Model answer 1 — December 2024, Q. 8(a): “Discuss People’s Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (AIR 1982 SC 1473).”

15 marks · suggested length 900–1,100 words · Bhagwati, J. · Article 23 · Minimum Wages Act, 1948
Introduction

Before 1982, a wage below the notified minimum was a breach of the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, remediable in the ordinary way — through the inspectorate, claims under §20 and prosecution. In People’s Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India, AIR 1982 SC 1473, the Supreme Court holding the field through Bhagwati, J. converted that statutory breach into a violation of a fundamental right. The decision is the constitutional capstone of Indian wage law: it makes the minimum wage enforceable under Article 32, against private employers, at the instance of any public-spirited person, and it locates the source of that enforceability in Article 23’s prohibition of begar and other similar forms of forced labour.

Facts in brief

PUDR, acting on a report prepared by three social scientists who had investigated the conditions of workmen on the various construction projects connected with the Asian Games, 1982 in Delhi, wrote to the Court. The letter was treated as a writ petition. The works — flyovers, stadia, swimming pools, the Games village — were executed by contractors engaged by the Delhi Development Authority, the Delhi Administration and the New Delhi Municipal Committee, who were registered as principal employers under §7 of the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970. The workmen, brought from Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and Orissa by jamadars, were entitled to a minimum wage of ₹9.25 a day; the jamadars deducted ₹1 per day as commission, leaving ₹8.25. Women workers were paid ₹7 a day in breach of the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976; children below fourteen were employed; and the Contract Labour Act and the Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act, 1979 were not complied with. The Union of India candidly admitted in its affidavit that ₹1 per worker per day was being deducted from the wages payable.

Preliminary objections

Two objections were raised: (i) that PUDR had no locus standi, since the rights violated were the workers’ and not its own; and (ii) that no writ lay under Article 32, because what was alleged was breach of labour statutes and not of fundamental rights, and because the workmen were employees of the contractors, not of the respondents.

The decision
  1. Locus standi: the traditional rule has been jettisoned; where the persons whose rights are violated cannot approach the Court because of poverty, disability or social and economic disadvantage, any member of the public acting bona fide may move the Court — and may do so by letter.
  2. Article 32 was attracted through four routes: Article 24 (child labour); Article 14 (non-observance of the Equal Remuneration Act is “in effect and substance” a denial of equality); Article 21 (the Contract Labour Act and the Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act confer rights and benefits intended to ensure basic human dignity, and depriving workmen of them violates Article 21 as expanded in Maneka Gandhi and Francis Coralie Mullin); and Article 23.
  3. The core holding on Article 23: “Where a person provides labour or service to another for remuneration which is less than the minimum wage, the labour or service provided by him clearly falls within the scope and ambit of the words ‘forced labour’ under Article 23.” The Court reasoned that no one would ordinarily supply labour or service for less than the wage the law guarantees, and that the extra words “or other similar forms of forced labour” would become “a mere rope of sand” if they were confined to labour exacted without remuneration. “Force” was read to include compulsion arising from hunger and poverty, want and destitution — any factor that deprives a person of a choice of alternatives and compels a particular course of action. The Court drew on Bailey v. Alabama, 219 US 219, the ILO Forced Labour Conventions of 1930 and 1957, and Article 4 of the European Convention on Human Rights.
  4. Principal employers’ liability: §20 of the Contract Labour Act places the obligation to provide amenities on the principal employer where the contractor fails, and §§17–18 of the Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act make the principal employer liable to pay wages and allowances. The authorities “cannot fold their hands in despair and become silent spectators of the breach of a constitutional prohibition”.
  5. Relief: wages to be paid directly to the workmen without jamadar intervention and without deduction of commission; legal action by way of prosecution or recovery where shortfalls are found; a clause in future government contracts to that effect; and the appointment of three Ombudsmen to inspect the sites and report to the Court.
Significance and critical appreciation
  • It converts the wage floor into a right. Non-payment below the minimum is no longer merely a statutory default; it is a constitutional injury, and the Court’s writ jurisdiction is available even against private employers, because Article 23 (like Articles 17 and 24) binds the whole world and not merely the State.
  • It places a positive duty on the State. Wherever a fundamental right enforceable against private persons is being violated, the State must interdict the violation — here, by an effective system of inspection, occasional surprise inspections and prompt prosecution.
  • Its limits must be stated precisely. The holding is confined to payment below the statutory minimum wage. Payment below a fair or living wage does not attract Article 23, and an employer who pays at least the minimum commits no constitutional breach, however low the floor may be.
  • Its afterlife. The reasoning was applied in Sanjit Roy v. State of Rajasthan, (1983) 1 SCC 525 (famine relief workers paid below the minimum) and extended in Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India, (1984) 3 SCC 161, where Article 23 was read with Article 21 against bonded labour. The Asiad fact pattern — principal employer, contractor, labour intermediary, migrant workman, wage shortfall — is now regulated by the Building and Other Construction Workers Act, 1996 and, in wage terms, by Chapter II of the Code on Wages, 2019.
  • Harmonisation with International Human Rights (UDHR). Bhagwati J.’s judgment achieved an authoritative synchronisation between the Indian Constitution and international human rights law. By interpreting Article 23 through the prism of UDHR Article 4 (prohibition of servitude) and UDHR Article 23(3) (remuneration ensuring an existence worthy of human dignity), the Court established that international declarations are vital interpretive engines that expand the substantive reach of domestic Fundamental Rights under Articles 21 and 23.
  • A common criticism: PUDR sets an admirable standard but depends on an enforcement machinery that has historically been thin; the Court itself had to appoint Ombudsmen because the statutory inspectorate had failed. The same criticism now attaches to the Code’s Inspector-cum-Facilitator — the enforcement design, not the norm, is the weak link.
Conclusion

PUDR is the point at which wage law ceases to be a branch of commercial regulation and becomes a branch of constitutional law. Read with the Code on Wages, 2019, it explains why the floor wage under §9 — even before it is notified — must be understood as the statutory expression of a constitutional guarantee: a wage below the legal minimum is not merely underpayment; it is the extraction of forced labour.

Model answer 2 — December 2024, Q. 8(b): “Discuss the fixation of fair wages by the industrial adjudicator.”

15 marks · suggested length 900–1,100 words · Fair Wages Committee, 1948 · Greaves Cotton · capacity to pay
Introduction — why the adjudicator fixes wages at all

In India wages were historically determined by collective bargaining or by industrial adjudication, because there was no comprehensive wage-fixation statute until the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, and even that Act reached only “scheduled employments”. Industrial tribunals therefore became, in effect, wage-fixing bodies for the organised sector — a development the Supreme Court accepted in Crown Aluminium Works v. Workmen, AIR 1958 SC 130, while cautioning that the tribunal’s power is not one of “wish-fulfilment” but a “sober and dispassionate” exercise guided by the requirements of social justice on the one hand and the financial capacity of the industry on the other.

The three-tier framework the adjudicator works within

The Committee on Fair Wages (1948) distinguished three concepts. The living wage (Article 43) represents frugal comfort — food, clothing and shelter, plus education, health, social needs and provision against old age. The minimum wage provides not merely for bare sustenance but for the preservation of the worker’s efficiency, and must be paid irrespective of the employer’s capacity to pay. The fair wage lies between the two: it is above the minimum and below the living wage, and it takes into account the capacity of the industry to pay, the prevailing rates of wages in the same or similar industries in the region, the productivity of labour, the level of national income and its distribution, and the place of the industry in the economy. It is in this middle band that the adjudicator does his real work.

The formula the adjudicator applies — industry-cum-region

The governing formula is Greaves Cotton & Co. Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1964 SC 689. The Supreme Court held that the fair wage must be determined on an industry-cum-region basis — that is, by comparing the wage packet of the employer’s workmen with the wage packet in comparable concerns both within the same industry and in the same region — and that the question whether the “industry aspect” or the “region aspect” predominates depends on the density of comparable units. Where there are many comparable concerns, the industry aspect predominates and the wages they pay become the standard (Hindusthan Motors Ltd. v. Workmen). Where the employer is the sole or almost the sole concern of its kind in the region, the region aspect predominates and the adjudicator must look to the general level of wages in the region (French Motor Car Co. v. Workmen, AIR 1962 SC 1323). The comparison must be of the total wage packet — basic wage plus dearness allowance and other allowances — and not of one component in isolation; and the adjudicator should seek a “fair” and not an “identical” comparison, because the capricious inclusion of unsuitable units would distort the result.

The two constraints on the adjudicator’s power
  1. The floor is absolute. The adjudicator cannot fix or approve a wage below the minimum; and, as Unichoyi v. State of Kerala, AIR 1962 SC 12 lays down, the employer’s inability to pay is irrelevant to the minimum. Nor may a wage structure be revised downwards so as to prejudice workmen who are at or near the minimum (Crown Aluminium; Hydro (Engineers) (P) Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1969 SC 182).
  2. Above the floor, capacity matters. Once the wage is above the minimum, the adjudicator must consider the employer’s financial capacity; a reduction may be justified by proved inability to pay (Mill Owners’ Association, Bombay v. Textile Labour Association, AIR 1966 SC 497). But the burden of establishing incapacity lies on the employer, and it must be shown that the wage is “well above” the minimum before capacity becomes a decisive factor (Reptakos Brett).
How the adjudicator actually proceeds — the practical sequence
  1. Determine the applicable statutory minimum (and, after the Code on Wages, 2019, the notified minimum under §6 read with the §9 floor wage).
  2. Fix the correct comparable set: same industry and same region; exclude units that are dissimilar in size, plant or product unless no better comparison exists.
  3. Compare total wage packets — basic, DA and allowances — and not basic alone.
  4. Assess capacity to pay on the material placed on record, remembering that a wage above the floor can be trimmed only on proved incapacity and that the workmen cannot be made to bear a loss they did not cause.
  5. Do justice to both sides: the award must be “fair and just” to the employer as well as to the employees, must be of a standard “which the industry can bear”, and must not create a spiral that threatens the industry’s survival (Crown Aluminium).
The current setting

After the Code on Wages, 2019 (in force from 21 November 2025), statutory wage fixation is done by the appropriate Government under §§6–8 within the ceiling set by the §9 floor wage, and adjudication of wage disputes — including claims for less payment — moves to the claims authority under §45, with an appeal under §49. But wage fixation through adjudication has not disappeared: disputes over wage structures, revisions, dearness allowance and arrears arising out of awards and settlements remain industrial disputes, and the industry-cum-region formula continues to govern the tribunal’s approach. The Supreme Court’s own observation in Greaves Cotton — that the formula is one of “fair comparison”, not of mechanical equation — is the practical heart of the answer.

Conclusion

The industrial adjudicator is, in substance, a wage-fixer operating between two fixed points: an unyielding floor below which no consideration of capacity can push him, and the living wage above, which remains a constitutional aspiration. Within that band his tool is the industry-cum-region comparison, his governing test is the density of comparable units, and his duty is to balance the worker’s need for a wage that supports efficiency and dignity against the employer’s capacity to sustain the industry.

Model answer 3 — May–June 2025, Q. 7 (High difficulty): “Explain the concept of minimum, fair and living wages with reference to Crown Aluminium Works v. Workmen.”

15 marks · suggested length 1,000–1,200 words · Part A + Case 24
Introduction

Wages are the point at which the two great claims of industrial law — the employer’s claim to a viable enterprise and the worker’s claim to a life of human dignity — meet and are reconciled. Indian law reconciles them through a three-tier hierarchy of wage concepts derived from the report of the Committee on Fair Wages (1948) and constitutionalised by Article 43 (living wage), Article 39(a) (adequate means of livelihood) and Article 23 (forced labour), and applied in a line of decisions of which Crown Aluminium Works v. Workmen, AIR 1958 SC 130, is the starting point.

1. The three tiers

2. The 1957 norms and the sixth component

The 15th session of the Indian Labour Conference (1957) accepted five norms for a need-based minimum wage: three consumption units for one earner; 2,700 calories of food per adult per day (Dr Aykroyd’s standard); 18 yards of cloth per person per annum (72 yards for the standard family of four); rent corresponding to the minimum housing scheme area; and 20% of the total minimum wage for fuel, lighting and miscellaneous items. In The Workmen v. Reptakos Brett & Co. Ltd., AIR 1992 SC 504, the Supreme Court held that this 1957 formula, being “a quarter century old”, no longer reflected “the changed socio-economic condition of the country”, and added a sixth component — children’s education, medical requirement, minimum recreation including festivals and ceremonies, and provision for old age, marriage, etc. — at 25% of the minimum wage. The Court treated the burden of proving that the workmen were paid well above the minimum, and that the employer lacked the capacity to bear the additional burden, as lying on the employer.

3. Crown Aluminium Works v. Workmen — the facts and holding

The employer, a comparatively new undertaking, claimed that it could not bear the wage burden and sought a review of the wage scales fixed by the earlier award. A proceeding before the 6th Industrial Tribunal (West Bengal) and an arbitration by Mr Palit had preceded the reference; 52 workmen had been retrenched earlier and another 227 were sought to be retrenched during the proceeding. The Supreme Court, through Gajendragadkar, J., laid down the principles that govern both the fixation and the revision of a wage structure:

  • No industry has a right to exist unless it can pay its workmen at least a bare minimum wage; but equally, the tribunal is not bound to fix a wage that a full-fed economy could support — the wage must be such that the employer can bear it, and disputes must be settled by “a fair and just decision”.
  • A wage structure cannot be revised to the prejudice of workmen who are paid the bare minimum wage. The minimum is a floor of social interest: it does not depend on the profitability of the particular undertaking.
  • Above the minimum, revision is possible on merits; the Court identified the relevant considerations — whether retrenchment had already been effected, the duration of the undertaking’s life, and the need to maintain industrial peace — as the guide to how far a concession could be withdrawn or a reduction justified.
  • Concessions of long standing form part of the wage structure and are not a bounty that the employer can summarily withdraw; where they have been enjoyed for years, the onus lies heavily on the employer to justify their withdrawal.
4. The safeguard principle continued

Crown Aluminium was carried forward in Hydro (Engineers) (P) Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1969 SC 182, where a wage structure was held incapable of being revised downwards, even with the consent of the workmen, where they were at or near the minimum wage and the undertaking was running at a loss — capacity to pay is irrelevant at the floor. The same principle now finds statutory expression in §9(2) of the Code on Wages, 2019: a State may not fix a minimum rate below the floor wage, and may not reduce a minimum rate that is higher than the floor. The judicial principle and the statutory rule are the same rule.

5. Application and conclusion

Applied to a problem: first, ascertain the notified minimum wage for the relevant skill category and area (and, once notified, the §9 floor wage); if the workmen are paid at or near that level, no consideration of the employer’s losses, over-neutralisation of dearness allowance or falling demand can justify a reduction — the answer is that the wage structure cannot be revised to the prejudice of the workmen. If the workmen are paid well above the minimum, the employer may seek a revision, but must plead and prove incapacity to pay, and the tribunal must weigh need, capacity, comparable wage packets and industrial peace. What the employer can never say is: “we cannot pay even the minimum.” That proposition — the substance of the tiered framework, of the 1957 norms, of Crown Aluminium, Unichoyi and Reptakos Brett, and of the Code on Wages — is the constant of Indian wage law.

Model answer 4 — May–June 2025, Q. 8(b) and November–December 2025, Q. 8(a): Short note on the industry-cum-region formula

5–7 marks · suggested length 300–400 words · Greaves Cotton, AIR 1964 SC 689
The note

Fair wages — wages above the statutory minimum but below the living wage — are fixed on the industry-cum-region principle laid down in Greaves Cotton & Co. Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1964 SC 689. The phrase, borrowed from the report of the Committee on Fair Wages (1948), requires the wage of a concern’s workmen to be compared with the wages paid in comparable concerns in the same industry and in the same region. It has two aspects, and the question which predominates is answered by the density of comparable units: where there are many comparable concerns of similar standing, the industry aspect predominates and their wage packets set the standard (Hindusthan Motors Ltd. v. Workmen); where the employer is the sole or almost sole concern of its kind in the region, the region aspect predominates and the adjudicator must look to the general level of wages in the region (French Motor Car Co. v. Workmen, AIR 1962 SC 1323).

Three practical rules follow. First, the comparison is of the total wage packet — and the whole of it, that is, basic wage, dearness allowance and other allowances — not of the basic wage alone, since a comparison of one component alone can be misleading. Second, the comparison must be with genuinely comparable concerns; the object is a fair comparison, not an identical one, and the inclusion of unsuitable or capricious units would defeat the exercise. Third, the formula is not a mechanical equation but an instrument of social justice: it is applied alongside the minimum wage, which no reduction may touch, and alongside capacity to pay, which becomes relevant only above the floor and only on proof. The formula therefore does the work of reconciling the workman’s need for a wage that supports his efficiency and dignity with the employer’s claim that the industry must survive — the balance the Supreme Court described in Crown Aluminium Works as “a fair and just decision” between the two parties.

UDHR, 1948 (Arts. 4, 23, 24, 25) & Article 43 (living wage) set the international and constitutional destination: remuneration ensuring an existence worthy of human dignity → the Committee on Fair Wages, 1948 splits the journey into three tiers (minimum / fair / living) → the 15th Indian Labour Conference, 1957 gives the minimum wage its five norms (3 consumption units; 2,700 calories; 18 yards of cloth; housing-scheme rent; 20% fuel and miscellaneous) mirroring UDHR Art. 25(1) → Crown Aluminium (1958) protects the floor against downward revision → Greaves Cotton (1964) builds the fair wage on the industry-cum-region formula → Reptakos Brett (1992) adds the sixth component at 25% and shifts the burden of proof to the employer → PUDR (1982) reads UDHR Arts. 4 & 23 into Articles 21, 23 and 32, making the wage floor a fundamental right → the Code on Wages, 2019 (in force 21 Nov 2025) gives it statutory machinery: one definition of wages (§2(y)), universal minimum wage coverage (§§5–6), a national floor wage (§9), gender wage equality (§3 mirroring UDHR Art. 23(2)), disciplined mode and time of payment (§§15–17), and an Inspector-cum-Facilitator (§51).

I.2 Mnemonics and memory hooks

For the 1957 norms — “3–27–18–20–R”

3 consumption units · 2700 calories · 18 yards of cloth · 20% fuel and miscellaneous · Rent under the Industrial Housing Scheme. Then add the sixth: Education, Medical, Recreation, Old age and marriage = 25% → “3–27–18–20–R + EMRO 25”.

For the three tiers — “FLOOR, FARE, FLOURISH”

Floor = minimum wage, absolute, capacity irrelevant. Fare = fair wage, industry-cum-region, capacity relevant. Flourish = living wage, Article 43, the aspiration.

For the density test — “many = industry, few = region”

Many comparable concerns → the industry aspect prevails (Hindusthan Motors). Few or none → the region aspect prevails (French Motor Car). And always compare the total wage packet.

For §17 deadlines — “Shift, Holiday, Second, Seventh, Two”

Daily = end of shift. Weekly = before the weekly holiday. Fortnightly = before the end of the second day. Monthly = before the seventh day. Exit = within two working days.

I.3 Glossary for Unit 7

TermMeaning to be used in the paper
Wage / remunerationWage is the price of labour. “Wages” in the narrow statutory sense is defined by §2(y); “remuneration” is the wider economic concept that includes everything the employee receives.
Basic wageThe rate fixed for the job or the class of work, exclusive of allowances; the anchor of the wage packet, and the base that the 50% add-back in §2(y) protects.
Dearness allowance (DA)An allowance payable to compensate for the erosion of real wages by the rise in the cost of living; it is part of the statutory minimum wage under §7(1)(a).
NeutralisationThe extent to which DA compensates for price rise — full (100%) where DA rises exactly as prices do, over-neutralised where it exceeds the price rise, under-neutralised where it lags. Reptakos Brett shows why a percentage of an unrealistic base makes the neutralisation figure meaningless.
Cost of living indexThe consumer price index for industrial workers used to compute DA; its revisions are the standard trigger for wage adjustment.
Consumption unitA scale for converting the family’s members into the equivalent of adult earners; the 1957 norm takes one earner to support three consumption units.
Need-based minimum wageThe wage computed from the 1957 norms, i.e. from the family’s minimum needs — the sense in which the Indian minimum wage is a “need-based” rather than a purely market wage.
Capacity to payThe employer’s/industry’s ability to bear a wage cost. Irrelevant at the minimum wage (Unichoyi); relevant above it, but as a burden of proof on the employer (Reptakos; Ahmedabad Mills).
Wage structure / wage differentialsThe whole set of rates, scales, allowances and increments for the categories of work in an undertaking; a revision that disturbs relativities between categories is as important as a change in the total.
Wage boardA tripartite body (for example under the Working Journalists Act, 1955) that fixes wages for an industry as a whole, outside the minimum-wage machinery.
Statutory minimum vs fair wage vs living wageStatutory minimum = the legal floor (Code, Chapter II); fair wage = the adjudicated norm above it; living wage = the constitutional goal of Article 43.

I.4 Common errors that cost marks

Errors of law

  • Writing that the minimum wage can be reduced because the industry is making losses. It cannot — capacity to pay is irrelevant at the floor (Unichoyi).
  • Treating the fair wage as if it were the minimum, or the living wage as a statutorily payable rate. Only two of the three tiers have legal bite: the statutory minimum (and the floor wage) bind; the fair wage binds the adjudicator; the living wage binds the State as a directive.
  • Saying that PUDR makes payment below a fair wage forced labour. The holding is confined to the statutory minimum.
  • Describing Article 23 as available only against the State. It binds private persons as well.
  • Reciting the 1957 norms without the sixth component, or the sixth component without the 25% figure.

Errors of accuracy

  • Quoting the obsolete “scheduled employments” limitation of the Minimum Wages Act as if it survived the Code.
  • Omitting the 50% add-back in §2(y), the 15% in-kind limit, or the second proviso to §2(y) — these are exactly the parts of the definition that examiners test.
  • Stating that the statutory floor wage has been notified. It has not (as on 28 September 2026); the advisory National Floor Level Minimum Wage is a different thing.
  • Giving the wrong time limit under §17 (seventh day of the succeeding month for monthly wages; two working days on exit).
  • Citing Greaves Cotton as AIR 1964 SC 639 without noting the discrepancy — the casebook gives 639 at one place and 689 elsewhere, and 689 is the correct citation.
  • Confusing the industry-cum-region formula with the density test: the formula is the principle; the density test is the tie-breaker between its two aspects.

Last-minute revision — six sentences that carry the unit
  1. The minimum wage is an absolute floor — depending not on the employer’s capacity but on the worker’s need, computed on the 1957 norms plus the 25% sixth component of Reptakos Brett.
  2. The fair wage lies between minimum and living wage and is fixed on the industry-cum-region formula, with the density test deciding which aspect predominates.
  3. Crown Aluminium forbids any revision to the prejudice of workmen at the minimum; above the floor, revision is a question of merits and of proved capacity to pay.
  4. PUDR converts payment below the minimum into forced labour under Article 23 — enforceable against private employers under Article 32, at the instance of a public-spirited person.
  5. The Code on Wages, 2019 compresses four statutes into one, defines wages in §2(y) with a 50% add-back, makes minimum wage coverage universal, creates a floor wage under §9 that no State minimum may fall below, and disciplines the mode, time and deductions of payment in Chapter III.
  6. The Inspector-cum-Facilitator under §51 — advising before prosecuting, inspecting by web-based and randomised selection — is the Code’s answer to the enforcement failure that PUDR itself exposed.

J.2 Verification notes — what to double-check before you write

Points of care
  1. Citation of Greaves Cotton. The syllabus image gives AIR 1964 SC 639; the casebook gives 689 elsewhere and Mendiratta gives 689. The commonly accepted citation is AIR 1964 SC 689. In an examination, write 689 and add a parenthetical note that some course materials print 639. Do not silently “correct” the question — acknowledge the discrepancy.
  2. The floor wage. Verify the position before you write. As on 28 September 2026 the statutory floor wage under §9 has not been notified. Do not confuse it with the advisory National Floor Level Minimum Wage (₹176 a day from 2017; commonly cited as ₹178 a day after 2019), which is neither statutory nor binding.
  3. Numbers in the Code. The penalty amounts in §54 (up to ₹50,000; repeat up to three months’ imprisonment or ₹1,00,000; other contraventions up to ₹20,000; records up to ₹10,000), the 50% deduction ceiling, the 3% fine ceiling, the three-month decision target and the three-year limitation in §45, and the 90-day appeal period in §49 are figures that examiners ask for — quote them exactly.
  4. Chapter numbering. The Code has nine chapters (the arrangement of sections labels the last one “Chapter X”) — a drafting slip. Students who write “Chapter IX” or “Chapter X” for the miscellaneous provisions are both, strictly, defensible; the safer course is to cite the sections, not the chapter.
  5. Historical figures in the cases. ₹9.25 a day (1982, PUDR), ₹26 and ₹50–55 (1940, Reptakos Brett discussing Standard Vacuum), and the retrenchment figures in Crown Aluminium (52 already retrenched and 227 proposed) are historical facts. Quote them as facts of the case and never as current rates.
  6. Wage ceilings elsewhere. The provident fund wage ceiling was raised from ₹15,000 to ₹25,000 a month with effect from 17 September 2026; the Employees’ State Insurance ceiling is ₹21,000 (₹25,000 for persons with disability); the bonus eligibility ceiling is ₹21,000 a month with a calculation ceiling of ₹7,000. These ceilings change by notification — check the current figure before relying on any of them.
  7. Where the Code text was read from. The official document hosts for the Code on Wages PDF were unavailable when this file was prepared, and the text was therefore read from a complete published text of the Act and cross-checked against a second source for §9. If you are quoting a provision in an examination, quote from your own statute book rather than from the web.

J.3 How to keep this file current

Two notifications to watch

  1. The floor wage under §9 — once notified it becomes the operative national (or area-wise) baseline, and the first thing to be added to Part F.4.
  2. The norms of fixation under §6(6)(c) and the manner of calculating the floor wage under the Rules — these prescribe the methodology, and the criteria reported in the 2025–26 draft (three consumption units; 2,700 calories; rent 10%; fuel and electricity 20%; education, medical and recreation 25%) will become part of the statutory scheme once formally adopted.

What will not change

The case law. Crown Aluminium, Greaves Cotton, Reptakos Brett and PUDR remain the analytical core of the unit: the tiers, the industry-cum-region formula and the density test, the sixth component and the burden of proof, and Article 23’s prohibition of sub-minimum labour. The Code has not displaced any of them; it has given them statutory machinery and a floor.

✱A closing note — why this unit is worth the trouble

Unit 7 looks, on the syllabus, like a small statutory unit with two concepts and four cases. It is in fact the unit in which Indian labour law shows its foundations most clearly. Every other unit of this course assumes that the worker has something to bargain with — a job, a union, a forum, a procedure. Unit 7 asks the prior question: what is the worker entitled to be paid before he bargains at all? The answer the law gives has three layers, and each layer was built by a court that refused to let the market decide.

The first layer is the minimum wage. It is not a market wage and not a subsistence ration: it is a need-based figure computed on the 1957 norms — three consumption units, 2,700 calories, eighteen yards of cloth, housing-scheme rent and a 20% margin for fuel and the rest — enlarged in 1992 by the sixth component of education, medical care, recreation, old age and marriage, fixed at 25% of the wage. Its defining feature is not its amount but its independence from the employer’s capacity to pay: no industry has a right to exist, the Court said in 1958, unless it can pay a bare minimum wage.

The second layer is the protection of what has already been fixed. Crown Aluminium holds that a wage structure cannot be revised to the prejudice of workmen at the floor, that long-standing concessions are part of the wage structure and not a bounty, and that above the floor a reduction must be justified on merits and on proved incapacity. The same rule now appears as a statute: §9(2) of the Code on Wages forbids any minimum rate below the floor wage and forbids the reduction of a rate that is higher. Doctrine became legislation without changing its content.

The third layer is constitutional. In PUDR, the Supreme Court held that labour rendered for less than the minimum wage is forced labour under Article 23 — enforceable against private employers, at the instance of a stranger to the employment, and remediable under Article 32. From that point the wage floor ceased to be a matter of labour administration and became a matter of human dignity. It also explains the design of the statutory remedies we now have: compensation up to ten times the shortfall, a three-year limitation, a single application for any number of workmen, recovery as arrears of land revenue, and the burden of proof placed on the employer.

What remains, and what a good answer should acknowledge, is the gap between the floor and the ceiling. The fair wage — determined by the industry-cum-region formula, with the density of comparable concerns deciding whether industry or region predominates — is where most organised-sector disputes are actually fought. The living wage of Article 43, with its promise of frugal comfort, remains an aspiration that no Indian statute yet delivers, and the one instrument designed to close the gap between the States — the floor wage under §9 of the Code on Wages — is in force in the statute but still awaiting notification. A student who can state that position clearly — the doctrine firm, the machinery new, the enforcement uncertain, and the constitutional right settled — has understood the unit better than one who has merely memorised the five norms.

The single sentence to carry into the examination hall

A minimum wage in Indian law is not the lowest price at which labour can be bought; it is the price at which labour can be bought without the buyer committing forced labour — which is why it does not depend on the employer’s capacity to pay, why it cannot be revised downward, and why, since PUDR, its breach is a violation of Article 23.
