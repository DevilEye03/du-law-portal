PART I · 1. Unit 2 in the scheme of the Code — from "reference" to "application"

Under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 the reference was the hinge of the whole adjudication system. No Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal could touch a dispute unless the appropriate Government, by an order in writing under IDA s.10(1) (or s.10(1A) for a National Tribunal, or s.12(5) after a conciliation failure report), referred it. Three consequences followed, and all three are the subject-matter of Unit 2:

  1. The nature and scope of the referring power — is it administrative or judicial? can the Government refuse? on what grounds? can a court compel a reference? (Telco Convoy, Sharad Kumar; and for the National Tribunal, FDC).
  2. The jurisdiction of the adjudicating authority — the Tribunal is a creature of the statute and of the reference; it "acquires jurisdiction on the basis of reference made to it" (TISCO, ¶18) and must confine itself to "the points specifically referred and matters incidental thereto" (IDA s.10(4); DCGM, Express Newspapers).
  3. Defective reference — what happens when the Government drafts the reference badly: vaguely (Sarathy), one-sidedly so that it pre-judges the real dispute (ITDC, Moolchand, TISCO), for a demand never raised (Sindhu Resettlement), or to the wrong forum (FDC).
IDA → CODE — the single most important change for this unit

The Code abolishes the general Government reference. Under s.53(6) read with R.23(9) (Form II) any party may itself apply to the Industrial Tribunal within 90 days of the conciliation failure report; an individual worker may also move the Tribunal directly under s.4 read with s.2(q) (dismissal/discharge/retrenchment/termination disputes are "industrial disputes" without espousal). The Government is no longer a gatekeeper between the parties and the Tribunal. The only reference that survives is the one in the syllabus: the Central Government's power under s.54 to refer a dispute of national importance / multi-State interest to the National Industrial Tribunal (IDA ss.7B, 10(1A)) — plus three "reference-like" powers: transfer/withdrawal to the NIT (s.92(2)), reference of a question of interpretation of an award or settlement (s.95), and the arbitration route (s.42).

Therefore, in the exam: (i) the Telco/Sharad Kumar line ("Government cannot adjudicate while deciding whether to refer") now operates only on the Central Government under s.54 and, by analogy, on any authority that screens applications; (ii) the TISCO line ("frame the reference neutrally; Tribunal confined to points referred + incidental") now operates on the s.54 order of reference and, by analogy, on the Form II application and the issues framed by the Tribunal; (iii) FDC becomes the leading authority on the only reference power left. Examiners (Dec 2024 Q.1; May–June 2025 Q.6) expect exactly this two-layer answer — the IDA principle from the DU case, then its fate under the Code.

Routes into adjudication under the Code

Party-driven (no reference): application to Tribunal after conciliation failure s.53(6)/R.23(9); individual worker after GRC/conciliation s.4; Trade-Union disputes s.22; s.90 complaints s.91; recovery s.59(2); approval applications under the proviso to s.90(2).

Government-driven (reference survives)

Central Government → NIT s.54 (opinion: national importance / establishments in more than one State) · withdrawal & transfer to NIT s.92(2) · interpretation of award/settlement s.95 (appropriate Government → Tribunal/NIT) · declaration postponing enforceability of an award s.55(3) proviso.

Consensual

Arbitration agreement s.42 (Form V, R.20), before the dispute reaches the Tribunal; notification under s.42(5) binds non-signatories; arbitrator's award enforceable like a Tribunal award (Unit 1, §12).

Where the DU cases now "live"

FDC → s.54/s.46 · Telco, Sharad Kumar → s.54 opinion-forming + jurisdictional facts left to Tribunal (s.44) · TISCO, DCGM → drafting of s.54 references and framing of issues on Form II applications (s.49 procedure).
Exam tip — the one sentence that earns the "Code" marks

"Under the Code the Government's reference has been replaced by the parties' right to apply (s.53(6), s.4), so the K.P. Krishnan–Telco–Sharad Kumar problem of a Government usurping the Tribunal's role can now arise only in a s.54 reference to the National Industrial Tribunal, where FDC shows that the Central Government's opinion is reviewable for non-application of mind, absence of material and misreading of the statutory conditions."

2. Statutory framework — the Code provisions and their IDA ancestors

IR Code, 2020 — s.54 (the provision named in the syllabus)

54. Reference to and functions of National Industrial Tribunal.

(1) The Central Government may, by order in writing, refer to the National Industrial Tribunal any industrial dispute which, in the opinion of such Government, involves question of national importance or is of such a nature that industrial establishments situated in more than one State are likely to be interested in, or affected by, such dispute, for adjudication of the dispute, and the National Industrial Tribunal shall adjudicate such dispute.

(2) Where a dispute is referred to the National Industrial Tribunal under sub-section (1), it shall hold its proceedings expeditiously and shall submit its award within the period specified in the order of reference, or within such further period as the Central Government may extend.

(Paraphrased from the Gazette text; the marked phrases are the three statutory conditions that FDC extracts from the identically-worded IDA s.7B(1).)

s.46 — National Industrial Tribunal (constitution)

The Central Government may, by notification, constitute one or more National Industrial Tribunals for the adjudication of industrial disputes which, in its opinion, involve questions of national importance or are of such a nature that industrial establishments in more than one State are likely to be interested in or affected by them. The NIT consists of two members — a Judicial Member (a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court) and an Administrative Member (a person who is or has been Secretary to the Government of India or holds an equivalent rank). The Judicial Member presides; selection procedure, salaries and terms are prescribed by rules (s.99(2)(zj)). Contrast IDA s.7B: a one-member National Tribunal (a sitting or retired High Court Judge) with optional assessors.

s.92 — Power to transfer certain proceedings

(1) The appropriate Government may, by order in writing and for reasons to be stated, withdraw any proceeding pending before a Tribunal and transfer it to another Tribunal, which may proceed de novo or from the stage reached. (2) The Central Government may, by order in writing and for reasons to be stated, withdraw any proceeding pending before a Tribunal constituted by the Central Government or the State Government and transfer it to a National Industrial Tribunal. (3) The Central Government may, by notification and for reasons stated, empower a Tribunal constituted by a State Government to entertain and dispose of Central-sphere cases. (IDA s.33B and the third proviso to s.10(1).) Sub-section (2) is the Code's substitute for IDA s.10(6): instead of an automatic quashing of State proceedings on an NIT reference, the Central Government must consciously withdraw and transfer, with reasons.

The IDA provision every DU case construes — s.10 (with s.12(5)) in sub-section form
  • s.10(1) — "Where the appropriate Government is of opinion that any industrial dispute exists or is apprehended, it may at any time, by order in writing," (a) refer the dispute to a Board; (b) refer any matter connected with or relevant to it to a Court of Inquiry; (c) refer it, if it relates to a Second-Schedule matter, to a Labour Court; (d) refer it, whether Second or Third Schedule, to a Tribunal. First proviso: a Third-Schedule dispute not likely to affect more than 100 workmen may go to a Labour Court. Second proviso: in a public utility service where a s.22 notice has been given, the Government shall refer unless the notice is frivolous or vexatious or reference is inexpedient. Third proviso: the Central Government may refer to a State-constituted Labour Court/Tribunal.
  • s.10(1A) — Central Government, "whether or not it is the appropriate Government", may at any time refer a dispute involving a question of national importance or of multi-State interest to a National Tribunal (the direct ancestor of Code s.54).
  • s.10(2) — on a joint application by the parties (representing the majority of each side) the Government shall refer.
  • s.10(2A) — the order may fix the period for the award (three months for individual-workman disputes; extendable; no lapse on expiry).
  • s.10(3) — on making a reference the Government may prohibit the continuance of any strike or lock-out connected with the dispute.
  • s.10(4) — "Where … the appropriate Government has specified the points of dispute for adjudication, the Labour Court or the Tribunal or the National Tribunal … shall confine its adjudication to those points and matters incidental thereto."
  • s.10(5) — the Government may, at the time of reference or later but before the award, include other establishments or classes of establishments likely to be interested in or affected by the dispute, "whether or not at the time of such inclusion any dispute exists or is apprehended" in them.
  • s.10(6) — once a matter is referred to a National Tribunal, no Labour Court or Tribunal (Central or State) has jurisdiction over it; pending proceedings on that matter are deemed quashed; the appropriate Government cannot refer it elsewhere.
  • s.10(7) — for a National Tribunal reference in a State-sphere dispute, "appropriate Government" in ss.15, 17, 19, 33A, 33B, 36A means the Central Government.
  • s.10(8) — no proceeding lapses on the death of a workman party; the award is still made.
  • s.12(5) — on the failure report, "if … the appropriate Government is satisfied that there is a case for reference … it may make such reference. Where [it] does not make such a reference it shall record and communicate to the parties concerned its reasons therefor."
  • s.7B — constitution of National Tribunals (quoted in FDC ¶7): one member, High Court Judge; two assessors optional.
  • s.33B — withdrawal of a pending proceeding from one authority and transfer to another (the only "withdrawal" the Act allowed — D.N. Ganguly).
RELATED LAW — constitutional and procedural hooks used throughout Unit 2
  • Article 226 / 227 — the route to challenge a reference, a refusal to refer, or a Tribunal's excess of jurisdiction (writs of certiorari to quash; mandamus to reconsider or to refer). FDC (¶11) holds that a writ lies against the NIT too: "the National Tribunal and State Tribunals have same status".
  • Article 136 — special leave against High Court orders (all four DU cases except FDC reached the Supreme Court this way) and directly against Tribunal awards (Bharat Bank, Unit 1).
  • Article 14 — arbitrary refusal to refer identical disputes of similarly placed workmen is discriminatory (D.C. & G.M. Co. v. Shambhu Nath Mukherjee, AIR 1978 SC 8).
  • Article 323B and the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 / Finance Act, 2017 s.184 — service conditions of members of Central Tribunals; explains the two-member design of ss.44 and 46.
  • Seventh Schedule, List III, Entry 22 ("industrial and labour disputes") — competence for both the IDA and the Code; State amendments to the IDA on references (e.g., Maharashtra, U.P., Gujarat time-limits) lapsed with the repeal (s.104).
  • General Clauses Act, 1897, s.21 — a power to issue an order includes a power to rescind it "unless a different intention appears"; D.N. Ganguly held that the IDA's scheme (s.33B, s.19) shows the different intention, so a reference cannot be cancelled. The same reasoning transfers to s.54 read with s.92.
  • Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 — s.49 gives Tribunals/NIT civil-court powers and Order XXI execution; Limitation Act, 1963 does not apply to references/applications by its own force (Ajaib Singh), but the Code now supplies its own limits (90 days; 2 years).
  • Sales Promotion Employees (Conditions of Service) Act, 1976 — medical representatives (FDC) and sales executives are "workmen"/"workers" by statutory inclusion (Code s.2(zr) expressly includes sales promotion employees), which is why the "is he a workman?" objection in Sharad Kumar would be weaker today.

3. Evolution of the reference system — a timeline

1929 — Trade Disputes Act: Courts of Inquiry and Boards of Conciliation only; no compulsory adjudication.
1942–46 — Rule 81A, Defence of India Rules: Government could refer disputes to adjudicators and enforce awards — the war-time model copied into the IDA.
1947 — Industrial Disputes Act: s.10 reference to Board / Court / Tribunal; Government control over entry to adjudication; s.10(4) "points and matters incidental" not yet in its present form.
1950–53 — Bharat Bank (1950) makes Tribunals amenable to Art. 136; the All-India Industrial Tribunal (Bank Disputes) under Justice Sastry hands down the first pan-India bank award (1953) — the practical origin of the "national tribunal" idea. State of Madras v. C.P. Sarathy (1953): reference is an administrative act; a general reference to an industry is valid.
1956 (Act 36 of 1956) — Labour Courts (s.7), Tribunals (s.7A) and National Tribunals (s.7B) created; s.10(1A), 10(2A), 10(6), 10(7) inserted; s.10A voluntary arbitration; s.33B transfers. The three-tier system that lasted until 2025.
1958–60 — D.N. Ganguly (1958): no power to cancel/supersede a reference. Hotel Imperial (1959): incidental power to grant interim relief. State of Bombay v. K.P. Krishnan (1960): refusal to refer on extraneous grounds → mandamus. National Industrial Tribunal (Bank Disputes) under Justice K.T. Desai constituted under s.7B (award 1962) — the classic real-world use of the national reference.
1962–67 — Express Newspapers (1962): Government must draft references carefully because s.10(4) confines the Tribunal. Bombay Union of Journalists (1964): Government may consider prima facie merits but cannot decide them. DCGM v. Workmen (1967): "incidental" means ancillary, not the root of the dispute. Sindhu Resettlement (1968): no demand on the employer → no industrial dispute → reference incompetent.
1965 / 1971 — s.2A: individual dismissal disputes deemed industrial disputes (no espousal); s.11A: Tribunal's power to re-appraise punishment.
1978–89 — Shambhu Nath Mukherjee (1978, Art. 14); Avon Services (1979) and Western India Match (1970): Government may reconsider a refusal; Ram Avtar Sharma (1985) and M.P. Irrigation Karamchari Sangh (1985): Government cannot adjudicate but may decline perverse/frivolous demands; Telco Convoy Drivers (1989): Supreme Court directs a reference after persistent refusals.
1982 amendments — s.10(2A) time-limits; ITDC v. Delhi Administration (Del FB 1982): reference presupposing a lock-out quashed — the seed of TISCO.
1995–2002 — Bombay Gumasta Union v. Bhope (1995): Government may amend/modify a reference; Moolchand Kharati Ram Hospital (Del 1998; SC 2002); National Engineering Industries (2000): Tribunal cannot travel beyond the reference; Indian Tea Assn. v. Ajit Kumar Barat (2000): the five-point summary of the Government's role; Sharad Kumar (2002): designation is not the test; nature of duties is for the Tribunal.
2007 — FDC Ltd. v. Union of India (Del HC): first detailed judicial exposition of "national importance" and "establishments in more than one State" under s.7B; notification quashed for non-application of mind.
2010 amendment — s.2A(2)–(3): a dismissed workman may apply directly to the Labour Court/Tribunal 45 days after his conciliation application, within three years — the first statutory bypass of the reference.
2014 — TISCO v. State of Jharkhand: reference that presupposed the employees' "transfer" quashed; Government directed to make a fresh, neutrally worded reference within two months.
2020 / 21 Nov 2025 / 8 May 2026 — IR Code enacted (Act 35 of 2020), brought into force, and the IR (Central) Rules, 2026 notified: general reference abolished (s.53(6), s.4); s.54 NIT reference retained; s.92(2) transfer to NIT; two-member Tribunals.
Real example — what a "national" reference looked like in practice

The bank-employee wage disputes of the 1950s–60s are the textbook illustration of a dispute "of such a nature that industrial establishments situated in more than one State are likely to be interested in, or affected by" it: the same banks operated branches in every State, the demands (scales of pay, dearness allowance, categorisation of areas) were identical everywhere, and inconsistent State awards would have been unworkable. The Central Government therefore constituted an all-India tribunal (Sastry Award, 1953) and later a National Industrial Tribunal under s.7B presided over by Justice K.T. Desai (Desai Award, 1962), whose scales governed banking across India. Compare FDC (2007): 58 medical representatives of one company dismissed on different facts in different States — the Delhi High Court held that such a "cluster of individual disputes" is not a dispute of national importance and that one company's branches are not "establishments in more than one State".

PART II · 4. Anatomy of s.54 — elements of the Central Government's power

Section 54 is short, but every word in it has a history in the IDA cases. Break it into its seven components; an examiner who asks "discuss the nature and scope of the power of the Central Government under s.54" is asking you to walk through these components with authority for each.

ComponentWhat the Code says / requiresAuthority & comment
1. Who"The Central Government" — not "the appropriate Government". Power exercisable through officers/authorities to whom it is delegated by notification under s.100. In practice the Ministry of Labour & Employment issues the order (in FDC the notification of 22.5.2001 was issued by the "Government of India, Ministry of Labour").Under IDA s.10(1A) the Central Government could refer "whether or not it is the appropriate Government"; the Code omits the phrase but the result is the same because s.46 lets only the Central Government constitute an NIT and s.92(2) lets it pull a State-Tribunal case into the NIT. FDC itself concerned a pharmaceutical company in the State sphere.
2. Subject-matter"any industrial dispute" — must satisfy s.2(q): a dispute or difference between employers and workers (or employers/employers, workers/workers) connected with employment, non-employment, terms of employment or conditions of labour, including an individual worker's termination dispute.The dispute must exist as a dispute: a demand must have been raised and refused (Sindhu Resettlement, quoted in TISCO); it may be one that is "apprehended" (Sarathy — the Code drops the words "exists or is apprehended" but s.53(1) keeps them for conciliation, and nothing in s.54 requires an actual strike). Whether the persons are "workers" or the enterprise an "industry" are disputed facts for the NIT, not for the Government (Telco, Sharad Kumar).
3. Condition A"involves question of national importance".FDC ¶9: a dispute "where some important question of law is involved which is going to affect the fate of the workmen in general throughout India or fate of the employers throughout India … which would determine the future course or future service conditions or future relationship of working class in general or the employers in general" — e.g., whether a hospital or a research institute is an "industry"; a dispute affecting "large scale of identical or unidentical workmen". Not: a cluster of fact-bound individual terminations.
4. Condition B"of such a nature that industrial establishments situated in more than one State are likely to be interested in, or affected by, such dispute".FDC ¶9: means establishments "of the same nature or diverse nature but they must not be of same group or family"; "if one establishment has branches in different States … that would be one establishment having branches or factories in different States", not "different establishments in different States". The banking wage disputes (many banks, all States) satisfy it; one company's medical representatives posted in many States do not.
5. The opinion"in the opinion of such Government" that A or B is satisfied. Subjective in form but must be an opinion actually formed, on material, on the correct legal meaning of A/B.FDC ¶8: the third pre-requisite is that "the Central Government has formed an opinion that either of the above two situations was there"; ¶12: "there was no application of mind before passing the order". Sarathy: the court will not weigh the sufficiency of the material; K.P. Krishnan/Barat: but it will strike down an opinion based on irrelevant/extraneous considerations or a misreading of the statute.
6. Form"by order in writing" — in practice a Gazette notification (s.2(za)) which (i) refers the dispute, (ii) states the points of dispute (the "terms of reference") and (iii) fixes the period for the award (s.54(2)). The NIT must already be constituted under s.46 (or is constituted by the same notification, as in FDC).The order must be drafted carefully: "the questions which are intended to be tried … should be so worded as to leave no scope for ambiguity or controversy" (Express Newspapers, quoted in TISCO ¶16); it must not presuppose contested facts (TISCO, ITDC). A general reference naming an industry rather than each dispute is valid if the dispute is identifiable (Sarathy).
7. Consequences(i) NIT "shall adjudicate" (duty, not discretion) — s.54(1); (ii) proceedings "expeditiously", award within the period fixed or extended — s.54(2); (iii) strikes/lock-outs barred during pendency + 60 days — s.62(1)(e)–(f); (iv) conditions of service frozen — s.90; (v) award in writing, communicated, enforceable after 30 days unless the Central Government postpones it — s.55; binding — s.57; operative one year — s.58; (vi) recovery — s.59; execution — s.49(9); (vii) related proceedings in a Tribunal may be withdrawn to the NIT — s.92(2); (viii) review only under Arts. 226/227/136 — FDC ¶11.Under the IDA, s.10(6) automatically quashed parallel State proceedings; the Code requires a reasoned s.92(2) transfer — a point to make when comparing "scope".

4.1 The National Industrial Tribunal that receives the reference (s.46) — related provisions

Constitution
By the Central Government by notification; "one or more" NITs; two members (Judicial Member — High Court Judge, presiding; Administrative Member — Secretary to the Government of India rank). Under the IDA, one member only (High Court Judge) with optional assessors (s.7B(4)); FDC records that the NIT sat at Calcutta "because the presiding officer was to be of a status of a High Court Judge and only a Judge of Calcutta High Court was available" (¶6).
Powers & procedure
Same as a Tribunal: s.49 (civil-court powers, entry, assessors, costs, execution), s.50 (relief, interim relief), s.47 (difference of opinion → Judicial Member of another Tribunal/NIT), s.48 (disqualifications), s.94 (representation; lawyers only with consent and leave).
Time-frame
Award within the period in the order of reference or as extended (s.54(2)); communicated to the parties and the Central Government (s.55); enforceable after 30 days (s.55(3)) unless postponed by notification on grounds of national economy or social justice — order laid before Parliament within 90 days; award enforceable 15 days after laying (s.55(4)–(5) pattern of IDA s.17A).
Status vis-à-vis High Courts
FDC ¶11: "The plea … that the National Tribunal has status of a High Court, is not correct. The National Tribunal and State Tribunals have same status. Both are industrial tribunals and a writ under Articles 226 and 227 … lies against the decision of the National Tribunal as well." Relevance: the Government cannot use s.54 to insulate a dispute from judicial review; and territorial jurisdiction of the High Court follows the cause of action/seat of the NIT (Art. 226(2)).
Relationship with Tribunals
Code: s.92(2) — Central Government may withdraw a proceeding pending before any Tribunal (Central- or State-constituted) and transfer it to the NIT, with reasons; the NIT may proceed de novo or from the stage reached. IDA: s.10(6) — automatic ouster and deemed quashing. Exam point: the Code's version demands a reasoned, reviewable order and does not quash pending proceedings ipso jure.
Rules
IR (Central) Rules, 2026: procedure for selection, salaries and terms of NIT members (s.99(2)(zj)); additional civil-court powers (s.99(2)(zk)); e-portal filing and service (R.46–47); Form II applications go to the Tribunal, not the NIT — the NIT receives disputes only by reference (s.54) or transfer (s.92(2)) or a s.95 interpretation reference or a s.91 complaint in a pending matter.
Worked examples — applying the two conditions
  • Pan-India payments bank, 14 States, dispute over a new grade structure for "banking correspondents" (2,000 workers). Banking is Central-sphere (s.2(b)); the question (are BCs "workers"? what scales?) is common to all branches, but they are branches of one establishment — FDC says that alone is not Condition B. Condition A may still be satisfied because the answer "would determine the future course or future service conditions … of working class in general" in the payments-bank sector, and other banks using the BC model are "likely to be interested". A carefully reasoned s.54 order can survive; an order that merely recites the section cannot.
  • Cement industry wage revision demanded by unions in plants of eight companies in five States. Condition B squarely satisfied (many establishments, different owners, several States) — the classic Wage-Board-type national reference.
  • 30 delivery riders of one app-based platform dismissed in six cities after separate misconduct inquiries. Fact-bound individual terminations — FDC applies; each rider (or the union) must apply to the Tribunal having jurisdiction under s.53(6)/s.4; a s.54 reference would be quashed, and would in any event deprive workers of a convenient forum ("asking an individual, who was appointed in Chandigarh to go to Calcutta … would be too much" — FDC ¶10).
  • Whether the employees of a chain of charitable hospitals across India fall within "industry" (s.2(p)) after the Code's exclusion of charitable institutions. FDC ¶9 itself gives "whether a hospital is an industry" as an example of a question of national importance — a s.54 reference (or a s.92(2) transfer of a test case) is appropriate.

5. Nature of the power — administrative, discretionary, subjective, but not unfettered

The DU cases settle the character of the referring power (whether under IDA s.10(1)/12(5) or, identically, under s.7B/10(1A) and now s.54). The propositions below are cumulative; cite them in this order.

PropositionContent and authority
1. Administrative, not judicial or quasi-judicial"While exercising power under Section 10(1) of the Act, the function of the appropriate government is an administrative function and not a judicial or quasi-judicial function" — Telco Convoy ¶12, following State of Madras v. C.P. Sarathy, AIR 1953 SC 53 and Bombay Union of Journalists v. State of Bombay, AIR 1964 SC 1617. Consequences: no lis before the Government; no obligation to hear the parties before referring or refusing (Sultan Singh v. State of Haryana, (1996) 2 SCC 66; Avon Services Production Agencies v. Industrial Tribunal, Haryana, AIR 1979 SC 170); no Art. 136 petition against the Government's order because it is not a "tribunal" (Bharat Bank test; Jaswant Sugar Mills) — the remedy is Art. 226.
2. Founded on the Government's subjective opinionThe Government "should satisfy itself, on the facts and circumstances brought to its notice, … that an industrial dispute exists or is apprehended"; "the factual existence of a dispute or its apprehension and the expediency of making a reference are matters entirely for the government to decide"; the High Court cannot examine whether there was material to support the conclusion (the four Sarathy propositions). Under s.54 the opinion is about the two statutory conditions (FDC).
3. Discretionary — "may", not "shall"IDA s.10(1) and s.12(5) used "may"; so does s.54(1). Exceptions under the IDA: public-utility s.22 notice (second proviso — "shall unless…"), joint application s.10(2) ("shall"). The Code has no "shall refer" situations because the parties can themselves apply; the only mandatory step is that once a s.54 reference is made "the National Industrial Tribunal shall adjudicate".
4. A screening power, not an adjudicating power"The formation of opinion as to whether an industrial dispute 'exists or is apprehended' is not the same thing as to adjudicate the dispute itself on its merits" — Telco ¶10. The Government "cannot delve into the merits of the dispute and take upon itself the determination of the lis, which would certainly be in excess of the power conferred on it" — ¶12. Whether the claimants are workmen (Telco), whether the claimant's duties make him a workman despite his designation (Sharad Kumar ¶31), whether employees were "transferred" or taken over by a new employer (TISCO ¶11, ¶20), whether a termination was justified (Ram Avtar Sharma v. State of Haryana, AIR 1985 SC 915) — all are for the Tribunal.
5. But a prima facie look is permittedThe Government may decline where the claim is "either perverse or frivolous" (M.P. Irrigation Karamchari Sangh v. State of M.P., (1985) 2 SCC 103, applied in Telco ¶13), patently stale, or plainly not an industrial dispute (e.g., no demand at all — Sindhu); it may consider whether the claim is "prima facie … frivolous or … not an industrial dispute" (Bombay Union of Journalists). "The government should be very slow to attempt an examination of the demand with a view to declining reference and courts will always be vigilant whenever the government attempts to usurp the powers of the Tribunal" (Telco ¶13).
6. Exercisable "at any time"; no limitation; reconsideration allowedIDA s.10(1) said "at any time": no limitation period (Shalimar Works v. Workmen, AIR 1959 SC 1217), though a dead or stale dispute should not be referred (Nedungadi Bank Ltd. v. K.P. Madhavankutty, (2000) 2 SCC 455 — reference after seven years quashed). A refusal is not final: the Government may reconsider and refer later on the same or fresh material (Western India Match Co. v. Western India Match Co. Workers Union, AIR 1970 SC 1205; Avon Services, 1979; Telco — the Supreme Court itself sent the matter back for reconsideration, ¶8). The Code omits "at any time" from s.54 but, being a Government reference, no limitation applies to it either; contrast the party-driven routes (90 days / 2 years).
7. Once made, cannot be withdrawn or cancelledState of Bihar v. D.N. Ganguly, AIR 1958 SC 1018: the Act contains no power to cancel or supersede a reference; s.21 General Clauses Act is excluded by the scheme (the parties' rights under ss.15, 17, 19, 33 attach on reference); a settlement reached during pendency is to be recorded by the Tribunal as an award. The only "withdrawal" is withdrawal-and-transfer under IDA s.33B — now s.92. A second reference during the pendency of the first is valid — and is neither a withdrawal nor a supersession of the first — when it comprises matters totally unconnected with or different from those originally referred (State of Maharashtra v. K.E. Union, AIR 1955 SC 635); the Government may thus refer disputes piecemeal as exigencies demand (F.M. Chakravarty v. State of Assam, AIR 1960 Assam 11) and add items to a pending reference. What it cannot do is split connected matters between a Labour Court and a Tribunal at the same time (the Textile Finishing Mills case, (1964) I LLJ 460 (SC), cited by Jain) — a point now moot under the Code's single Tribunal.
8. Can be amended, clarified and enlargedThe Government may correct clerical errors, add parties or establishments (IDA s.10(5)), and modify the terms of reference before the award (Bombay Gumasta Union v. M.R. Bhope, 1995 — the Bombay High Court upheld an amendment substituting the correct employer). Under the Code the s.54 order can likewise be amended by a further order (s.21 General Clauses Act applies to amendment as opposed to cancellation), and the NIT can itself implead necessary parties through its civil-court powers (s.49) — TISCO ¶11 ("M/s Lafarge also becomes a necessary party").
9. Reasons: none required for a reference; required for a refusalIDA s.12(5) obliged the Government to "record and communicate" reasons for not referring after conciliation failure; K.P. Krishnan held that those reasons must be germane, else mandamus lies. A reference itself need not be a speaking order (Sarathy), but for an NIT reference FDC ¶4–¶12 effectively requires that the record "sufficiently demonstrate that there was basis for forming such an opinion" — because the two conditions are jurisdictional. Under the Code there is no refusal-to-refer stage for ordinary disputes; for s.54 the safe course is a reasoned order.
10. Not an appellate or supervisory power over the TribunalThe Government cannot direct the Tribunal how to decide, cannot review the award (except the limited s.55(3) proviso postponement on economic grounds and the s.95 interpretation reference), and cannot "adjudicate" through the drafting of the reference (TISCO ¶20: by presupposing facts "the appropriate Government has itself decided those contentious issues and assumed the role of an adjudicator").
Quotable — the five-point summary approved in Sharad Kumar

In Secretary, Indian Tea Association v. Ajit Kumar Barat, (2000) 3 SCC 93 (relied on in Sharad Kumar in the paragraphs omitted from the DU extract), the Supreme Court summarised the position: (1) the appropriate Government would not be justified in making a reference without satisfying itself on the facts and circumstances brought to its notice that an industrial dispute exists or is apprehended, and it is desirable that the order indicate the nature of the dispute; (2) the order of reference is an administrative order and not a judicial or quasi-judicial one, so the court cannot canvass it closely to see if there was material to support it; (3) being administrative, no lis is involved and the order rests on subjective satisfaction; (4) if the reasons given show that the Government took into account irrelevant or foreign material, the court may consider a writ of mandamus; (5) it is open to a party to show that what was referred was not an industrial dispute at all. — Memorise these five; they structure any "nature and scope" answer, and FDC adds a sixth for s.54: the two statutory conditions are jurisdictional and their misreading is reviewable.

Exam tip — remedies ladder (write it as three rungs)

Rung 1 — reconsideration: K.P. Krishnan: mandamus "to reconsider in accordance with law"; the court will not order a reference at first instance because the discretion is the Government's. Rung 2 — direction to refer: where the Government has repeatedly refused on an impermissible ground (Telco: refusal in 1979 and 1986; reconsideration on the Supreme Court's own order in 1989 still refused on the merits → "we think we should direct the government to make such a reference"; Sharad Kumar ¶32: "directed to refer the dispute … including the question whether the appellant is a workman"). Rung 3 — fresh reference on corrected terms: TISCO ¶21 ("quash the references made in the present form … direction … to make fresh reference, incorporating real essence of the dispute … within a period of two months"). For an NIT reference that fails the s.54 conditions, the only relief is quashing (FDC) — the court cannot order the Central Government to refer to an ordinary Tribunal under the Code because that route belongs to the parties.

RELATED LAW — natural justice and reasons in administrative decisions (why Sultan Singh and K.P. Krishnan co-exist)

Administrative orders that affect rights ordinarily attract the duty to act fairly (A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India, AIR 1970 SC 150) and, where a statute requires reasons, the reasons must be genuine and relevant (S.N. Mukherjee v. Union of India, AIR 1990 SC 1984). The reference power is treated as an exception to the hearing rule because it decides nothing finally — the referred party gets a full hearing before the Tribunal (Sultan Singh; Avon Services). But a refusal ends the matter for the workman, which is why the IDA insisted on recorded and communicated reasons (s.12(5)) and why courts scrutinise them (K.P. Krishnan). Under the Code the s.54 order should record the basis of the Central Government's opinion; FDC shows that a bald notification will not survive if the record contradicts it.

7. Case 3 — FDC Limited v. Union of India & Ors., 136 (2007) DLT 266 : (2007) 2 LLJ 413 (Del) — DU material pp. 31–34

CASE — Delhi High Court · Shiv Narayan Dhingra, J. · writ petition under Art. 226 · "s.54 IR Code pari materia with s.7B IDA"
Background & facts
FDC Ltd. is a pharmaceutical company with medical representatives (MRs) posted across India. Between 1997 and 1999 it terminated the services of a number of MRs, in each case on that MR's own facts (misconduct of different kinds; some during probation, some during training). Individual MRs raised disputes in the States where they worked; statements of claim were filed before Labour Courts/Tribunals in various States, some proceedings were pending and in some awards had already been passed. By notification dated 22.5.2001 the Government of India, Ministry of Labour, in exercise of powers under IDA s.7B, constituted a National Tribunal with headquarters at Calcutta and referred to it the dispute: "Whether the actions of the management in relation to FDC Ltd. in terminating the services of 58 medical representatives as given in Annexure are legal and justified? If not, to what relief are the medical representatives entitled to?" (¶1). FDC challenged the notification.
Issues
(1) What are the pre-requisites for a valid reference to a National Tribunal under s.7B (now Code s.54/s.46)? (2) Does a dispute about the individual terminations of 58 employees of one company, posted in different States, involve a "question of national importance" or is it "of such a nature that industrial establishments situated in more than one State are likely to be interested in, or affected by" it? (3) Was the opinion of the Central Government formed with application of mind and on material? (4) Is the National Tribunal beyond the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts (as the Government contended)?
Petitioner's (FDC's) arguments
(i) The parties and records were spread all over the country; each MR's case turned on his individual facts, requiring separate witnesses and records — a single tribunal at Calcutta was oppressive (¶3). (ii) The reference was mala fide and made with ulterior motives (¶3). (iii) Only a dispute of national importance, or one in which establishments in more than one State are interested, can go to a National Tribunal; that MRs of one establishment work in several States does not satisfy the section (¶4). (iv) There was no common cause among the MRs except that each faced action for misconduct; each had raised his own dispute in his own State (¶4). (v) Non-application of mind: of the 58 named MRs, 24 had raised no dispute, 4 had resigned and collected dues, 2 were still in employment, 9 were terminated during probation and 6 during training (¶4). (vi) s.7B requires the Central Government's due satisfaction as a pre-condition, exercised "in a guided and rationale manner"; the notification should demonstrate the basis of the opinion, and there was none (¶4).
Respondent's (Union of India's) arguments
(i) 58 MRs were terminated without retrenchment compensation or were charge-sheeted; non-reference "would have sent wrong signals to the workmen and adversely affected the industrial relations in vital pharmaceuticals industries" (¶5). (ii) The Ministry's concern was to give relief to workmen spread over different States; adjudication by different State Tribunals risked conflicting decisions, so the "entire issue of termination" was sent to one National Tribunal to harmonise outcomes (¶5–¶6). (iii) The dispute showed worsening industrial relations and raised questions of natural justice (¶5). (iv) Since the dispute had arisen in different States, the requirement of s.7B was satisfied (¶5). (v) The National Tribunal, having "the same status as that of High Court", was beyond Articles 226/227, so parties could not delay matters by writs (¶6). (vi) Inconvenience could be met by shifting the venue on request; pending State proceedings would be transferred to the National Tribunal (¶6).
Decision & reasoning
Writ allowed; notification set aside (¶13). The Court read s.7B and extracted the three pre-requisites (¶8): (i) the dispute involves a question of national importance; or (ii) it is of such a nature that industrial establishments situated in more than one State are likely to be interested in or affected; and (iii) the Central Government has formed an opinion that either situation exists. "The emphasis is on the nature of dispute only and its important character" (¶9). National importance means a dispute "where some important question of law is involved which is going to affect the fate of the workmen in general throughout India or fate of the employers throughout India … which would determine the future course or future service conditions or future relationship of working class in general or the employers in general" — e.g., whether a hospital or a research institute is an industry; or issues where "the fate of large scale of identical or unidentical workmen is involved" (¶9). A "cluster of disputes" requiring adjudication of each workman's facts "cannot be considered a single dispute of national importance"; "the adjudication of the individual dispute of different workmen cannot be referred to a National Tribunal because the workmen are spread over in different States" (¶9). Establishments in more than one State does not mean one establishment with branches in different States — "that would be one establishment having branches or factories in different States"; the phrase means establishments "of the same nature or diverse nature but they must not be of same group or family" (¶9). On the facts, each MR was dismissed on a different date, in a different State, on different facts; "dismissal of a group of workmen cannot be considered an important question of law or a question of national importance"; the Government had not controverted the figures showing that many of the 58 had no live dispute at all (¶10). The plea that the National Tribunal has High-Court status "is not correct. The National Tribunal and State Tribunals have same status … a writ under Articles 226 and 227 … lies against the decision of the National Tribunal as well" (¶11). "It is apparent that there was no application of mind before passing the order"; far from benefiting workmen, the order "would cause justice for the workmen more distant and expensive", there being no provision for their travelling expenses (¶12).
Principles evolved
  1. The power to refer to a National Tribunal is conditional: the two statutory conditions are jurisdictional facts about the nature of the dispute; the Government's opinion must be an opinion about them, formed on material.
  2. "National importance" = an important question of law or policy whose answer affects workmen or employers generally across India, or the fate of a large class of workmen — not a bundle of fact-specific individual cases, however many States they touch.
  3. "Industrial establishments situated in more than one State" = plural, independent establishments (same or different industries), not the branches of one enterprise or group.
  4. Convenience of the workmen, avoidance of conflicting State awards and a wish to keep the matter out of High Courts are not statutory grounds; relying on them shows non-application of mind.
  5. The National Tribunal is an industrial tribunal like any other, subject to Articles 226/227.
  6. Judicial review of a reference (as distinct from a refusal) is available where the statutory conditions are misread or the order is passed mechanically — an application of Barat principle (5) and of the "irrelevant consideration" ground to the referring side.
Under the Code
Section 54(1) reproduces the s.7B/s.10(1A) language word for word ("in the opinion of such Government involves question of national importance or is of such a nature that industrial establishments situated in more than one State are likely to be interested in, or affected by"). FDC is therefore the direct authority on the only surviving reference power. Additional Code points: the NIT now has two members (s.46); the Central Government may also reach the same result by withdrawing pending Tribunal cases into the NIT under s.92(2) — but that power too requires "reasons to be stated" and the same conditions, so FDC governs it; the individual MRs' remedy today is a Form II application to the Tribunal of their own State/region under s.53(6) or s.4 — precisely the "convenient forum" the Court was protecting.
How to use it in the exam
Lead authority for "nature and scope of the power under s.54" (syllabus head (a)); the source of the three pre-requisites and both definitions; a review-of-reference (not refusal) case; combine with Sarathy (subjective opinion) to show the balance — the Court does not weigh the sufficiency of material but insists on the correct statutory question being asked.
Real example — the s.92(2) "back door" and why FDC closes it

Suppose, after the Code, a multinational's 40 sales officers dismissed across 12 States each file Form II applications before 12 different Industrial Tribunals. The Central Government (which is not even the appropriate Government for a private pharma company) cannot consolidate them in the NIT by a s.92(2) order merely to avoid inconsistent awards: s.92(2) requires "reasons to be stated" and the matter must be one the NIT can adjudicate under s.46/s.54, i.e., of national importance or multi-State establishment interest. FDC holds that such a cluster fails both tests. The correct tools are (i) the Tribunal's own power to consolidate matters before it, (ii) s.92(1) transfers by the appropriate Government within one State, and (iii) an agreed test case.

8. Case 4 — Telco Convoy Drivers Mazdoor Sangh v. State of Bihar, (1989) 3 SCC 271 : AIR 1989 SC 1565 — DU material pp. 35–39

CASE — Supreme Court · M.M. Dutt, J. (for the Bench) · appeal by special leave from the Patna High Court (Ranchi Bench)
Facts
The appellant Sangh represented about 900 convoy drivers who drove newly manufactured TELCO vehicles from the Jamshedpur works to destinations across the country. By a letter of demand dated 16-10-1986 addressed to the General Manager, TELCO, the Sangh demanded that all convoy drivers be given permanent status and the facilities available to permanent TELCO employees from the dates of their appointment — a demand that "proceeds on the basis that the convoy drivers are all workmen of TELCO" (¶2). The Deputy Labour Commissioner, by letter dated 26-2-1979 (in an earlier round) informed the Sangh that, in view of the Law Department's opinion of 1973 that there was no relationship of master and servant between TELCO and the convoy drivers, the demands did not come within the purview of the Act and no action could be taken (¶3). The Sangh's first writ petition was dismissed by a Single Judge, but with liberty to re-agitate before the Government, the Judge observing that the letter merely cited the 1973 opinion "without indicating in what context and under what circumstance" (¶3). On the fresh representation, the Deputy Labour Commissioner, Jamshedpur, by letter dated 6-11-1986 gave the same reply (¶4). The second writ petition was summarily dismissed by the High Court holding that the appellants had failed to show prima facie that they were employed by TELCO or by the Telco Contractors' Association (¶5). Hence the appeal.
Issues
(1) In deciding whether to refer under s.10(1), can the appropriate Government decide the very question in dispute — whether the persons raising it are "workmen" of the employer? (2) Is the Government's function under s.10(1) administrative or judicial? (3) Where the Government persistently refuses on that basis, what order should the Court make — remit for reconsideration, or direct a reference?
Appellants' arguments (Mr. Pai)
The Government "exceeded its jurisdiction in purporting to decide the dispute raised by the appellant Sangh in the said letter of demand"; it should have referred the dispute to the Industrial Tribunal "and should not have embarked upon the task of deciding the dispute on its merits through the Deputy Labour Commissioner" (¶6).
Respondent TELCO's arguments (Mr. Shanti Bhushan)
The Government has jurisdiction to consider whether any industrial dispute exists; having found that the convoy drivers were not even workmen of TELCO (no master–servant relationship), it rightly refused because "in the opinion of the government, there was no existence of any industrial dispute" (¶7). Further, unless those raising the dispute are workmen there can be no "industrial dispute" under s.2(k); so one of the factors the Government must consider in forming its opinion is whether the claimants are workmen (¶11).
Course of the proceedings in the Supreme Court
After hearing, the Court gave the Government "one more chance" — by order dated 30-3-1989 it directed reconsideration of the question of reference, keeping the appeal pending (¶8). The Government's order dated 13-4-1989 again refused, and "in refusing to make a reference, the government has adjudicated the dispute on its merits" (¶9).
Decision & reasoning
(a) The Government may form an opinion whether a dispute "exists or is apprehended", but "the formation of opinion … is not the same thing as to adjudicate the dispute itself on its merits". Here the dispute was whether the drivers were TELCO's workmen; by holding they were not, "the dispute has been decided by the government which is, undoubtedly, not permissible" (¶10). (b) TELCO's contention, "attractive though" it was, was rejected: "while exercising power under Section 10(1) of the Act, the function of the appropriate government is an administrative function and not a judicial or quasi-judicial function, and … in performing this administrative function the government cannot delve into the merits of the dispute and take upon itself the determination of the lis, which would certainly be in excess of the power conferred on it by Section 10" (¶12). (c) Applying M.P. Irrigation Karamchari Sangh v. State of M.P., (1985) 2 SCR 1019: "there may be exceptional cases in which the State Government may, on a proper examination of the demand, come to a conclusion that the demands are either perverse or frivolous and do not merit a reference. Further, the government should be very slow to attempt an examination of the demand with a view to declining reference and courts will always be vigilant whenever the government attempts to usurp the powers of the Tribunal for adjudication of valid disputes, and … to allow the government to do so would be to render Section 10 and Section 12(5) of the Act nugatory" (¶13). (d) The State Government "was not justified in adjudicating the dispute, namely, whether the convoy drivers are workmen or employees of TELCO or not"; the orders of the Deputy Labour Commissioner and of the Government could not be sustained (¶14). (e) Relief: since the Government "has persistently declined" even after the Court's own remand, "we think we should direct the government to make such a reference. In several instances this Court had to direct the government to make a reference under Section 10(1)…" (¶15). The State of Bihar was directed to refer the dispute raised by the letter of 16-10-1986 to an appropriate Industrial Tribunal within one month; the High Court's judgment and the impugned orders were set aside (¶16–¶17).
Principles evolved
  1. The referring function is administrative; the Government forms a prima facie opinion on the existence of a dispute — it does not adjudicate.
  2. Whether the claimants are "workmen"/"employees" of the respondent employer is the jurisdictional question for the Tribunal; when it is the very matter in dispute, the Government cannot decide it against the claimants and refuse a reference on that basis — that "usurps the powers of the Tribunal".
  3. The narrow exception: a demand that is perverse or frivolous on a proper examination (M.P. Irrigation); the Government must be "very slow" to use it.
  4. A refusal grounded on an old, generalised legal opinion (the 1973 Law Department opinion), unrelated to the material placed by the union, is a refusal on extraneous/irrelevant grounds.
  5. Remedy: ordinarily a direction to reconsider; where the Government has repeatedly refused on an impermissible ground even after a judicial remand, the Court may direct the reference itself, with a time-limit.
  6. Delegation: an order by the Deputy Labour Commissioner "acting on behalf of the government" is the Government's order and is reviewed as such (IDA s.39 → Code s.100).
Under the Code
The convoy drivers would today file a Form II application before the Industrial Tribunal (s.53(6)) after conciliation, or the Sangh would raise the collective demand and apply; the question whether they are "workers" of TELCO (s.2(zr); contractor-engaged workers are within "industry" under s.2(p) "whether such worker is employed by such employer directly or by or through any agency, including a contractor") is decided by the Tribunal as a jurisdictional fact. No Government can screen them out. The principle nevertheless governs (i) the Central Government under s.54 — it cannot refuse an NIT reference by deciding a contested status question, nor make one presupposing it; (ii) the framing of issues by the Tribunal — status must be tried, not assumed; (iii) the Government's role under s.95 and s.92 (administrative, not adjudicatory).
Exam use
The "usurpation" case: quote ¶10 and ¶12; contrast the M.P. Irrigation exception; pair with Sharad Kumar (same principle, different fact — designation) and with Indian Tea Assn. v. Barat (refusal upheld where status was clear on record) to show the boundary. Use it for Dec 2024 Q.1 (Sheetal) and Cornell's predicted "750 contract workers" problem.
Supporting case relied on in Telco — M.P. Irrigation Karamchari Sangh v. State of M.P., (1985) 2 SCC 103

Daily-rated employees of the M.P. Irrigation Department demanded regularisation and equal pay; the State refused a reference, holding that the demands were unjustified. The Supreme Court held that the Government cannot "sit in judgment over the demands" or refuse a reference on the ground that the claim is unsustainable on merits; only where, on a proper examination, the demands are "perverse or frivolous" may it decline; and the courts "will always be vigilant whenever the government attempts to usurp the powers of the Tribunal". The Court directed a reference. Use: it is the source of both the exception and the warning quoted in Telco ¶13.

9. Case 5 — Sharad Kumar v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi, (2002) 4 SCC 490 : AIR 2002 SC 1724 — DU material pp. 40–44

CASE — Supreme Court · D.P. Mohapatra, J. · appeal from the Delhi High Court's order of 10-7-2000 · (DU extract omits ¶¶11–15, 17, 21–30, which survey the precedents)
Facts
The appellant was employed by M/s Usha International Ltd. and held the post of "Area Sales Executive" (appointment order dated 21/22-4-1983) when his services were terminated by order dated 20-12-1995 (communicated 28-12-1995). No show-cause notice was served and no enquiry held; one month's salary was sent with the letter (¶3). He challenged the termination; conciliation failed and the Conciliation Officer sent a failure report on 23-10-1996. By order dated 14-7-1998 the Government of NCT of Delhi declined to refer the dispute to the Industrial Tribunal/Labour Court, recording: "Admittedly, the applicant was designated as Area Sales Executive and performing the duties of an Area Sales Executive, as such he is not covered by the definition of 'workman' as defined under Section 2(s)" (¶3). The Delhi High Court dismissed his writ petition on 10-7-2000 in four sentences: "an officer appointed as an Area Sales Executive cannot be considered to be a workman" (¶5).
Issues
(1) "Whether on the facts and circumstances of the case the State Government was right in rejecting the appellant's request for a reference and thereby nipping the proceeding at the threshold. Is it a just and proper exercise of the jurisdiction vested under the statute?" (¶7). (2) What is "the scope and extent of the power to be exercised by the appropriate government" under s.10(1) read with s.12(5) when the objection is that the claimant is not a workman? (¶18). (3) Is designation the test of "workman" status?
Appellant's arguments (Shri S. Prasad)
The Government erred in refusing "merely going by the designation of the post"; the appellant performed "multifarious duties" within the first part of s.2(s) and outside its exceptions; whether he was a workman "involves inquiry into facts which could not be finally decided by the State Government while exercising the power under Section 10(1)"; the Government should have referred the dispute "including the question whether the respondent was a 'workman'" (¶8).
Employer's arguments (Shri V.R. Reddy for Usha International)
On the materials produced by the appellant himself in conciliation, he did not fall within any category in the first part of s.2(s) (manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical or supervisory work), so he was not a workman and the refusal was correct (¶9).
Government's arguments (Shri B.A. Mohanty for GNCTD)
Under s.10(1) it is for the Government to decide whether the dispute raised is an "industrial dispute" under s.2(k), which requires it to ascertain whether the dispute is between an employer and a workman; it was therefore "absolutely necessary" for the Government to satisfy itself on the appellant's status, and it did so; no interference was warranted (¶10).
Decision & reasoning
(a) It was "not disputed … that the jurisdiction vested in the appropriate government to make a reference or refuse to do so is administrative in nature and depends on the opinion formed by it on perusal of the report and the materials received from the Conciliation Officer" (¶18); the question was the scope of that power. (b) On s.2(s): a person is a workman if he does any of the kinds of work in the first part; only then does one ask whether an exclusion (managerial/administrative; supervisory above the wage ceiling or mainly managerial functions) applies (¶19–¶20). Whether a person falls in the first part "depends upon the nature of duties assigned to him and/or discharged by him", which may appear from service rules, standing orders, the appointment order or other material; where the duties are "multifarious", the further question is which is the principal duty and which are ancillary — "determination of the question is not easy at the stage when the State Government is exercising the administrative jurisdiction vested in it for the limited purpose of satisfying itself whether the dispute raised is an industrial dispute". "Designation of the employee is not of much importance and certainly not conclusive" (¶20). (c) Applying the decided cases (¶31): the High Court "was clearly in error in confirming the order of rejection … merely taking note of the designation of the post"; neither the Government nor the High Court "made any attempt to go into the different types of duties discharged by the appellant"; the Government "merely considered the designation of the post held by him, which is extraneous to the matters relevant for the purpose"; from the appointment order it could not be held that he fell outside the first part of s.2(s); the question "requires examination of factual matters for which materials including oral evidence will have to be considered. In such a matter the State Government could not arrogate on to itself the power to adjudicate on the question … thereby terminating the proceedings prematurely. Such a matter should be decided by the Industrial Tribunal or the Labour Court on the basis of the materials to be placed before it by the parties." (d) Order: appeal allowed; GNCTD "directed to refer the dispute raised by the appellant including the question whether the appellant is a workman under the Act, to the Industrial Tribunal/Labour Court for adjudication" (¶32).
Principles evolved
  1. The Government's power under s.10(1)/12(5) is administrative and limited to satisfying itself, prima facie, that an industrial dispute exists; it cannot finally decide a mixed question of fact and law such as "workman" status where that requires evidence.
  2. Nature-of-duties test: workman status depends on the work actually done (principal v. ancillary duties), not on designation or label; the two-step structure of s.2(s) (inclusive first part, then exclusions).
  3. Reliance on designation alone is reliance on an extraneous consideration — a recognised ground of judicial review (K.P. Krishnan); the refusal is therefore quashed.
  4. The proper course is to refer the dispute together with the status question, leaving the Tribunal to decide it as a preliminary/jurisdictional issue on evidence.
  5. Where the record is clear the Government may still decline (Barat, distinguished in the omitted paragraphs); the line is between reading the record and weighing contested evidence.
Under the Code
Sharad Kumar would file a Form II application (s.53(6)) or proceed under s.4; the Tribunal (two-member bench, since it is a termination dispute — s.44(7)) decides whether he is a "worker" under s.2(zr). Two Code changes strengthen his case: (i) s.2(zr) expressly includes sales promotion employees (Sales Promotion Employees (Conditions of Service) Act, 1976) and working journalists; (ii) the supervisory exclusion applies only above ₹18,000 p.m. (or as notified) and the managerial/administrative exclusion remains a nature-of-duties question. The case therefore lives on as the leading authority that jurisdictional facts (worker, industry, employer) are for the adjudicator, and as a caution to the Central Government under s.54 and to any Tribunal tempted to dismiss an application "at the threshold" on a label.
Exam use
Perfect for problem questions where the Government/authority refuses because the claimant is a "Zonal Manager", "Senior Sales Executive" (Dec 2024 Q.1 — Sheetal) or "Area Sales Executive": state the nature-of-duties test, the administrative-limits principle, the extraneous-consideration ground, and the remedy (reference including the status question). Add Ananda Bazar Patrika v. Workmen, AIR 1970 SC 1760 (principal-duties test) and Arkal Govind Raj Rao v. Ciba Geigy, (1985) 3 SCC 371 (multifarious duties — dominant nature) as supporting cases if you know them.

10. Supporting authorities on the reference power (from Sarathy to Barat)

These are the cases the DU judgments cite or assume. Each is presented brief-style so that it can be used as a supporting authority; the first (Sarathy) is examined in full because Nov–Dec 2025 Q.2 was built on its facts.

CASE — State of Madras v. C.P. Sarathy, AIR 1953 SC 53 (Patanjali Sastri C.J.) — the foundation case
Facts
The South Indian Cinema Employees' Association submitted a memorandum to the Labour Commissioner, Madras, demanding increased wages and dearness allowance, bonus of three months' wages, leave, provident fund etc. from the managements of the cinema theatres in Madras city. Conciliation failed; the Commissioner's report said the managements would not accept his terms. The Government referred "the industrial dispute between the workers and managements of the cinema talkies in the city of Madras" generally to an Industrial Tribunal — without naming individual theatres or specifying items. Twenty-four theatres received notice. Four (including Prabhat Talkies) objected that no dispute at all existed between them and their own workers, who had raised no demand; the Tribunal refused to exclude them. The award (15-12-1947) was declared binding on 13-2-1948. Prabhat Talkies did not implement it and its manager, C.P. Sarathy, was prosecuted under IDA s.29 (breach of a binding award). The Madras High Court quashed the prosecution, holding the reference bad because no specific dispute had been referred; the State appealed.
Issues
(1) Must the Government specify the parties and the items of dispute in the order of reference? (2) Is a reference of a dispute "between the workers and managements" of an industry in a city valid against an establishment where no demand had been made? (3) Can the High Court examine whether the Government had material to form its opinion? (4) Could the accused, in a prosecution under s.29, question the validity of the reference and award?
Arguments
State: the Government's opinion that a dispute exists or is apprehended is subjective; the reference, being administrative, is not open to scrutiny for sufficiency of material; the dispute was general to the industry and the award binds all under s.18. Sarathy: a reference must be of a specific dispute between specific parties; there was no dispute with Prabhat Talkies; an award founded on an invalid reference is void, so no offence under s.29 was committed.
Held
Appeal allowed; prosecution to proceed. (i) The Government must satisfy itself, on the facts and circumstances brought to its notice, that an industrial dispute "exists or is apprehended"; the factual existence or apprehension of a dispute and the expediency of a reference are matters entirely for the Government. (ii) The order of reference is an administrative act, not judicial or quasi-judicial, and the High Court cannot, under Art. 226, examine whether there was material to support the conclusion. (iii) A general reference of an industry-wide dispute is valid; the word "apprehended" covers establishments where no demand had yet been made but where the dispute was likely to spread — so the four theatres were validly bound. (iv) It is, however, "desirable" that the Government specify the disputes and parties wherever possible, because a vague reference breeds litigation. (v) The validity of the reference cannot be collaterally attacked in the s.29 prosecution on the ground of insufficiency of material.
Principle / relevance
Source of the "administrative act / subjective opinion / no review for sufficiency of material" doctrine that Telco, Sharad Kumar, TISCO and FDC all assume; also the first "defective (vague) reference" case — vagueness is not fatal if the dispute is identifiable. Under the Code the same doctrine applies to the s.54 opinion; the s.29 scenario becomes an s.86 offence (breach of an award/settlement) compoundable under s.89, and the binding effect on non-participating establishments flows from s.57(3).
CASE — State of Bombay v. K.P. Krishnan, AIR 1960 SC 1223

Facts: Firestone workmen raised four demands (gratuity, holidays, classification, unconditional bonus for 1953). The Conciliation Officer's failure report noted "considerable substance" in the bonus claim. The Government refused a reference under s.12(5) because the workmen had resorted to go-slow during the year — i.e., to punish them. Held: s.12(5) requires the Government to consider the report and decide whether "there is a case for reference"; it may take into account the prima facie merits and expediency, but the reasons it records must be germane. A refusal on a punitive, extraneous ground is an improper exercise of discretion; mandamus issues directing the Government to reconsider (the Court will not itself order a reference at the first instance). Use: the origin of judicial review of refusals; the "reconsider, don't compel" remedy that Telco escalates.

CASE — Bombay Union of Journalists v. State of Bombay, AIR 1964 SC 1617

Facts: Two employees of the "Free Press Journal" were retrenched; the union sought a reference; the Government declined, recording reasons that the union attacked as an adjudication of the merits and as taking irrelevant matters into account. Held: In deciding whether to refer, the Government may consider whether the claim is prima facie frivolous or belated, whether it is an industrial dispute at all (espousal), and expediency — but it "cannot delve into the merits". The Government's function is administrative; its consideration of prima facie merits does not convert it into a quasi-judicial function. Use: authority that a prima facie look is allowed (balances Telco); also on espousal (now unnecessary for termination disputes — s.2(q)).

CASE — Ram Avtar Sharma v. State of Haryana, AIR 1985 SC 915

The Government refused to refer termination disputes, recording that the workmen's services had been "terminated in accordance with the terms of appointment" and that the terminations were justified. Held: the Government "cannot adjudicate on the merits of the dispute"; such a refusal is a usurpation of the Tribunal's jurisdiction; the Court quashed the refusal and directed a reference. Together with M.P. Irrigation it supplies the "merits are for the Tribunal" rule that Telco ¶12 calls "well settled".

CASE — Secretary, Indian Tea Association v. Ajit Kumar Barat, (2000) 3 SCC 93

An Assistant Manager of a tea garden claimed to be a workman; the Government refused a reference after examining the record (his managerial functions were undisputed on the documents). Held: the refusal was valid — the Government may look at the material to see whether the person is prima facie a workman and whether an industrial dispute exists, provided it does not decide contested facts. The Court laid down the five-point summary (see §5 quotable box). Use: the counter-weight to Telco/Sharad Kumar: where the record is clear, a refusal on status is not usurpation; where status turns on contested evidence, it is.

CASE — Sultan Singh v. State of Haryana, (1996) 2 SCC 66 · Avon Services Production Agencies v. Industrial Tribunal, Haryana, AIR 1979 SC 170 · Nagalinga Nadar Sons v. Headload Workers' Union, AIR 1951 SC 203

Sultan Singh: no hearing need be given to the employer before the Government makes a reference, since no rights are decided and the employer gets a full hearing before the Tribunal. Avon Services: an earlier refusal does not exhaust the power — the Government may later refer the same dispute, on fresh material or on reconsideration, without notice to the employer. Nagalinga Nadar: the reference being administrative, audi alteram partem is not attracted. Note the High Court conflict Jain records: Escorts Ltd. v. Industrial Tribunal, Haryana, 1983 LIC 223 and Electronics Ltd. v. State of Haryana, 1994 II LLJ 29 (hearing required before a reference made after an earlier refusal) v. Goodyear India (Raj), American Express (Cal) and G. Muthukrishnan (Mad FB) (no hearing) — the Supreme Court line (Avon, Sultan Singh) supports the latter.

CASE — Western India Match Co. Ltd. v. Western India Match Co. Workers Union, AIR 1970 SC 1205

The Government refused a reference in 1962 and, on a fresh application, referred the same dispute in 1968. Held: the words "at any time" in s.10(1) impose no time-limit and a refusal is not a final adjudication; the Government may reconsider and refer, though it should not refer a dispute so stale as to be dead. Use: reconsideration power; the same logic explains why the Supreme Court in Telco could send the matter back "for reconsideration".

CASE — Delhi Cloth & General Mills Co. v. Shambhu Nath Mukherjee, AIR 1978 SC 8

Several workmen struck off the rolls for absence; the Government referred the disputes of some and refused others in identical situations. Held: an unexplained differential refusal is discriminatory and violates Article 14; the reference was directed. Use: adds a constitutional ground to the administrative-law grounds of review; also relevant to a Central Government that refers one company's multi-State dispute to the NIT but refuses an identical one.

CASE — State of Bihar v. D.N. Ganguly, AIR 1958 SC 1018 (and State of Maharashtra v. K.E. Union, AIR 1955 SC 635)

Facts (Ganguly): After referring a dispute about dismissed workmen of a Jamshedpur company, the Government cancelled the reference (on a settlement with some workmen) and made a fresh one. Held: the IDA gives no power to cancel or supersede a reference; s.21 General Clauses Act does not apply because the Act discloses a contrary intention (rights attach on reference; s.33B is the only withdrawal-and-transfer power); the cancellation and the second reference were invalid; the Tribunal must proceed to an award and may record a settlement as its award. K.E. Union: a second reference of different or unconnected matters during pendency is neither a withdrawal nor a supersession and is valid. Code: same result under s.54/s.92 — the Central Government may transfer to the NIT (s.92(2)) or between Tribunals (s.92(1)), but not recall a reference.

CASE — Nedungadi Bank Ltd. v. K.P. Madhavankutty, (2000) 2 SCC 455 · Shalimar Works Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1959 SC 1217 · Ajaib Singh v. Sirhind Co-op. Society, (1999) 6 SCC 82

Shalimar Works: no period of limitation governs a reference. Ajaib Singh: the Limitation Act does not apply to references, but delay may affect relief. Nedungadi Bank: a reference made seven years after dismissal (the workman having accepted the decision) was of a "dead" dispute and was quashed — "the Government must be satisfied that a dispute exists". Code: the problem is now met by statute — Form II applications within 90 days of the failure report (s.53(6); R.23(9)); no conciliation of an individual dispute after two years (s.53(1) proviso); no limitation for a s.54 reference, so Nedungadi still applies to it.

CASE — Sindhu Resettlement Corporation Ltd. v. Industrial Tribunal of Gujarat, (1968) I LLJ 834 (SC) (quoted in TISCO ¶16)

A retrenched employee and his union demanded only retrenchment compensation from the management, but asked the Government to refer a dispute about reinstatement. Held: "If no dispute at all is raised by the employees with the management, any request sent by them to the Government would only be a demand by them and not an industrial dispute between them and their employer"; a reference of reinstatement, never demanded, was incompetent. Principles: (i) a demand on the employer and its refusal (express or implied) is the sine qua non of an "industrial dispute"; (ii) the reference cannot enlarge the dispute beyond what was raised; (iii) the Government's opinion must rest on the dispute that actually exists. Code: the same logic governs what a party may put in a Form II application (the dispute that went through conciliation) and what the Central Government may refer under s.54.

CASE — Bombay Gumasta Union v. M.R. Bhope, 1995 II CLR 453 (Bom) · Indian Paper Pulp Co. Ltd. v. Workers' Union, AIR 1949 FC 148 · British India Corporation v. Industrial Tribunal, Punjab, AIR 1957 SC 354

Bhope: the Government "can add, amend or modify the reference without cancelling or withdrawing the original reference" and correct clerical mistakes. Indian Paper Pulp: "a mere defect in drafting will not invalidate the reference" — but the order must be self-contained (reasons cannot later be supplemented: Commissioner of Police v. Gordhandas Bhanji, AIR 1952 SC 16). British India Corporation: the person alleging mala fides in a reference bears the burden. Use: together these define the curable end of the defective-reference spectrum (§14).

PART III · 11. Jurisdiction of adjudicatory authorities — sources and kinds

"Jurisdiction" of an industrial adjudicator has to be examined on five axes. The DU cases speak to the first and the fourth (TISCO ¶18: the Tribunal "is a creature of that statute. It acquires jurisdiction on the basis of reference made to it"; Telco/Sharad Kumar: jurisdictional facts are for the Tribunal); the Code supplies the rest.

1 · Source (how the matter comes in)

Tribunal (s.44): Form II application after conciliation failure s.53(6)/R.23(9) · individual grievance route s.4 · Trade-Union disputes s.22 · approval applications s.90(2) proviso · complaints s.91 · money claims s.59(2) · interpretation reference s.95 · transfers s.92(1),(3) · TU appeals s.10, standing-orders appeals (Ch. IV) · EPF appeals (Finance Act 2017; Code s.102). NIT (s.46): only by s.54 reference, s.92(2) transfer, s.95, or s.91 in a pending matter. Arbitrator (s.42): only by agreement.

2 · Subject-matter

An "industrial dispute" s.2(q) in an "industry" s.2(p) between "employer" s.2(m) and "worker" s.2(zr), or the specific matters listed in axis 1. Excluded: matters of purely civil/contractual nature outside the Code (but s.97 bars civil courts wherever the Code applies); sovereign functions, domestic service, charitable institutions etc. excluded from "industry".

3 · Territorial / "which Government"

Central v. State Tribunal fixed by the appropriate Government s.2(b); within a Government's sphere by the notification constituting the Tribunal for an area (e.g., 22 Central Government Industrial Tribunals; State Tribunals district/zone-wise); the Central Government may empower a State Tribunal for Central matters s.92(3). Cause of action = where the worker worked / the order took effect.

4 · Scope (how far it may go)

Confined to the points in the reference/application and matters incidental thereto (IDA s.10(4) principle — DCGM, Express Newspapers, National Engineering, TISCO); but full power to decide its own jurisdictional facts (Telco, Sharad Kumar), to grant interim relief s.50 (Hotel Imperial), to re-appraise punishment s.50, to award costs, to execute s.49.

5 · Time & composition

Limitation: 90 days from the failure report (Form II); 2 years from the date the individual dispute arose (s.53(1) proviso; s.4); none for s.54 references (but Nedungadi). Composition: two-member bench mandatory for s.44(7) matters (standing orders; discharge/dismissal; legality of strike/lock-out; retrenchment/closure; TU disputes); otherwise a single member may sit; disqualification/age s.48; difference of opinion s.47.
s.44 — Industrial Tribunal (the ordinary adjudicator) — sub-section map relevant to jurisdiction
  • 44(1) — the appropriate Government may, by notification, constitute one or more Industrial Tribunals for the adjudication of industrial disputes and for performing such other functions as may be assigned to them under the Code (the "other functions" are the axis-1 list above). Central Tribunals continue to function as EPF appellate tribunals (s.102).
  • 44(2)–(6) — two members: Judicial Member (District Judge / High Court Judge qualifications) and Administrative Member (Deputy Secretary/Joint Secretary rank etc.); qualifications, appointment, salaries and terms — for Central Tribunals per the Finance Act, 2017 s.184 / Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 framework; for State Tribunals per State rules.
  • 44(7) — matters that shall be heard by a bench of both members: (i) standing orders; (ii) discharge or dismissal of a worker including reinstatement/back-wages; (iii) legality of a strike or lock-out; (iv) retrenchment/closure (Chs. IX–X); (v) Trade-Union disputes (s.22); other matters may be heard by a single member as the Government notifies — a composition limb of jurisdiction; an award of a single member in a dismissal case is without jurisdiction.
  • 44(8)–(9) — procedure for distribution of business and for vacancies (proceedings continue).
  • 45 — decisions final. 47 — difference of opinion → notified Judicial Member. 48 — disqualifications. 49 — procedure/powers. 50 — relief and interim relief. 51 — pending IDA proceedings transferred to the corresponding Tribunal.
RELATED LAW — the "appropriate Government" (s.2(b)) as a jurisdictional fact

The Central Government is the appropriate Government for: establishments carried on by or under its authority; railways (including metro railways); mines; oil fields; major ports; air transport services; telecommunication; banking and insurance companies; corporations or authorities established by a Central Act; Central public-sector undertakings and their subsidiaries/autonomous bodies (and the establishments of contractors for these); the State Government for all others. Consequences for Unit 2: (i) an application under s.53(6) must go to the Tribunal constituted by the right Government — a State Tribunal's award in a banking dispute is a nullity (contrast the Central Government's power to empower a State Tribunal, s.92(3)); (ii) only the appropriate Government can transfer under s.92(1) or refer under s.95; (iii) the s.54 power belongs to the Central Government irrespective of who the appropriate Government is (the IDA said so expressly; the Code achieves it through ss.46 and 92(2)). Leading IDA authorities on the concept: Heavy Engineering Mazdoor Union v. State of Bihar, AIR 1970 SC 82 (company not "under the authority of" the Central Government merely because wholly owned) and Steel Authority of India v. National Union Waterfront Workers, (2001) 7 SCC 1 — the Code's list now settles most of these questions.

Real example — the Central Tribunal map

The Central Government has for decades run Central Government Industrial Tribunal-cum-Labour Courts at about twenty-two seats (e.g., New Delhi — two, Mumbai — two, Kolkata, Chennai, Dhanbad — two, Jabalpur, Chandigarh, Kanpur, Lucknow, Bangalore, Hyderabad, Bhubaneswar, Guwahati, Ahmedabad, Jaipur, Nagpur, Asansol, Ernakulam). Under s.44 they continue as Industrial Tribunals for banking, mining, ports, PSU and other Central-sphere disputes; each also hears EPF appeals. A dispute of the workers of a public-sector bank branch in Ghaziabad therefore goes to the Central Tribunal with territorial jurisdiction over western U.P. (not to the U.P. State Tribunal), while the dispute of a private hospital's employees in Ghaziabad goes to the U.P. Tribunal. If the Central Government thinks the banking dispute raises an all-India question (e.g., the status of "business correspondents"), it may refer it to the NIT (s.54) or, if already pending, withdraw it to the NIT (s.92(2)).

12. Scope of adjudication — "points of dispute" and "matters incidental thereto"

IDA s.10(4) said that where the Government has specified the points of dispute, the adjudicator "shall confine its adjudication to those points and matters incidental thereto". The Code has no verbatim s.10(4), but the rule survives in three ways: (i) for the NIT — the s.54 order of reference defines the dispute; (ii) for the Tribunal — the Form II application and reply define the lis (a Tribunal is "a creature of the statute" that cannot adjudicate what is not before it — TISCO ¶18; National Engineering Industries v. State of Rajasthan, (2000) 1 SCC 371); (iii) as a principle of natural justice — no party can be condemned on a case it was never called upon to meet. The content of "incidental" comes from the cases.

CASE — Delhi Cloth & General Mills Co. Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1967 SC 469 (former DU case 6 — not in the 2026 list but indispensable)
Facts
Two units of the company — the Delhi Cloth Mills and the Swatantra Bharat Mills — saw work stoppages in 1962–63. The management's case was that the workmen went on a strike at DCM and a sit-down strike at SBM; it declared a lock-out at DCM. The Government referred several items to the Industrial Tribunal, the material terms being framed on the footing that there had been a "strike" at both units and a "lock-out" at DCM, and asking whether the strikes were justified/legal and whether the workmen were entitled to wages for the lock-out period at DCM and for the sit-down-strike period at SBM. Before the Tribunal the workmen contended that there had been no strike at all but a lock-out/closure from the start; the Tribunal examined that question and held partly in their favour.
Issue
Under s.10(4), could the Tribunal, on a reference that assumed a strike and a lock-out, go into whether there was a strike at all — was that a "matter incidental" to the points referred?
Held
No. "Incidental" means something that is ancillary or subordinate to the point referred, an adjunct; it cannot mean a question that goes to the root of the reference and converts it into something else. The reference having been made on the basis that there was a strike at DCM and a sit-down strike at SBM, and a lock-out at DCM, "the Tribunal had to examine Issues 3 and 4 on the basis that there was a strike … and that there was a lockout … It was for the Tribunal to examine the evidence only on the question as to whether the strikes were justified and legal", and then decide the wage claims. Whether the "strike" was in truth a lock-out or closure was not incidental — "under Section 10(4) of the Act it is not competent to the Tribunal to entertain such a question". The Court also held that while the Tribunal may look at the pleadings to understand the points referred, it cannot use them to enlarge the reference. The Court applied Burma-Shell Oil Storage v. Their Workmen, (1961) II LLJ 124 (SC) (clerical-staff grievances not included in the reference → no authority to adjudicate them) and distinguished the two cases the unions relied on: Express Newspapers (there, whether the action was a closure or a lock-out went to the Tribunal's very jurisdiction and the reference left it open) and Syndicate Bank v. Workmen (where the question whether certain employees were "workmen" was implicit in the reference, the Tribunal could decide it) — here the Tribunal plainly had jurisdiction and the workers were merely trying to re-describe the events on which the reference was founded.
Principle
(1) The adjudicator's jurisdiction is bounded by the terms of reference; "incidental" matters are those necessary to answer the referred points, not matters that alter their premise. (2) Where the Government has framed the reference on an assumed state of facts, that assumption binds the Tribunal — which is precisely why the party prejudiced by the assumption must attack the reference itself (ITDC, Moolchand, TISCO). (3) Corollary for the Government: draft neutrally (Express Newspapers). DCGM is thus the mirror image of TISCO: DCGM shows the consequence for the Tribunal; TISCO the consequence for the Government.
Code
Applies directly to s.54 references. For Form II applications, the Tribunal frames issues from the pleadings, so a lock-out v. closure dispute should be framed as "whether the stoppage was a lock-out or a closure, and if a lock-out, whether it was legal/justified and what wages are payable" — the neutral formulation the DU cases require.

Within jurisdiction — checklist of "incidental" matters
  • Deciding its own jurisdictional facts: is the applicant a "worker"? is the respondent the "employer"? is the activity an "industry"? (Telco, Sharad Kumar, TISCO ¶11 — the Tribunal, not the Government, decides.)
  • Consequential relief: back-wages, continuity, compensation in lieu of reinstatement, interest; interim relief (Hotel Imperial; s.50).
  • Sub-questions necessary to answer the referred point (fitment, classification, computation — Precision Bearings).
  • Validity/fairness of a domestic enquiry and re-appraisal of punishment in a dismissal reference (s.50; IDA s.11A).
  • Whether a settlement pleaded in defence binds the workmen (National Engineering).
  • Impleading a necessary party (Lafarge in TISCO); binding parties "summoned to appear" (s.57(3)(b)).
Outside jurisdiction
  • Re-opening a fact the reference assumes (strike/lock-out/closure/transfer) — DCGM, Pottery Mazdoor; the remedy is to attack the reference (TISCO).
  • A demand never raised on the employer or never referred (Sindhu, Jaipur Udyog).
  • Granting relief to persons not covered by the reference/application (except through s.57(3)(d) collective effect).
  • Deciding a matter pending before the NIT (IDA s.10(6); Code s.92(2) transfer) or one reserved to another authority (e.g., certifying officer's functions; Registrar's functions).
  • Adjudicating a second time a dispute already covered by a binding award/settlement in operation (s.57–58), unless the reference is about its interpretation (s.95) or breach.

Jurisdictional facts
Facts on which the existence of jurisdiction depends — status of parties, existence of an industrial dispute, existence of an "industry", the appropriate Government. Under the IDA the Government took a prima facie view for reference; the Tribunal decided finally (Dharangadhra Chemical Works v. State of Saurashtra, AIR 1957 SC 264 — "workman" status is a question of fact for the Tribunal). Under the Code the Tribunal decides from the start; a s.54 reference does not conclusively establish them either (TISCO — Lafarge/TISCO status remained open on a properly worded reference).
Preliminary objections
Permitted (the Tribunal may frame a preliminary issue), but the Supreme Court has repeatedly deprecated the practice of deciding preliminary issues first and then approaching the High Court, which "prolongs the life of industrial adjudication" (Express Newspapers; D.P. Maheshwari: "it was just the sort of dispute which should not have been allowed to be decided as a preliminary issue"). Hindustan Lever and River Steam Navigation hold that hyper-technical objections to the wording of a reference must not be entertained where the real dispute is clear. TISCO shows the exception: where the wording forecloses the defence, the objection goes to the root and the writ court intervenes.
Transfers
s.92(1) appropriate Government between Tribunals (reasons; de novo or from the stage); s.92(2) Central Government from any Tribunal to the NIT; s.92(3) empowering a State Tribunal for Central matters; s.51 transitional transfer of IDA proceedings; s.47 reference of a point of difference to another Judicial Member. IDA s.33B and D.N. Ganguly: transfer is the only permitted form of "withdrawal".
NIT v. Tribunal
IDA s.10(6): on an NIT reference, Labour Court/Tribunal proceedings on the same matter stood quashed and no fresh reference could be made — an ouster. Code: no automatic ouster; concurrent proceedings are avoided by a reasoned s.92(2) transfer; the NIT's award binds like any award (s.57) and its enforceability may be postponed only by the Central Government (s.55).
Civil courts
s.97: "No civil court shall have jurisdiction in respect of any matter to which any provision of this Code applies and no injunction shall be granted by any civil court in respect of anything which is done or intended to be done by or under this Code." IDA background: Premier Automobiles Ltd. v. K.S. Wadke, AIR 1975 SC 2238 (rights created by the Act enforceable only in the Act's forums; common-law rights in civil courts); Rajasthan SRTC v. Krishna Kant, (1995) 5 SCC 75 and Jitendra Nath Biswas v. Empire of India & Ceylon Tea Co., (1989) 3 SCC 582 (dismissal disputes governed by standing orders/IDA go to the industrial forum). The Code's express bar makes the industrial forum exclusive.
High Courts
Arts. 226/227 remain; the NIT enjoys no immunity (FDC ¶11). The appellate route under Art. 136 lies against Tribunal/NIT awards (Bharat Bank) but is exercised sparingly; against the Government's administrative orders the remedy is Art. 226 first.
Interpretation
s.95: doubts about the meaning of an award or settlement are referred by the appropriate Government to a Tribunal/NIT, which decides after hearing the parties; decision final and binding (IDA s.36A) — the Tribunal cannot use this to re-write the award.

PART IV · 14. Defective reference — concept, typology, consequences and cure

A reference is "defective" when the order that is supposed to confer jurisdiction on the adjudicator and define the lis fails to do one or both of those jobs correctly. Because the adjudicator "acquires jurisdiction on the basis of reference made to it" and "cannot travel beyond the same" (TISCO ¶18), a defect in the reference is not a mere irregularity: it either deprives the tribunal of jurisdiction, distorts the dispute it must decide, or shuts out a party's case. The DU material illustrates three species — the vague/general reference (Sarathy), the reference to a forum whose statutory conditions are not met (FDC), and the reference that presupposes the very fact in dispute (ITDC, Moolchand, TISCO). 15. Case 7 — M/s Tata Iron & Steel Co. Ltd. v. State of Jharkhand & Ors., (2014) 1 SCC 536 — DU material pp. 45–50

CASE — Supreme Court · K.S. Radhakrishnan & A.K. Sikri, JJ. (judgment by Sikri, J.) · appeals by special leave from the Jharkhand High Court (Single Judge and Division Bench)
Facts
TISCO (now Tata Steel Ltd.), besides steel, ran a cement division. Following a disinvestment policy it sold the cement division to Lafarge India Pvt. Ltd. by a Business Transfer Agreement dated 9.3.1999, effective 1.11.1999. The BTA provided that Lafarge would take over the company personnel "in terms of Section 25FF" of the IDA on the statutory conditions: (a) service not interrupted by the transfer; (b) terms after transfer not less favourable; (c) the purchaser liable to pay retrenchment compensation on the basis of continuous service (¶3). The decision was communicated to the cement-division employees; according to TISCO, Lafarge issued them fresh letters of appointment and they started working with Lafarge — including respondents 8–82 (¶4). Dissatisfied with conditions at Lafarge, 74 workmen submitted a statement of demand to TISCO on 15.9.2003: they had been "directed to work with M/s Lafarge without taking their consent", on the impression that it was a temporary posting to a unit that was "a part of the appellant organization" after which they would return to the parent department; Lafarge had not given them all their earlier benefits; they demanded to be taken back by TISCO (¶5). TISCO ignored the demand; the workmen approached the Deputy Labour Commissioner, Jamshedpur. In conciliation TISCO pleaded that from 1.11.1999 the cement division belonged to Lafarge, the workmen had become Lafarge's employees under fresh appointment letters and had ceased to be TISCO's employees (¶6). Conciliation failed; on the failure report the Government of Jharkhand made two references under s.10(1) to the Labour Court, Jamshedpur, in identical terms: "Whether not to take back Shri K. Chandrashekhar Rao and 73 other workmen (list enclosed) of M/s TISCO Limited, Jamshedpur in service by their own TISCO Management after their transfer to M/s Lafarge India Limited, is justified? If not what relief they are entitled to?" (¶6). TISCO's writ petitions to quash the references were dismissed by a Single Judge (the Labour Court "can very well adjudicate and answer the reference after considering all the points raised by the parties") and the intra-court appeals were dismissed by the Division Bench ("as there is a dispute between parties") (¶8).
Issues
(1) Did an "industrial dispute" exist between TISCO and the workmen at all, given TISCO's case that they were no longer its employees? (2) Were the terms of reference, which described the workmen as "workmen of M/s TISCO" and spoke of "their transfer to M/s Lafarge", appropriately worded — or did they presuppose the very facts in dispute and shut out TISCO's defence? (3) What is the duty of the appropriate Government in drafting a reference, having regard to s.10(4)? (4) What relief follows when a reference is defectively worded?
Appellant TISCO's arguments
(i) The workmen were not in its employment and could not raise a dispute against it; "no industrial dispute at all existed between the appellant and the respondent workmen" (¶7). (ii) If Lafarge failed to honour the assured terms, the workmen's dispute lay only against Lafarge, "which was their real employer and … was not even made a party" (¶7). (iii) The Conciliation Officer had not considered the material and submitted the failure report without applying his mind (¶7). (iv) "The manner in which the references are worded, do not depict the true nature of the dispute": they assume that the workmen are TISCO's employees who were merely "transferred", and confine the Labour Court to whether TISCO must take them back (¶7, ¶12).
Respondent workmen's / State's position (as recorded)
They "continued to be the workers of the appellant and were wrongly 'transferred' to M/s Lafarge" without consent, on a representation that the posting was temporary; therefore they were entitled to serve TISCO (¶5, ¶10). The State/High Court view: a dispute plainly existed; the Labour Court could consider all points and evidence in the reference proceedings; writ interference at the threshold was unwarranted (¶8).
Decision & reasoning
(a) A dispute exists and the Government could not decide it. "The High Court is right in holding that the Industrial Dispute has arisen" — the workers assert they remain TISCO's workers wrongly transferred; TISCO says the relationship "ceased to exist"; "this obviously is the 'dispute' within the meaning of Section 2(k)" (¶10). "Here, the appellant is denying the respondents to be its workmen. On the other hand, respondents are asserting that they continue to be the employees … This itself would be a 'dispute' which has to be determined by means of adjudication. Once these respective contentions were raised before the Labour Department, it was not within the powers of the Labour Department/appropriate Government [to] decide this dispute and assume the adjudicatory role as its role is confined to discharge administrative function of referring the matter … Of course, in a dispute like this, M/s Lafarge also becomes a necessary party" (¶11). (b) But the terms of reference are defective. They "do not reflect the real dispute between the parties. The reference pre-supposes that the respondents workmen are the employees of the appellant. The reference also proceeds on the foundation that their services have been 'transferred' to M/s Lafarge. On these suppositions the limited scope of adjudication is confined to decide as to whether appellant is under an obligation to take back these workmen … It not only depicts the version of the respondents workmen, but in fact accepts the same … the appellant's right to put it as its defence, as a demurrer, is altogether shut and taken away, in the manner the references are worded" (¶12). "Though the jurisdiction of the Tribunal is confined to the terms of reference, … it is empowered to go into the incidental issues. Had the reference been appropriately worded … probably it was still open to the appellant to contend and prove that the Respondent workmen ceased to be their employees. However, the reference in the present form does not leave that scope for the appellant at all" (¶13). (c) Precedents. ITDC v. Delhi Administration, 1982 Lab IC 1309 (Del FB): reference of "wages for a period of lock-out w.e.f. 1.1.81" quashed because the real dispute — lock-out or closure after workers' violence at the Sona Rupa restaurant — was assumed away, "the jurisdiction of the Court/Industrial Tribunal … is limited to the points specifically referred … and the matters incidental thereto" (¶14–¶15). Moolchand Kharati Ram Hospital v. Labour Commissioner, 1998 (III) LLJ 1139 (Del), affirmed by the Supreme Court, 2002 (10) SCC 708: "wages for the lock-out period" reference quashed where the management's case was a strike (¶16–¶17) — quoting Express Newspapers (Government "should frame the relevant orders of reference carefully … so worded as to leave no scope for ambiguity or controversy") and Sindhu Resettlement (a request to the Government without a demand on the management "would only be a demand … and not an industrial dispute"; a reference of reinstatement where only compensation was demanded "is not competent") (¶16). National Engineering Industries v. State of Rajasthan, (2000) 1 SCC 371: the Tribunal "is a creature of that statute. It acquires jurisdiction on the basis of reference made to it … has to confine itself within the scope of the subject matter of reference and cannot travel beyond the same" (¶18). (d) The Government's duty. "It is for this reason that it becomes the bounden duty of the appropriate Government to make the reference appropriately which is reflective of the real/exact nature of 'dispute' between the parties" (¶19). The real questions here: (i) were the workmen simply transferred by TISCO to Lafarge, or were their services taken over by Lafarge so that they became Lafarge's employees? (ii) if their service conditions were not protected by Lafarge, do they have a right to rejoin TISCO? (iii) if their conditions were violated, can they claim protection from Lafarge or return to TISCO? (¶19). (e) Conclusion. "The reference in the present form is clearly defective as it does not take care of the correct and precise nature of the dispute … the manner in which the reference is worded shows that it has already been decided that the respondent workmen continue to be the employees of the appellant and further that their services were simply transferred … This shall preclude the appellant to put forth and prove its case … It also implies that by presuming so, the appropriate Government has itself decided those contentious issues and assumed the role of an adjudicator which is, otherwise, reserved for the Labour Court/Industrial Tribunal" (¶20). Order: appeals allowed; High Court judgment set aside; references "in the present form" quashed; the appropriate Government directed "to make fresh reference, incorporating real essence of the dispute as discussed in this judgment, within a period of two months" (¶21–¶22).
Principles evolved
  1. The Government's role is administrative: when the employer denies the employment relationship and the workmen assert it, that is the industrial dispute — the Government must refer it, not decide it (continuity with Telco, Sharad Kumar).
  2. Corollary: the Government must not decide it by drafting either. A reference that presupposes a contested jurisdictional fact (employment, transfer, lock-out, strike) is "defective" because, s.10(4)-bound, the Tribunal cannot re-open the assumption (DCGM, Pottery Mazdoor) and the party's defence "as a demurrer" is "shut and taken away".
  3. "Bounden duty" of the appropriate Government to word the reference so as to reflect the "real/exact nature" of the dispute (Express Newspapers elevated to a duty).
  4. All necessary parties (the alleged successor-employer) must be before the Tribunal.
  5. Remedy: quash the reference in its existing form and direct a fresh, neutral reference within a time-limit — the dispute itself is not killed; the High Court should intervene at the threshold where the defect goes to the root, notwithstanding the general reluctance to interfere with pending references.
  6. The Tribunal's jurisdiction is derivative ("creature of the statute … acquires jurisdiction on the basis of reference") and bounded ("cannot travel beyond") — the syllabus head (b) principle stated in its modern form.
Under the Code
(i) For s.54 references the rule is identical: the Central Government's order must state the dispute neutrally ("whether the respondents continued to be workers of X after the transfer of the undertaking to Y, and if so, to what relief they are entitled") and name both X and Y. (ii) For ordinary disputes the workmen would apply in Form II after conciliation; TISCO's defence (transfer of undertaking under s.73 of the Code — successor to IDA s.25FF — with fresh appointment letters) would be pleaded in reply, and the Tribunal must frame the issues neutrally: "Whether the applicants ceased to be employees of TISCO on 1.11.1999 by virtue of the transfer of the cement division to Lafarge?" — the TISCO duty migrates from the Government to the adjudicator, and Lafarge is impleaded under s.49. (iii) Substantively, the Code's s.73 (compensation on transfer of undertaking, with the same three conditions as s.25FF) and s.2(zh) (retrenchment) decide the merits — mention them for the "related law" marks.
Exam use
The lead case for syllabus head (c) and the modern statement of head (b). Structure: dispute exists (¶10–11) → Government cannot decide (¶11) → but wording pre-judges (¶12–13) → precedents (¶14–18) → duty (¶19) → defect & remedy (¶20–21). Always add the DCGM mirror-image point (why the assumption binds the Tribunal) — it explains why the wording matters.
CASE — Indian Tourism Development Corporation v. Delhi Administration, 1982 Lab IC 1309 (Del FB) (as narrated in TISCO ¶14–15)

Facts: A worker at the sweets counter of ITDC's Sona Rupa Restaurant was caught misappropriating sale proceeds; after initially admitting the theft he instigated other employees to "militant and violent acts" and abstention from work. The management decided to close down the restaurant, informed the workmen, and issued a closure notice offering full and final settlement. The workmen raised a dispute alleging a lock-out; the management in conciliation said it was a closure. The Government referred: "Whether the workmen … are entitled to wages for a period of lock-out w.e.f. 1.1.81 and if so, what directions are necessary". Held (Full Bench): the real dispute — whether there was a lock-out at all or a closure prompted by the workers' violence — "had not been referred to. Instead lock-out was presumed in the reference itself on imaginating and fictitious basis"; since the Tribunal's jurisdiction "is limited to the points specifically referred … and the matters incidental thereto", the management was "precluded from proving … that there was no lock out". Reference quashed. Use: the first authority for type-2 defect; the fact pattern of May–June 2025 Q.4 and the older C.L.C./L.C.II "closure not lock-out" problems.

CASE — Moolchand Kharati Ram Hospital v. Labour Commissioner, 1998 (III) LLJ 1139 (Del); appeal dismissed, (2002) 10 SCC 708 (as narrated in TISCO ¶16–17)

Facts: The management said the workmen had gone on strike; the workmen said the management had declared a lock-out. The reference asked only "whether the workmen were entitled to wages for the lock-out period". Held: since the existence of a lock-out was itself disputed, "this kind of reference would not permit the management to prove that it was in fact a case of 'strike'"; following ITDC, the reference was quashed. The Court quoted Express Newspapers on careful drafting and Sindhu Resettlement on the need for an actual demand. The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal — which, TISCO says, "shows that view of the Delhi High Court … has been given imprimatur by this Court" (¶17). Use: second authority for type-2; shows the defect is symmetrical (it can prejudice workmen too, if a reference presumed a "strike").

16. Drafting a valid reference / application — checklist and the adjudicator's approach

Checklist for the Central Government drafting a s.54 order (or a Government drafting any reference)
  1. Identify the dispute that actually exists from the demand, the conciliation record and the failure report (Sindhu; Jaipur Udyog). Do not add items never demanded; do not omit items demanded without a conscious decision (Precision Bearings).
  2. Record the s.54 opinion — which condition (national importance / multi-State establishments) and on what material (FDC).
  3. Name all parties — every employer against whom relief is sought, including an alleged successor (TISCO); representative unions; where establishments other than the disputants are to be bound, say so (Sarathy; s.57(3)).
  4. Word each point neutrally: "whether the stoppage from … was a lock-out or a closure; if a lock-out, whether legal and justified and what wages are payable" (not "wages for the lock-out period" — ITDC, Moolchand); "whether the applicants continued in the employment of X after …" (not "workmen of X … after their transfer" — TISCO); "whether the persons named are workers of X and, if so, whether they are entitled to permanency" (Telco).
  5. Include the status question expressly when disputed (Sharad Kumar ¶32: "including the question whether the appellant is a workman").
  6. Add the relief limb ("if not, to what relief are they entitled") so that consequential/incidental relief is clearly within scope (Hotel Imperial; s.50).
  7. Fix the period for the award (s.54(2)) and make the order self-contained (Gordhandas Bhanji).
  8. Avoid legal conclusions in the terms ("illegal strike", "wrongful dismissal", "transfer") — describe events, let the Tribunal characterise them (DCGM).
The adjudicator's approach to an alleged defect (what the NIT/Tribunal should do)
  1. Read the reference/application purposively with the demand and the failure report; refuse hyper-technical objections (Sarathy, ITC Monghyr, Hindustan Lever, River Steam).
  2. Decide jurisdictional facts (worker, employer, industry, appropriate Government, limitation) itself on evidence — never treat the Government's or applicant's label as conclusive (Telco, Sharad Kumar, Dharangadhra).
  3. Where the terms assume a contested fact, recognise that it cannot re-open the assumption (DCGM, Pottery Mazdoor) — and say so early, so that the prejudiced party can seek amendment or a writ (TISCO) rather than discover the problem at the award stage.
  4. On a Form II application, frame issues neutrally after the reply; implead necessary parties (s.49; s.57(3)(b)); allow amendment of pleadings within the conciliated dispute.
  5. Avoid piecemeal preliminary awards; decide preliminary issues with the merits unless the objection goes to the root (D.P. Maheshwari).
  6. Grant incidental relief (interim relief, consequential benefits) but not relief on demands the Government deliberately excluded or the parties never raised (Hotel Imperial v. Precision Bearings).
  7. Record any settlement reached during pendency as an award (D.N. Ganguly; s.57(3)).
Worked drafting example — the TISCO dispute redrafted for a s.54/s.92(2) NIT order or as Tribunal issues

Defective (actual): "Whether not to take back Shri K. Chandrashekhar Rao and 73 other workmen (list enclosed) of M/s TISCO Limited, Jamshedpur in service by their own TISCO Management after their transfer to M/s Lafarge India Limited, is justified? If not what relief they are entitled to?"

Neutral redraft: "(1) Whether, upon the transfer of the cement division of Tata Steel Ltd. to Lafarge India Pvt. Ltd. with effect from 1.11.1999, the 74 persons named in the Annexure ceased to be employees of Tata Steel Ltd. and became employees of Lafarge India Pvt. Ltd.; (2) if they did not so cease, whether the refusal of Tata Steel Ltd. to take them back in service is justified; (3) if they did so cease, whether Lafarge India Pvt. Ltd. has failed to provide them the conditions of service protected under the transfer, and (4) in either case, to what relief are the said persons entitled and against whom?" — Parties: the 74 workmen/union; Tata Steel Ltd.; Lafarge India Pvt. Ltd. — This reflects the three questions the Supreme Court identified in ¶19 and leaves the "transfer v. take-over" issue genuinely open.

18. DU PYQs 2024–2025 with model answers

Dec 2024 · Q.1 · 15 marks (problem) · text reconstructed from topic analysis Sheetal worked for eight years as a "Senior Sales Executive" in a pharmaceutical company and was dismissed after a domestic inquiry for alleged misconduct. Her union raised a dispute; conciliation failed. The appropriate Government, acting on the opinion of its Law Department, declined to refer the dispute to the Labour Court, recording that (i) Sheetal, drawing ₹45,000 per month and supervising two junior salesmen, was not a "workman", and (ii) in the light of the legal opinion it would not be "just and appropriate" to make a reference. Advise Sheetal. Would your answer differ under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020?

Issues

(1) Can the appropriate Government, in deciding whether to refer under IDA s.10(1) read with s.12(5), decide that Sheetal is not a "workman"? (2) Is a refusal founded on a Law Department opinion that a reference would not be "just and appropriate" a valid exercise of the power? (3) What are her remedies? (4) How is the position altered by the Code?

Law

The referring power is administrative and rests on the Government's prima facie opinion that an industrial dispute exists or is apprehended (State of Madras v. C.P. Sarathy, AIR 1953 SC 53). Section 12(5) requires the Government, on the failure report, to consider whether "there is a case for reference" and, if it declines, to record and communicate reasons; those reasons must be germane — a refusal on extraneous or punitive considerations is quashed and the Government directed to reconsider (State of Bombay v. K.P. Krishnan, AIR 1960 SC 1223). The Government may weigh whether a claim is frivolous, perverse or stale (Bombay Union of Journalists v. State of Bombay, AIR 1964 SC 1617; M.P. Irrigation Karamchari Sangh, (1985) 2 SCC 103), but it "cannot delve into the merits of the dispute and take upon itself the determination of the lis" (Telco Convoy Drivers Mazdoor Sangh v. State of Bihar, (1989) 3 SCC 271, ¶12). Whether the claimant is a "workman" is, when disputed, exactly such a question: in Telco the Government's refusal on a 1973 Law Department opinion that the convoy drivers were not TELCO's workmen was held to be an impermissible adjudication and the Supreme Court directed a reference; in Sharad Kumar v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi, (2002) 4 SCC 490 the refusal because an "Area Sales Executive" was not a workman was quashed — "designation of the employee is not of much importance and certainly not conclusive"; the test is the nature of the duties (principal v. ancillary), which "requires examination of factual matters for which materials including oral evidence will have to be considered", so the Government "could not arrogate on to itself the power to adjudicate" (¶20, ¶31). Only where the record leaves no room for doubt (an undisputed managerial post) may the Government decline on status (Indian Tea Assn. v. Ajit Kumar Barat, (2000) 3 SCC 93).

Application

Ground (i): Sheetal's status is contested. She is a sales employee — sales promotion employees are workmen by statutory inclusion under the Sales Promotion Employees (Conditions of Service) Act, 1976 — and supervising two juniors does not by itself make her "supervisory" above the wage ceiling or "mainly managerial"; whether her principal duty was selling or managing is a question of evidence (Sharad Kumar; Ananda Bazar Patrika v. Workmen, AIR 1970 SC 1760). The Government has therefore decided the very lis — Telco ¶10 applies squarely, the more so because, as in Telco, the decision rests on an internal legal opinion rather than on the material placed by the union. Ground (ii): "just and appropriate" is not a statutory criterion; a legal opinion that the claim would fail is a merits assessment (Ram Avtar Sharma v. State of Haryana, AIR 1985 SC 915) and an extraneous consideration (K.P. Krishnan); the fairness of the domestic inquiry, if that is what the opinion addressed, is for the Tribunal under s.11A. Neither reason shows the claim to be perverse or frivolous under the narrow M.P. Irrigation exception. Remedies: a writ petition under Art. 226 for certiorari quashing the refusal and mandamus directing the Government to refer — at the first instance the court usually directs reconsideration (K.P. Krishnan), but where the ground is a plain usurpation the court may direct the reference itself "including the question whether [she] is a workman" (Sharad Kumar ¶32; Telco ¶15–16, one month). Alternatively (IDA s.2A(2), 2010 amendment) she could apply directly to the Labour Court 45 days after her conciliation application, within three years, bypassing the Government. No appeal lies to the Supreme Court directly against the Government's order (not a "tribunal"); SLP lies against the High Court's decision.

Under the Code

The answer changes in mechanism, not in principle. There is no Government reference for her dispute: dismissal of an individual worker is an "industrial dispute" (s.2(q)); after the GRC/conciliation stage she or her union applies to the Industrial Tribunal in Form II within 90 days of the failure report (s.53(6); R.23(9)) or under s.4, subject to the two-year limit from dismissal. The Government's opinion about her status or the "appropriateness" of adjudication is legally irrelevant; the Tribunal (two-member bench — s.44(7), dismissal) decides as a jurisdictional fact whether she is a "worker" under s.2(zr), which expressly includes sales promotion employees and excludes only managerial/administrative employees and supervisors drawing above ₹18,000 p.m. — a point she will press but which the Tribunal, not the Government, decides. The Telco/Sharad Kumar principle survives for the one reference that remains — the Central Government's power under s.54 to refer disputes of national importance to the NIT — where the Government still may not decide contested status questions, and where FDC Ltd. v. Union of India (Del 2007) shows that its opinion is reviewable for non-application of mind.

Conclusion

Under the IDA: the refusal is invalid on both grounds; advise a writ for mandamus to refer the dispute including the status question, or the s.2A(2) route. Under the Code: advise a Form II application to the Industrial Tribunal within the 90-day/2-year limits; the gatekeeper has been removed, so the refusal problem cannot arise.

May–June 2025 · Q.4 (problem) · text reconstructed from topic analysis Following violent incidents by some workmen, the management of a restaurant issued a notice on 31 December closing the establishment permanently and offering full and final settlement. The union contended that the management had declared a lock-out. Conciliation failed. The Government referred to the Tribunal: "Whether the workmen are entitled to wages for the period of lock-out w.e.f. 1 January, and if so, what directions are necessary?" The management challenges the reference contending that the real dispute — whether there was a lock-out or a closure — has not been referred and that the Tribunal cannot go into it. Decide. Would the position be different under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020?

Issues

(1) Under IDA s.10(4), can the Tribunal, on a reference worded as above, examine whether the stoppage was a closure rather than a lock-out? (2) If not, is the reference defective and what is the remedy? (3) Was the Government entitled to frame the reference on the assumption of a lock-out? (4) Code position.

Law

Section 10(4) confines the adjudicator "to those points [specified] and matters incidental thereto". "Incidental" means ancillary to the point referred, not a matter that alters its foundation: in Delhi Cloth & General Mills v. Workmen, AIR 1967 SC 469 a reference framed on the basis that there had been a strike and a lock-out bound the Tribunal to that basis — the workers could not deny the strike nor the management the lock-out; "under Section 10(4) … it is not competent to the Tribunal to entertain such a question". Likewise, on a reference whether a closure was justified, the Tribunal cannot inquire whether there was in fact a closure (Pottery Mazdoor Panchayat v. Perfect Pottery Co., AIR 1979 SC 1356). Where, however, the phraseology reasonably leaves the character of the stoppage open, the Tribunal may decide whether it was a lock-out or a closure (Express Newspapers v. Workers, (1962) II LLJ 227 (SC)), and the Court there warned that the Government "should frame the relevant orders of reference carefully … so worded as to leave no scope for ambiguity or controversy". On facts identical to the problem, the Full Bench of the Delhi High Court in ITDC v. Delhi Administration, 1982 Lab IC 1309 quashed a reference of "wages for a period of lock-out w.e.f. 1.1.81" made after the management closed the Sona Rupa restaurant following workers' violence: the real dispute (lock-out or closure) "had not been referred to. Instead lock-out was presumed in the reference itself", precluding the management from proving closure. Moolchand Kharati Ram Hospital v. Labour Commissioner (Del 1998; SC (2002) 10 SCC 708) followed it where the contest was strike v. lock-out. The Supreme Court adopted both in TISCO v. State of Jharkhand, (2014) 1 SCC 536: a reference that presupposes the contested fact "not only depicts the version of the respondents workmen, but in fact accepts the same"; the Government thereby "assumed the role of an adjudicator"; it is "the bounden duty of the appropriate Government to make the reference appropriately which is reflective of the real/exact nature of 'dispute'"; the reference was quashed with a direction to make a fresh reference within two months.

Application

The management's stand (closure) and the union's (lock-out) constitute the industrial dispute (TISCO ¶10–11); the Government could not decide it (Telco) — and by writing "the period of lock-out" it has done precisely that. On this wording the Tribunal is bound to treat the lock-out as a fact (DCGM, Pottery Mazdoor) and may only decide the wages consequent on it; whether there was a closure is not "incidental" but goes to the root. The management's defence "as a demurrer" is "shut and taken away" (TISCO ¶12). Nor is the case one of mere ambiguity curable by construction (Express Newspapers; ITC Monghyr): the words "the period of lock-out" admit of one meaning. The reference is therefore defective (ITDC on all fours). The High Court should quash it and direct a fresh reference in neutral terms — e.g., "whether the stoppage of work from 1 January was a lock-out or a closure; if a lock-out, whether it was legal and justified and what wages, if any, are payable; if a closure, to what compensation are the workmen entitled" — within a fixed time (TISCO ¶21); it should not leave the objection to be decided piecemeal by the Tribunal, because on this wording the Tribunal is powerless. (If the management had instead waited and raised the point before the Tribunal, the Tribunal would have had to answer the reference as framed — DCGM — which is why the challenge must be to the reference itself.) Note the counter-current: courts refuse hyper-technical objections to references (Workmen v. Hindustan Lever, AIR 1984 SC 1683), but here the defect is substantive.

Under the Code

There is no Government reference of such a dispute: the union applies to the Industrial Tribunal (Form II, s.53(6); strike/lock-out legality is a two-member matter — s.44(7)). The application will plead a lock-out; the management's reply will plead closure (s.2(h), s.2(u); closure procedure under Ch. X, ss.74, 80); the Tribunal frames the issues neutrally — the TISCO duty now lies on the adjudicator, and the DCGM problem of a binding assumption cannot arise because no Government has framed the points. The problem's premise (a Government reference assuming a lock-out) can now occur only in a s.54 reference to the NIT or a s.92(2) transfer order, where ITDC/TISCO apply exactly as before. Substantively, the distinction remains vital: wages for a lock-out period depend on its legality/justification, whereas a genuine closure attracts compensation under s.75 (IDA s.25FFF) and, for large establishments, prior permission (s.80).

Conclusion

The reference, by presupposing a lock-out, decides the real dispute and disables the Tribunal (s.10(4); DCGM) from examining the closure plea; following ITDC, Moolchand and TISCO it should be quashed and a neutrally worded fresh reference directed. Under the Code the union's application and the Tribunal's own framing of issues remove the difficulty, except for s.54/s.92(2) orders.

May–June 2025 · Q.6 (essay) · text reconstructed from topic analysis "The power of the appropriate Government to refer an industrial dispute for adjudication under section 10(1) of the Industrial Disputes Act is discretionary, but the discretion is controlled by judicial review." Discuss with reference to decided cases. How far does this statement hold good under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020?

1. The power and its discretionary character

Section 10(1) empowered the appropriate Government, "where [it] is of opinion that any industrial dispute exists or is apprehended", to refer the dispute "at any time, by order in writing" to a Board, Court, Labour Court, Tribunal or (s.10(1A)) National Tribunal; s.12(5) added that after a failure report it "may" refer if satisfied there is a case for reference and must otherwise record reasons. The discretion had three dimensions: whether to refer, when ("at any time" — Western India Match, AIR 1970 SC 1205; no limitation — Shalimar Works), and what and to whom (points of dispute, s.10(4); forum; establishments to be included, s.10(5)). Only two situations made a reference obligatory: the second proviso (s.22 notice in a public utility) and s.10(2) (joint application).

2. Nature: administrative and subjective

State of Madras v. C.P. Sarathy, AIR 1953 SC 53 settled that the order of reference is an administrative act, not judicial or quasi-judicial; the factual existence or apprehension of a dispute and the expediency of a reference "are matters entirely for the Government"; the High Court cannot examine whether there was material to support the opinion. Consequences: no hearing is required before a reference (Sultan Singh, (1996) 2 SCC 66; Avon Services, 1979), no lis is decided, a refusal does not exhaust the power, and Art. 136 does not lie against the Government. Bombay Union of Journalists, AIR 1964 SC 1617 confirmed that the Government may look at the prima facie merits and expediency without thereby becoming a quasi-judicial body. Indian Tea Assn. v. Ajit Kumar Barat, (2000) 3 SCC 93 restated the position in five propositions (subjective satisfaction on material; administrative; no lis; mandamus if irrelevant material is relied on; a party may show that what was referred is not an industrial dispute).

3. The control: grounds of judicial review

Discretion is not licence; the courts developed a body of grounds under Art. 226: (a) extraneous or irrelevant considerations — State of Bombay v. K.P. Krishnan, AIR 1960 SC 1223 (refusal to punish a go-slow; mandamus to reconsider); Sharad Kumar, (2002) 4 SCC 490 (designation "extraneous to the matters relevant"); (b) usurpation of the Tribunal's function — Telco Convoy Drivers, (1989) 3 SCC 271 ("the government cannot delve into the merits of the dispute and take upon itself the determination of the lis"; direction to refer within one month); Ram Avtar Sharma, AIR 1985 SC 915; M.P. Irrigation Karamchari Sangh, (1985) 2 SCC 103 (only perverse or frivolous demands may be declined; courts "will always be vigilant"); (c) arbitrariness and discrimination — D.C. & G.M. v. Shambhu Nath Mukherjee, AIR 1978 SC 8 (Art. 14); Workmen of Syndicate Bank v. Government of India, AIR 1985 SC 819 (power discretionary but not absolute); (d) absence of any dispute / stale dispute — Sindhu Resettlement (1968) (no demand, reference incompetent); Nedungadi Bank, (2000) 2 SCC 455 (seven-year-old "dead" dispute; reference quashed); (e) mala fides — British India Corporation, AIR 1957 SC 354 (burden on the challenger); (f) non-application of mind and misreading of statutory conditions — FDC Ltd. v. Union of India (Del 2007): NIT reference of 58 individual terminations quashed because neither "national importance" nor "establishments in more than one State" existed and the Government had not noticed that many named workmen had no live dispute; (g) reference contrary to law or pre-judging the dispute — ITDC (1982), Moolchand (1998/2002), TISCO, (2014) 1 SCC 536 (reference presupposing employment and "transfer" quashed; fresh reference directed); (h) cancellation of a reference — D.N. Ganguly, AIR 1958 SC 1018 (no power to withdraw; only s.33B transfer). The remedies are graduated: reconsideration (K.P. Krishnan) → direction to refer (Telco, Sharad Kumar) → quashing with direction for a fresh reference (TISCO) → quashing simpliciter (FDC). What the courts do not do is weigh the sufficiency of material (Sarathy) or decide the dispute themselves.

4. Evaluation

The statement is accurate for the IDA: the Government's subjective opinion was protected as to sufficiency but policed as to legality — relevance, purpose, application of mind, non-usurpation, non-discrimination and fidelity to the statutory conditions. The tension between Barat (Government may read the record) and Telco/Sharad Kumar (Government may not weigh contested evidence) marks the boundary of the discretion.

5. Under the Code

The statement now needs re-writing. (i) The general referring discretion has been abolished: parties apply to the Tribunal themselves (s.53(6), s.4, s.22), so there is nothing to review — the legislature has adopted the Telco/Sharad Kumar policy wholesale. (ii) The one surviving discretion is the Central Government's power under s.54 to refer a dispute of national importance / multi-State interest to the NIT (and its cousin, the s.92(2) transfer). Here the statement holds good exactly: the opinion is subjective (Sarathy) but the two conditions are jurisdictional and their misreading, non-application of mind or extraneous purpose is reviewable (FDC); the terms of reference must be neutral (TISCO); the Government cannot decide status questions (Telco). (iii) Minor discretions — s.95 interpretation references, s.92(1) transfers ("reasons to be stated"), the s.55(3) proviso postponement of an award — are equally reviewable on the same grounds. (iv) The DCGM-type consequence of a Government assumption is reduced because the Tribunal frames its own issues on applications. Conclusion: the discretion has shrunk to the national tier, and there it remains "controlled by judicial review" in the manner the cases describe.

Nov–Dec 2025 · Q.2 (problem) · text reconstructed from topic analysis The employees' association of cinema theatres in a city submitted demands for higher wages and bonus to the Labour Commissioner. Conciliation with the managements failed. The Government referred "the industrial dispute between the workers and managements of the cinema theatres in the city" to the Tribunal without naming the theatres or the items. Prabhat Talkies, one of the theatres, whose workers had raised no demand, objected before the Tribunal that no dispute existed between it and its workers; the objection was rejected and an award was passed and declared binding. Prabhat Talkies did not implement it and its manager was prosecuted for breach of the award. He contends that the reference was invalid for vagueness and for want of any dispute concerning his theatre, so that the award is void. Decide. How would the situation be dealt with under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020?

Issues

(1) Is a general reference of an industry-wide dispute, without naming parties or items, valid? (2) Can an establishment where no demand was made be bound? (3) Can the validity of the reference/award be attacked collaterally in the prosecution? (4) Code position.

Law and application

The facts are those of State of Madras v. C.P. Sarathy, AIR 1953 SC 53. The Supreme Court (reversing the Madras High Court, which had quashed the prosecution under IDA s.29) held: (a) the Government must satisfy itself on the facts brought to its notice that a dispute "exists or is apprehended", but the existence/apprehension of the dispute and the expediency of a reference are matters "entirely for the Government"; (b) the order of reference is an administrative act whose sufficiency of material the court cannot examine; (c) a reference of the dispute between the workers and managements of an industry in a city is not invalid for generality if the dispute is identifiable — though it is desirable to specify parties and points; (d) the word "apprehended" covers establishments where no demand had yet been made but where the dispute was likely to spread, so Prabhat Talkies was validly within the reference and the award bound it (s.18(3)); (e) the accused could not, in the s.29 prosecution, impeach the reference on the ground that there was no material for the Government's opinion. Applying this: the reference is valid; the objection that no dispute existed with Prabhat Talkies fails on the "apprehended" limb — the demands were industry-wide and the association's memorandum concerned all theatres; the Tribunal rightly refused to exclude it; the award binds under s.18(3)(b)/(d) (party summoned; all present and future workmen), and the manager's collateral attack fails; the prosecution proceeds. Two qualifications complete the answer: (i) vagueness is fatal only where the dispute cannot be identified at all — the reference is read with the demand and the failure report (ITC Monghyr; Jaipur Udyog); (ii) Sarathy does not permit reference of an item never demanded by anyone (Sindhu Resettlement), nor does it dilute the later duty of careful drafting where a reference prejudges a contested fact (Express Newspapers; TISCO).

Under the Code

(i) A State Government cannot refer at all; the association (as negotiating union under s.14, if applicable) would raise the industry-wide demand, conciliation would follow (s.53) and the union/employers' association would apply to the Tribunal (Form II, s.53(6)). An award binds "all parties", parties "summoned to appear" unless summoned without proper cause, and all present and future workers of the establishments concerned (s.57(3)) — so Prabhat Talkies, if summoned and heard, is bound as under Sarathy. (ii) A general "industry" reference remains possible only where the Central Government refers a dispute of national importance/multi-State interest to the NIT under s.54 (e.g., a multiplex chain across States would still fail FDC's "one establishment with branches" test; a dispute of all cinema workers across States raised by a federation would not). (iii) Breach of a binding award is an offence under s.86, cognizable only on complaint (s.87) and compoundable under s.89 (fine-only offences at 50% of the maximum fine; imprisonment-or-fine offences at 75%); the manager could also be proceeded against for recovery of the money due under s.59. The Sarathy rule against collateral attack on the award in the prosecution applies equally to s.86.

Conclusion

Following Sarathy, the general reference was valid, Prabhat Talkies was bound through the "apprehended dispute" limb and s.18(3), and the manager cannot impeach the award in the prosecution. Under the Code the same result follows through the parties' application, s.57(3) and s.86/s.89 — with the Government reference surviving only at the NIT level.

19-1 · L.C.II 2001 / 2002 / 2005 (essay) Describe the powers of the appropriate Government to refer industrial disputes for adjudication under section 10(1) of the I.D. Act.

Structure. (1) Text and pre-conditions: opinion that a dispute "exists or is apprehended" (Sarathy: subjective, on facts brought to notice; "apprehended" covers likely spread); "at any time" (no limitation — Shalimar Works; reconsideration — Western India Match; but not a dead dispute — Nedungadi Bank); "by order in writing" (no form prescribed; drafting defects not fatal — Indian Paper Pulp; but self-contained — Gordhandas Bhanji). (2) Choice of forum: Board (a), Court of Inquiry (b), Labour Court for Second-Schedule matters (c), Tribunal for Second/Third (d); provisos — Third-Schedule dispute affecting ≤100 workmen may go to a Labour Court; public-utility s.22 notice → "shall" refer unless frivolous/vexatious/inexpedient; Central Government may use State forums; s.10(1A) National Tribunal for national-importance/multi-State disputes (FDC). (3) Mandatory references: s.10(2) joint application ("Ten-Two reference"). (4) Ancillary powers: fix the period for the award (s.10(2A)); prohibit strikes/lock-outs (s.10(3); Edward Keventers — cannot be used to prejudge closure v. lock-out); specify points of dispute — Tribunal confined to them and incidental matters (s.10(4); DCGM); include other establishments (s.10(5)); ouster of other forums on NIT reference (s.10(6)); s.10(7)–(8). (5) Nature: administrative (Sarathy, Bombay Union of Journalists, Telco); no hearing (Sultan Singh, Nagalinga Nadar); no cancellation once made (D.N. Ganguly) but amendment/addition (Bhope; K.E. Union) and transfer (s.33B). (6) Limits: cannot adjudicate (Telco, Sharad Kumar, Ram Avtar Sharma); may decline perverse/frivolous demands (M.P. 19-2 · L.C. 2001 / 2002 (problem) During the pendency of the adjudication proceedings, the employer made a representation to the Government that the reference of the dispute should not have been made. The Government, on being satisfied with the representation, withdrew the said adjudication proceedings. Discuss the validity of the order of withdrawal. Can the court direct the Government to withdraw the reference?

Law. The IDA conferred no power to cancel, withdraw or supersede a reference. In State of Bihar v. D.N. Ganguly, AIR 1958 SC 1018 the Government cancelled a pending reference and made a fresh one; the Supreme Court held the cancellation invalid: s.21 of the General Clauses Act (power to rescind) does not apply because the Act shows a "different intention" — once a reference is made, statutory consequences follow (Tribunal's duty to adjudicate, ss.15, 17 publication, s.19 operation, s.23 strike bar, s.33 freeze), and the parties acquire rights which the Government cannot destroy; the 1956 insertion of s.33B, allowing withdrawal only for transfer to another forum, confirms that no general power exists. Consequently the Tribunal must proceed to an award; even a settlement reached during pendency is to be recorded as an award, not treated as a withdrawal (see 19-3). A second reference of different matters is valid (K.E. Union, AIR 1955 SC 635), but that is not withdrawal. Application. The order withdrawing the proceedings on the employer's representation is ultra vires and void; the Tribunal remains seised and must make its award; the employer's real remedy against a reference it considers bad is a writ under Art. 226 (grounds in §6 — e.g., no dispute, mala fides, defective wording — TISCO), not an executive recall. Nor can the Government use s.33B as a device: a s.33B order must transfer to another forum, for reasons stated. Can a court direct withdrawal? No — mandamus lies only to compel performance of a legal duty or to restrain an illegal act; since the Government has no power to withdraw, a court cannot direct it to do so. The court may itself quash a reference that is illegal (FDC, TISCO), or direct a s.33B transfer in an appropriate case, but it cannot order a "withdrawal". Code. Identical result for a s.54 reference: no recall power; the Central Government may only transfer (s.92) for reasons stated; a party aggrieved by a reference moves under Art. 226. For Form II applications the question does not arise — only the applicant can withdraw an application (subject to the Tribunal's leave), and the Government is not a party.

19-3 · L.C. 2003 (problem) Before the Tribunal could submit its award, the parties to the dispute arrived at a settlement. Can the Tribunal treat the reference as cancelled or withdrawn? What should it do?

No. D.N. Ganguly holds that a reference, once made, "must result in adjudication and must end with the passing of the award"; the Tribunal "has no power to treat the reference as withdrawn". The correct course is for the Tribunal to incorporate the terms of the settlement in its award (a consent/settlement award), after satisfying itself that the settlement is fair and was arrived at by the parties properly represented — a practice affirmed in later cases and reflected in the requirement that awards be in writing and signed (IDA s.16; Code s.55(1)). Two consequences follow: (i) the settlement so incorporated binds under s.18(3) (Code s.57(3)) — i.e., all present and future workmen of the establishment, not merely the signatories as a private settlement would (s.18(1)/s.57(1)); (ii) the award's operation and termination are governed by s.19 (Code s.58). If the settlement covers only part of the reference, the Tribunal records it as to that part and adjudicates the rest. A settlement reached in conciliation during pendency is separately binding under s.18(3)/s.57(3) and the Tribunal should take note of it (National Engineering Industries). Under the Code the position is the same for the NIT; for Form II applications the applicant may withdraw on settlement, but a Tribunal-recorded award is preferable for the same binding-effect reasons.

19-5 · L.C.I 2002 / 2003 / 2005 Write a short note on / critically examine the decision in State of Madras v. C.P. Sarathy.

Note: facts, holding and the four propositions as in §10. Critical examination: (i) Strengths — it keeps the Government out of adjudication and prevents employers from stalling industry-wide adjudication by technical pleas; the "apprehended" limb allows industry-level settlement of common demands (the wage-board logic later used for national references); it protects awards from collateral attack in prosecutions, preserving finality (now s.45/s.57). (ii) Weaknesses — an unreviewable "subjective opinion" can shelter arbitrary references; binding an establishment that had no dispute sits uneasily with the definition of "industrial dispute" (a dispute between these employers and their workmen — cf. Sindhu) and with natural justice, though s.18(3)(b) (party summoned) mitigates it; the Court's own advice to specify parties and points was honoured in the breach, producing the defective-reference litigation from Express Newspapers to TISCO. (iii) Later development — the courts qualified the "unreviewable" language: refusals are reviewed for relevance (K.P. Krishnan), usurpation (Telco) and discrimination (Shambhu Nath); references are reviewed for absence of dispute (Sindhu), staleness (Nedungadi), mala fides, statutory conditions (FDC) and prejudicial wording (TISCO) — so Sarathy now stands only for "no review of sufficiency of material and expediency". (iv) Code — with the general reference abolished, Sarathy's administrative-act doctrine governs the s.54 opinion and the Government's other executive decisions under the Code; its "apprehended dispute" reasoning survives in s.53(1) (conciliation of apprehended disputes) and in the binding effect of awards on summoned parties and future workers (s.57(3)).

19-8 · L.C.I 2004; C.L.C. 2004 (problem) The workmen demanded the introduction of a gratuity scheme. On conciliation failure the Government referred the demand to the Tribunal. Problem 1 — s.54 reference of a pan-India banking dispute Unions of twelve public-sector banks demand a uniform five-day week. After conciliation fails in several States, the Central Government refers "the dispute between the managements of the banks named in Schedule A and their workmen regarding the demand for a five-day working week" to the National Industrial Tribunal. Two banks contend that no demand was ever served on them and that the NIT lacks jurisdiction. Decide.

Skeleton: s.54 conditions — many establishments (twelve banks, different owners), all States → Condition B satisfied; also arguably Condition A (future service conditions of banking workers generally) — FDC distinguished (not one establishment's branches). Opinion formed on failure reports → material exists; court will not weigh its sufficiency (Sarathy). Banks without a served demand: bound through the "apprehended dispute" limb and their inclusion as parties (Sarathy; s.57(3)(b) — summoned parties unless summoned without proper cause); a demand on the industry through IBA-level negotiations suffices (Sindhu distinguished — a demand did exist industry-wide). Terms are neutral (demand identified; no assumption). Result: reference valid; NIT has jurisdiction; the two banks may show before the NIT that they were summoned without proper cause. Add: NIT composition (s.46), award timeline (s.54(2), s.55), postponement power of the Central Government (s.55(3) proviso) for an award with national-economy implications.

Problem 2 — s.92(2) transfer to the NIT Forty Form II applications by dismissed gig-platform delivery workers are pending before Tribunals in nine States. The Central Government, "to avoid conflicting awards", withdraws all forty and transfers them to the NIT under s.92(2), without further reasons. Advise the platform and the workers.

Skeleton: s.92(2) requires "reasons to be stated" and presupposes a dispute the NIT can adjudicate under s.46 (national importance / multi-State establishments). Forty fact-bound individual dismissals of one platform = FDC's "cluster of individual disputes"; one platform operating in nine States = "one establishment having branches" — Condition B fails; avoiding conflicting awards is not a statutory criterion (FDC ¶5–¶9, ¶12). "Without further reasons" = non-application of mind. Both sides may challenge under Art. 226 (writ lies against NIT-related orders — FDC ¶11); workers additionally on convenience/access (¶10, ¶12). Contrast: if the common question were whether platform workers are "workers" under s.2(zr) at all — a question of law affecting the sector nationally — a test-case transfer with reasons could satisfy Condition A. Also note that unlike IDA s.10(6) there is no automatic quashing of the State proceedings; until a valid transfer, they continue.

Problem 3 — one-sided Form II application A union's Form II application asks the Tribunal to decide "the quantum of wages payable for the illegal lock-out from 1 March". The employer's reply pleads a lawful closure on 28 February after a s.80 permission. The Tribunal frames a single issue: "What wages are payable for the lock-out period?" The employer seeks a writ. Advise.

Skeleton: Under the Code the application is not a Government reference, so the DCGM assumption problem does not arise from the applicant's pleading — the Tribunal must frame issues from both pleadings. By framing an issue that assumes an "illegal lock-out", the Tribunal has committed the TISCO/ITDC error itself: it has decided the real dispute (closure v. lock-out; legality) without trial and shut out the defence. Remedy: first, an application to the Tribunal to recast the issues (it has full control of its procedure — s.49) and to implead/verify the s.80 permission; if refused, Art. 227 supervisory jurisdiction (error of jurisdiction in framing) — High Courts will intervene where the framing forecloses a defence, though not for merely inartistic issues (Hindustan Lever). Proper issues: (1) whether the establishment was closed on 28 February with valid permission under s.80; (2) if not, whether the stoppage was a lock-out and whether it was legal/justified (s.62–63); (3) wages/compensation. Two-member bench required (s.44(7): legality of lock-out; closure).

Problem 4 — wrong Tribunal (appropriate Government) Workers of a contractor engaged by a major port trust apply to the State Industrial Tribunal, which passes an award of reinstatement. The port trust challenges it as a nullity. Decide.

Skeleton: Major ports are in the Central sphere (s.2(b)), and the definition extends to the establishments of contractors for such establishments; the appropriate Government is the Central Government; the State Tribunal had no jurisdiction unless the Central Government had empowered it under s.92(3). Jurisdiction cannot be conferred by consent or waiver; the award is a nullity (IDA analogues: Heavy Engineering Mazdoor Union; SAIL). Remedy for workers: fresh Form II application to the Central Industrial Tribunal; limitation problem — the 90-day period has run; argue exclusion of time spent bona fide in a wrong forum (s.14 Limitation Act by analogy; the Code's period is procedural and the Tribunal may condone if the Rules permit) — flag as an unsettled point. Also mention s.92(1) cannot cure it (transfer only between Tribunals of the same appropriate Government).

Problem 5 — stale dispute A worker dismissed in January 2023 applies to the GRC in June 2025, then to the conciliation officer, and files Form II in December 2025. The employer objects on limitation. Decide under the Code; contrast the IDA.

Skeleton: Code: GRC application within one year of the cause (s.4(5)) — missed; conciliation officer shall not hold proceedings for an individual dispute after two years from the date the dispute arose (s.53(1) proviso) — the January 2025 outer limit has passed; Form II within 90 days of the failure report (s.53(6); R.23(9)). The application is barred at the conciliation stage itself; the Tribunal, deciding its own jurisdictional facts, will dismiss it as not maintainable unless the delay can be explained within whatever condonation the Rules allow (flag). IDA contrast: no limitation for a reference (Shalimar Works); s.2A(3) three years for direct applications; but courts refused stale references (Nedungadi Bank — seven years) and moulded relief for delay (Ajaib Singh). Policy point: the Code trades the Government's discretion on staleness for fixed periods.

Problem 6 — single-member award in a dismissal case The Administrative Member alone hears and decides a dismissal application, the Judicial Member being on leave. Is the award valid?

Skeleton: s.44(7) makes discharge/dismissal matters triable by a bench of both members; composition is a limb of jurisdiction; s.44(9) allows proceedings to continue during vacancies, not single-member decisions of two-member matters; award without jurisdiction → quashed under Art. 226/227; matter reheard by a full bench; s.47 applies only to differences of opinion between two sitting members. Compare IDA: awards by an unqualified presiding officer were nullities (s.7C disqualifications) — same logic.

Problem 7 — State Government "refers" a dispute After the Code's commencement a State Government, by notification, "refers" a wage dispute in a sugar mill to the Industrial Tribunal "under section 54". The employer objects. Decide.

Skeleton: s.54 vests the power in the Central Government alone and only for the NIT; a State Government has no referring power under the Code; the notification is a nullity (Jain ground (i) — wrong author). The Tribunal, however, may treat the union's demand as an application if the union files Form II within time after conciliation (s.53(6)); the Government cannot be a proxy applicant. If the sugar mill's dispute is genuinely of national importance (e.g., a question about the applicability of the Code to seasonal establishments across States), the State may request the Central Government to consider s.54 — the Central Government's refusal or grant would then be reviewable on FDC/Telco lines.

Problem 8 — application on a matter covered by a subsisting settlement A settlement in conciliation (2024, three years) fixes wages. In 2025 the union applies to the Tribunal for a wage revision, contending the settlement was unfair. The employer objects that the matter is not referable/adjudicable.

Skeleton: A conciliation settlement binds all workers (s.57(3)) for its period and thereafter until terminated by 60 days' notice (s.58); during subsistence, a dispute on the covered matter is ordinarily not adjudicable (IDA cases on s.19; Jain ground (x)); but whether the settlement is binding/fair or was obtained by fraud is itself an adjudicable dispute (National Engineering Industries — Tribunal may examine a settlement pleaded in bar). The Tribunal decides the preliminary question with the merits (D.P. Maheshwari); if the settlement is upheld, the application is dismissed as premature; the union's course is to terminate the settlement after its period (s.58) and raise a fresh demand (or negotiate under s.14). Strike on the covered matter meanwhile barred (s.62(1)(g)).

Viva / short-answer bank
  • Can a State Government refer a dispute under the Code? No; only the Central Government to the NIT (s.54). States' role: constituting Tribunals (s.44), transfers within their Tribunals (s.92(1)), s.95 references, rules (s.99).
  • Is "at any time" in s.54? No, but no limitation applies; Nedungadi still bars dead disputes.
  • Does s.54 require the Central Government to be the appropriate Government? No — s.46/s.92(2) make the NIT a Central forum for any establishment; FDC concerned a State-sphere company.
  • Can the NIT reference be withdrawn? No (Ganguly); it can be amended or the proceeding transferred (s.92).
  • Who decides whether the applicant is a "worker"? The Tribunal, on evidence (Telco, Sharad Kumar, Dharangadhra).
  • What is a "matter incidental"? One necessary to answer the point referred — computation, consequential relief, interim relief, implicit status — not one that changes the premise (DCGM).
  • Remedy against a badly worded s.54 reference? Art. 226 — quash with direction for a fresh, neutral reference (TISCO); or Government amendment (Bhope).
  • Can the Tribunal cure a defective reference itself? It can construe purposively and frame issues within it, not enlarge or reverse an assumption (DCGM, Pottery Mazdoor).
  • What replaced IDA s.10(6)? s.92(2) reasoned transfer to the NIT; no automatic quashing.
  • Does Sarathy survive? Yes, for the s.54 opinion (no review of sufficiency) and for the binding effect of general awards (s.57(3)); but qualified by K.P. Krishnan–FDC on legality.
  • Which DU case is a High Court decision? FDC (Delhi High Court, Dhingra J.); the other three are Supreme Court decisions.
  • Which DU case directed the Government to refer? Telco (within one month) and Sharad Kumar (including the status question); TISCO directed a fresh reference (two months); FDC simply quashed.
Glossary
Reference
An order in writing by which a Government places an industrial dispute (and its points) before an adjudicator; under the Code only the Central Government's s.54 order to the NIT (and s.95 interpretation references).
Appropriate Government
The Government (Central or State) competent for an establishment under s.2(b); fixes the Tribunal and the Government exercising ss.92(1), 95, 96.
Terms / points of reference
The questions framed in the order; they bound the Tribunal under IDA s.10(4) and bind the NIT under s.54; "matters incidental" are ancillary questions necessary to answer them.
Defective reference
A reference that is vague, prejudges a contested fact, refers an un-demanded item, goes to an incompetent forum, omits a necessary party, or is made without application of mind/mala fide; consequences range from curable (construction/amendment) to void (quashing).
Jurisdictional fact
A fact on whose existence jurisdiction depends (worker, employer, industry, industrial dispute, appropriate Government, limitation); decided by the adjudicator, not conclusively by the Government.
Usurpation
The Government deciding the lis (status, merits) while deciding whether to refer — forbidden (Telco).
Mandamus / certiorari
Writs under Art. 226: certiorari quashes an illegal reference/refusal/award; mandamus compels reconsideration or, exceptionally, a reference.
Form II
The application to the Industrial Tribunal under s.53(6) (R.23(9)) — the Code's substitute for the s.12(5) reference.
NIT
National Industrial Tribunal (s.46): two members; receives disputes only by s.54 reference or s.92(2) transfer (or s.95/s.91 in a pending matter).
Espousal
Support of an individual's cause by a union/substantial body of workmen — required under the IDA for non-s.2A disputes (Bombay Union of Journalists v. The Hindu); unnecessary under s.2(q) for termination disputes.
Apprehended dispute
A dispute likely to arise/spread though no demand has yet been made on the particular establishment (Sarathy); still relevant to conciliation (s.53(1)) and to who may be summoned and bound (s.57(3)(b)).
