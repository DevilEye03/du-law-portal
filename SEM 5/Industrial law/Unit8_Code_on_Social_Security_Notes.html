A

Framework: from nine Acts to one Code

What the Code on Social Security, 2020 is, why it matters for Unit 8, and how the three syllabus topics sit inside it

A.1 What the Code does

The Code on Social Security, 2020 (Act 36 of 2020) received Presidential assent on 28 September 2020 and was brought into force (with the other three Labour Codes) on 21 November 2025 (S.O. 5319(E)). Its preamble: “An Act to amend and consolidate the laws relating to social security with the goal to extend social security to all employees and workers either in the organised or unorganised or any other sectors.” It repeals and re-enacts nine central statutes (§164):

Repealed & subsumed (§164(1))
  1. Employee's Compensation Act, 1923 → Chapter VII
  2. Employees' State Insurance Act, 1948 → Chapter IV
  3. Employees' Provident Funds & MP Act, 1952 → Chapter III
  4. Employment Exchanges (CNV) Act, 1959 → Chapter XIII
  5. Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 → Chapter VI
  6. Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 → Chapter V
  7. Cine-Workers Welfare Fund Act, 1981
  8. Building & Other Construction Workers' Welfare Cess Act, 1996 → Chapter VIII
  9. Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act, 2008 → Chapter IX
Definition of “social security” — §2(78)

“the measures of protection afforded to employees, unorganised workers, gig workers and platform workers to ensure access to health care and to provide income security, particularly in cases of old age, unemployment, sickness, invalidity, work injury, maternity or loss of a breadwinner by means of rights conferred on them and schemes framed, under this Code.” — This mirrors ILO Convention No. 102 (Social Security (Minimum Standards), 1952), which lists nine branches of social security.

Savings — why old case law & notifications still matter

§164(2)(a): anything done under the repealed Acts (rules, notifications, schemes, orders, benefits) is deemed done under the corresponding provisions of the Code and continues until superseded. §164(3) applies §6 of the General Clauses Act. So the wage-ceiling notification of 3 Jan 2020 under the EC Act (Rs 15,000), the ESI coverage ceiling (Rs 21,000), and the gratuity ceiling (Rs 20 lakh) continue, and all decisions interpreting “arising out of and in the course of employment”, “continuous service”, “maternity benefit” etc. remain authoritative because the Code re-enacts the same language.

Key structural features relevant to Unit 8
  • Uniform definitions (§2) for all chapters — e.g. “employee” §2(26), “wages” §2(88), “dependant” §2(24), “employment injury” §2(28), “permanent partial/total disablement” §2(55)/(56), “temporary disablement” §2(83), “woman” §2(91).
  • Chapter-wise applicability is set out in the First Schedule (§1(4)); once a chapter applies it continues to apply even if numbers fall below the threshold (§1(8)).
  • Competent authority replaces the “Commissioner” (EC Act) and the “controlling authority” (PG Act); Inspector-cum-Facilitator replaces the “Inspector” (MB Act) — §2(16), §2(37), §58, §72, §91.
  • Overriding effect of the Code over inconsistent laws, awards, agreements and contracts, saving more favourable terms (§161) — the successor to §27 MB Act and §17 EC Act (contracting-out).
  • Legislative codification of case law: §74(4) (commuting accidents — the Daivshala rule), §34(3)–(4) (ESI commuting/employer's transport — old §§51E, 51C), §53(1)(d) & third proviso to §53(2) (pro-rata gratuity for fixed-term employees), §60(4) (adoptive and commissioning mothers), §60(5) (work from home).
  • New categories: gig worker §2(35), platform worker §2(61), aggregator §2(2), fixed term employment §2(34), home-based worker §2(36) — with scheme-based (not right-based) coverage under Chapter IX (§§109–114).

A.2 Constitutional and international backdrop

Directive Principles (Part IV) — the source of every social security statute

  • Art. 39(e): health and strength of workers not abused; citizens not forced by economic necessity into unsuited avocations.
  • Art. 41: right to work, education and public assistance in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement.
  • Art. 42: just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief (relied on in MCD v. Female Workers (Muster Roll), Deepika Singh, Dr. Kavita Yadav).
  • Art. 43: living wage and conditions ensuring a decent standard of life.
  • Arts. 14, 15(3), 21: equality, special provision for women, dignity/livelihood — used to strike down penalising motherhood (Air India v. Nergesh Meerza (1981) 4 SCC 335; Manisha Priyadarshini (Del HC 2019)).
  • Legislative competence: Entry 23, List III (social security and social insurance; employment and unemployment) and Entry 24, List III (welfare of labour including conditions of work, provident funds, employers' liability, workmen's compensation, invalidity and old-age pensions and maternity benefits).

ILO instruments that shaped the Indian scheme

  • C-17 (Workmen's Compensation (Accidents), 1925), C-18 (Occupational Diseases, 1925) and C-19 (Equality of Treatment (Accident Compensation), 1925) — the 1923 Act's occupational disease schedule and the equal treatment of foreign workers trace to these.
  • C-102 (Social Security (Minimum Standards), 1952) — nine branches; the template for §2(78).
  • C-121 (Employment Injury Benefits, 1964) — Article 7 asks States to define “industrial accident” including commuting accidents; expressly discussed by the 62nd Law Commission Report (1974) and relied upon in Daivshala (2025) to read commuting accidents into §3 EC Act.
  • C-183 (Maternity Protection, 2000) — 14 weeks minimum; India's 26 weeks (2017 amendment, now §60(3)) exceeds it.
  • CEDAW, Art. 11 — maternity leave with pay and prohibition of dismissal on grounds of pregnancy; relied upon in MCD v. Female Workers and quoted in Dr. Kavita Yadav.

Relationship between Chapter IV (ESI) and Chapter VII (EC) — the “either/or” rule

Chapter VII is residuary: it applies only where ESI (Chapter IV) does not. An insured person's remedy for an employment injury is the ESI disablement benefit / dependants' benefit (ESI Act §53 barred a parallel EC claim). Hence in Royal Western India Turf Club the fight was over which regime applied to casual race-day staff (and therefore who paid contributions), while in Agnes and Daivshala the establishments were outside ESI and the claims lay under the (Workmen's/Employee's) Compensation Act. The two statutes are pari materia — Daivshala uses ESI §§51C/51E to interpret EC §3, and the Code now places both under one roof with parallel deeming clauses (§34 for ESI; §74(2)–(4) for EC).

Example

A sugar factory near Osmanabad with 300 workers is in an ESI-implemented area → its workers are “insured persons” and claims go to ESIC. The same factory's night watchman in a non-implemented area (as in Daivshala, accident of 22 Apr 2003) is a Second Schedule employee [item (xxxiii): “employed as watchman in any factory or establishment”] → Chapter VII claim before the competent authority against the employer, with the employer's insurer (Oriental Insurance) answering under the policy.

B

Employees' Compensation — Definitions

§2 of the Code (with the corresponding provisions of the Employee's Compensation Act, 1923 in brackets) and the concepts the examiner tests through them

B.1 Object and nature of employees' compensation

Before 1923 an injured workman had only a tort action for negligence, defeated by the “unholy trinity” of common-law defences — volenti non fit injuria, contributory negligence and common employment (Priestley v. Fowler (1837)). The 1923 Act (following the English Acts of 1897–1906) created a statutory, no-fault liability of the employer: compensation is payable if the injury is caused by an accident arising out of and in the course of employment, irrespective of the employer's negligence. The 62nd Law Commission Report (quoted in Daivshala, para 40) states the purpose: “to eliminate the hardship experienced under the common law system by providing for payment of benefits regardless of fault and with a minimum of legal formality.”

Theoretical basis — (i) theory of industrial risk / “cost of production”: the industry that profits from labour should bear the cost of the human wastage it causes; (ii) theory of social insurance: society shifts the risk of injury from the individual worker to the enterprise (and, under ESI, to a pooled fund); (iii) welfare-legislation canon: beneficial statutes are construed liberally in favour of the worker (Turf Club, para 5; Daivshala, paras 39, 55).

Salient features of Chapter VII at a glance
  • Applies to persons in the Second Schedule employed in establishments outside ESI (First Schedule; §2(26) third proviso).
  • Employer's liability — §74; no-fault, but subject to three limited defences (not available in death/PTD cases).
  • Covers accidents (including commuting accidents on the nexus test — §74(4)) and occupational diseases (Third Schedule — §74(3)).
  • Quantum fixed by formula — §76 (50%/60% of monthly wages × age factor; 25% half-monthly for temporary disablement) — with a floor notified by the Centre, funeral expenses (≥ Rs 15,000) and reimbursement of actual medical expenditure.
  • Compulsory deposit of death compensation with the competent authority; no direct payment discharges the employer (§81).
  • Notice and two-year limitation (§82) with liberal condonation; principal employer liable for contractor's employees (§85); insolvency protection (§87).
  • Adjudication by the competent authority (§§90–98); appeal to the High Court only on a substantial question of law (§99).
  • Civil courts barred (§90(2)); election between a civil suit and a compensation claim (§74(7)).

B.2 “Employee” — §2(26) with the Second Schedule [EC Act §2(1)(dd), old “workman” §2(1)(n)]

§2(26) — text (relevant parts)

“employee” means any person (other than an apprentice engaged under the Apprentices Act, 1961) employed on wages by an establishment, either directly or through a contractor, to do any skilled, semi-skilled or unskilled, manual, operational, supervisory, managerial, administrative, technical, clerical or any other work, whether the terms of employment be express or implied, and also includes a person declared to be an employee by the appropriate Government, but does not include any member of the Armed Forces of the Union: … Provided also that for the purposes of Chapter VII, the term “employee” shall mean only such persons as specified in the Second Schedule and such other persons or class of persons as the Central Government, or as the case may be, the State Government may add to the said Schedule, by notification, for the purposes of that Government.

Points to note

  • The general definition is all-embracing (managerial and administrative staff included; no wage ceiling), but for Chapter VII only Second Schedule persons qualify — the Schedule is the old Schedule II of the 1923 Act, carried over with a saving clause.
  • “employed… either directly or through a contractor” — contract labour is covered; the principal employer is liable under §85 with a right of indemnity.
  • Apprentices under the Apprentices Act are excluded (they are covered by §16 of that Act, which itself applies the Employee's Compensation law); Armed Forces excluded.
  • The old requirement that employment be “otherwise than in a clerical capacity” was removed in 2009; the 2020 Code expressly includes clerical work.
  • “Employed on wages… whether the terms of employment be express or implied” — casual, temporary, part-time and daily-rated workers are employees if a contract of service exists (Turf Club on the analogous ESI definition; Regional Director, ESIC v. South India Flour Mills AIR 1986 SC 1686).

Second Schedule — flavour of the list (50 items)

  • (i) railways — lifts/vehicles; (ii) premises with a manufacturing process using power; (iii) making/altering/repairing articles in any premises (incl. persons outside premises on connected work); (iv) explosives; (v) mines; (vi) master/seaman of mechanically propelled ships; (vii) dock work; (viii) construction/repair/demolition of buildings above one storey or 12 ft, dams, roads, bridges, tunnels, canals, marine works; (ix) telegraph/telephone/electric lines; (xi) fire brigade; (xiii) RMS/postal outdoor staff; (xv) blasting; (xviii) tea/coffee/rubber/cardamom/cinchona estates; (xix) electricity/gas; (xxi) cinema production/exhibition; (xxii)/(xxiv) elephants/wild animals; (xxv) diver; (xxvi) warehouses/markets — handling goods; (xxvii) radium/X-ray; (xxviii) aircraft; (xxxii) circus; (xxxiii) “employed as watchman in any factory or establishment” (→ Daivshala); (xxxiv) sea fishing (→ Jain's tsunami problem); (xxxv) snakes; (xxxvi) horses/mules/bulls (→ race-course workers); (xxxvii) loading/unloading mechanically propelled vehicles; (xxxviii) cleaning sewer lines/septic tanks; (xli) cultivation, live-stock, forestry, fishing; (xlviii) newspaper employees on outdoor work; (xlix) sales promotion employees; (l) any other employee to whom the EC Act, 1923 applied in any State immediately before the Code (saves State extensions, e.g. to drivers, domestic workers etc.).
Exam use

In a problem, first locate the worker in the Second Schedule (or a State notification saved by item (l)). E.g. a bus driver (Agnes) is employed “in connection with the operation… of a vehicle propelled by… mechanical power” (item (i) for railways; for road transport most States notified motor-vehicle drivers/cleaners — saved by item (l)); a peon in an office is not a Second Schedule employee unless the office is part of a factory/establishment covered by an item.

B.3 Contract of service v. contract for service PYQ: short note, LC-I 2008

Compensation is payable only to an employee, i.e., one employed under a contract of service (master–servant), not an independent contractor working under a contract for service. The Code uses “employed on wages… express or implied” (§2(26)) — the same relationship test.

The same logic applies to Chapter VII: nothing in §2(26) or the Second Schedule requires a minimum period of employment. The Jain hypotheticals (peon, driver, fisherman, ticket checker) all presuppose a contract of service; the dispute is only whether the accident was employment-connected.

Related law — “wages” across the Codes

C

Injury “arising out of and in the course of employment”

§74(1) of the Code [§3(1) EC Act] — the single most examined idea in this paper

C.1 The statutory formula and its four conditions

§74(1) — Employer's liability for compensation

“If personal injury is caused to an employee by accident or an occupational disease listed in the Third Schedule arising out of and in the course of his employment, his employer shall be liable to pay compensation in accordance with the provisions of this Chapter: Provided that the employer shall not be so liable — (a) in respect of such injury which does not result in the total or partial disablement of the employee for a period exceeding three days; (b) in respect of such injury, not resulting in death or permanent total disablement, caused by an accident which is directly attributable to — (i) the employee having been at the time thereof under the influence of drink or drugs, or (ii) the wilful disobedience of the employee to an order expressly given, or to a rule expressly framed, for the purpose of securing the safety of employees, or (iii) the wilful removal or disregard by the employee of any safety guard or other device which he knew to have been provided for the purpose of securing the safety of employee.”

Burden of proof & the ESI presumption

Under Chapter VII the claimant must prove both limbs on a preponderance of probability, but the competent authority may draw reasonable inferences from circumstances (unexplained death at the place of duty raises a strong inference). Under Chapter IV (ESI), §34(1) [old §51A] provides a statutory presumption: an accident arising in the course of employment is presumed, absent contrary evidence, to have arisen out of it. Note the asymmetry — Cornell's predicted question “distinguish notional extension under the EC Act from §51C ESI” turns on such differences.

One-line tests to memorise
  • Lord Sumner (Lancashire & Yorkshire Rly v. Highley [1917] AC 352): “Was it part of the injured person's employment to hazard, to suffer, or to do that which caused his injury?”
  • Mackinnon Mackenzie v. Ibrahim (1969) 2 SCC 607: employment “as such — its nature, its conditions, its obligations and its incidents”; if these bring the workman “within the zone of special danger”, the injury arises out of employment — unless he exposed himself to an added peril by his own imprudent act.
  • Agnes (1964): “There is a notional extension of both the entry and exit by time and space.”
  • Daivshala (2025) / §74(4): commuting accident covered “provided the nexus between the circumstances, time and place in which the accident occurred and the employment is established.”

C.2 “Personal injury” and “accident”

Accident

  • Meaning: “an unlooked-for mishap or an untoward event which is not expected or designed” (Fenton v. Thorley [1903] AC 443, Lord Macnaghten) — judged from the victim's standpoint. A deliberate assault by a third party is still an “accident” qua the victim (Nisbet v. Rayne & Burn [1910] 2 KB 689 — cashier murdered while carrying wages by train; National Iron & Steel Co. v. Manorama Dassi AIR 1953 Cal 143 — tea-boy shot in police firing near the factory).
  • Injury need not be external: strain causing heart failure, rupture, stroke or nervous shock is an “injury by accident” if a specific event/strain at work can be identified — the “internal accident” cases.
  • Accident v. disease: a gradual process (dust inhalation) is a disease, compensable only if a scheduled occupational disease (§74(3)) or “directly attributable to a specific injury by accident” (§74(6)). Aggravation/acceleration of a pre-existing disease by a work event is an accident.
  • Time of accident: death may occur later (Agnes — accident 20 July 1957, death 26 July 1957); for occupational diseases §82 provides deemed dates.

Example — “proceeding to hospital after a workplace incident”

A furnace worker is scalded by a steam leak at 11 a.m.; the supervisor sends him to hospital in the company van; on the way he suffers a cardiac arrest and dies. Analysis: (i) the scald is an accident arising out of and in the course of employment; (ii) the journey to hospital for treatment of that injury is reasonably incidental to the employment (and within notional extension — he is where he is because of the employer's direction); (iii) the fatal cardiac event is either a direct consequence of the trauma/shock (chain of causation unbroken) or, on the Param Pal Singh logic, an internal accident triggered by work-induced strain. Liability follows; contrast a worker with a latent condition who merely felt unwell at his desk and died en route to a clinic with no identifiable work strain (Jyothi Ademma).

C.3 “Arising out of” — the causal limb

The phrase requires a causal relationship between the accident and the employment, but the cause need not be the work operation itself. As Mackinnon Mackenzie puts it, the expression “applies to employment as such — to its nature, its conditions, its obligations and its incidents. If by reason of any of those factors the workman is brought within the zone of special danger the injury would be one which arises ‘out of employment’.” The workman's own negligence is irrelevant (no-fault liability); what defeats the claim is an added peril — a risk he voluntarily created outside the scope of his duty.

Recognised categories

  • Risks inherent in the work — machinery, heights, explosives, animals (Second Schedule items), lightning striking a labourer at an exposed site (State of Rajasthan v. Ram Prasad (2001) 1 LLJ 177 (SC) — employment exposed her to the natural peril).
  • Street risks — where duties take the worker on to the road, the perils of the street are perils of the employment (Dennis v. A.J. White & Co. [1917] AC 479 — errand boy on bicycle; Salamabegum (Bom 1990) — jeep driver assaulted in a market while on duty; TNCS Corpn. v. Poomalai (Mad 1995) — riot en route). Contrast SAIL v. Kanchanbala (Ori 1994) — circuitous route chosen for private reasons: no.
  • Environmental / locality risks — wall collapse (Thom v. Sinclair [1917] AC 127), bomb in the premises (Trustees, Port of Bombay v. Yamunabai AIR 1952 Bom 382, Chagla CJ), police firing outside the gate (Manorama Dassi), crossing railway lines to reach the workmen's train (Works Manager, EIR v. Mahabir AIR 1954 All 132 — “environmental accidents”).
  • Acts of personal comfort — drinking water, tea break, answering nature's call, warming oneself are incidental to employment (Charles R. Davidson & Co. v. M'Robb [1918] AC 304).
  • Emergencies — rescuing a co-worker or protecting the employer's property (now expressly deemed for ESI — §34(2) [old §51D]; applied by analogy under Chapter VII).

What breaks the causal link

  • Added peril — Smt. Koduri Polangi v. Atchamma 1969 Lab IC 1415 (AP): workman climbed a tree to catch a rabbit and fell — his own frolic, not employment; Bombay Burma Trading Corpn. v. U.P. Hlaing AIR 1936 PC (Rang) — but note Indian Rare Earths v. Subaida Beevi (1981) 2 LLJ 293 (SC): cyclist crossing a road at a permitted point to reach the factory — not an added peril, claim allowed.
  • Personal enmity — assault for private reasons unconnected with the job (even at the workplace) does not arise out of employment; assault because of the job (cashier, watchman, ticket-checker) does.
  • Natural causes without work-strain — Jyothi Ademma; Burma Oil Co. v. Ma Hmwe Yin (snake-bite when the workman went early on his own), New India Assurance v. G. Krishna Rao (Ori 1995 — hut fire while sleeping, unconnected with work).
  • Unexplained disappearance — Mackinnon Mackenzie & Co. v. Ibrahim Mahmmed Issak (1969) 2 SCC 607 (AIR 1970 SC 1906): seaman found missing from the ship; the Court laid down the classic test, and (as Jain notes) held that mere disappearance without evidence linking it to a risk of the service did not discharge the burden.
Do not confuse

Vis major (act of God) is not a defence in itself. The question remains: did the employment expose the worker to the natural peril to a greater degree than the public? Lightning on an open field (yes — Ram Prasad); a tsunami sweeping a fisherman who had already mustered and was ten metres from his boat (Jain's CLC 2005 problem — yes: his employment placed him on the shore at that hour and he had reached the notionally extended workplace).

C.4 “In the course of” — time, place and circumstances

The accident must occur while the worker is doing what he is employed to do or something reasonably incidental to it, at a time and place at which he is entitled to be by virtue of the employment. The classic three tests (summarised in Jain from the English cases) — the workman was (1) employed on the duties of his employment at the time; (2) at the place where his duties required him to be; and (3) doing something in discharge of those duties or incidental thereto — not something wholly foreign to them (“a frolic of his own”).

When employment begins and ends

  • Ordinarily, when the worker reaches and leaves the employer's premises — including the means of access and egress under the employer's control (Weaver v. Tredegar Iron & Coal Co. [1940] 3 All ER 157 — colliery platform; Bhagubai v. Central Railway (1954) 2 LLJ 403 (Bom) — stabbed in railway premises on the way to duty: covered).
  • Employment “does not necessarily end when the ‘down tool’ signal is given” (Agnes, para 12): reasonable time to wash, change, collect wages, wait for transport is included.
  • Rest intervals on the premises are within the course; going out for a private purpose during the break is not, unless the route/means is dictated by the employer.
  • Errands and travel on duty — the whole journey is the course of employment (peon delivering mail; salesman on tour).
Jain's DU problems decoded (all asked in DU LL.B. papers 2000–2008)
  • Peon ‘A’ whose duties include delivering mail outside is killed while commuting to office [CLC 2001] → the commute itself is not duty; but under Daivshala/§74(4) the claim now succeeds if the time/place nexus is shown (going straight to office at the reporting hour on the usual route).
  • Driver ‘A’, given a rent-free house for doing the Manager's domestic chores, dies driving the Manager's children to school in the company vehicle [LC-II 2004] → private service to the manager, not the employer's business; no notional extension → fails (unless the employer had sanctioned/required the practice, making it an incident of employment).
  • Fisherman ‘A’, Great Nicobar, swept away by the tsunami 10 m from the work-spot after muster [CLC 2005] → within the notionally extended premises after mustering; employment exposed him to the peril → succeeds (cf. Dedhiben v. New Jehangir Vakil Mills AIR 1976 SC 1250, noted in Jain — worker close to the gate shortly before the shift held within the extension).
  • Ticket-checker ‘A’ with free travel on State Roadways buses leaves office to attend his personal court case and dies in a bus accident → travelling for a purpose foreign to employment; Agnes distinguished (there the driver was going home from duty, using a facility “as of right” for the efficiency of the service) → fails.
  • Silver Jubilee function: an employee attending the employer's function is killed crossing the public road outside the gate to board a bus [CLC 2006] → the road is a public risk shared with everyone; unless the employer arranged the transport or the attendance was compulsory duty, outside the course (compare Francis De Costa); if attendance was compulsory and the bus was the employer's, Agnes/§34(4) logic applies.

Step 1 — English origins: the “obligation” test and its critics

  • St. Helens Colliery Co. v. Hewitson [1924] AC 59 (HL): miner injured on a colliery-arranged train home; no compensation — he was under no contractual obligation to use the train (Lord Shaw dissenting). Cremins v. Guest, Keen & Nettlefolds (1908) 1 KB 469 and Alderman v. Great Western Rly [1937] AC 454 apply the same rule — transit outside employment absent obligation.
  • Weaver v. Tredegar Iron & Coal Co. [1940] 3 All ER 157 (HL): miner injured on the colliery platform (the only means of access) — covered; Lord Romer distinguished Hewitson: the platform was part of the means of access which the worker was entitled to use only as an employee. Evershed MR later framed the test as a nexus between presence at the spot and the employment (quoted in Agnes).
  • Lord Denning's summary (quoted in Daivshala para 34 from Vandyke v. Fender [1970] 2 QB 292): commuting accidents, even in the employer's transport, do not arise out of and in the course of employment unless a statute deems them to — “It needed a special ‘deeming’ provision” (s. 8 of the UK 1965 Act).

Step 2 — Saurashtra Salt Mfg. Co. v. Bai Valu Raja AIR 1958 SC 881 — the four propositions PYQ Nov-Dec 2025 Q.5 quotes them

Imam J. for the Court
  1. “As a rule, the employment of a workman does not commence until he has reached the place of employment and does not continue when he has left the place of employment, the journey to and from the place of employment being excluded.”
  2. “It is now well-settled, however, that this is subject to the theory of notional extension of the employer's premises so as to include an area which the workman passes and repasses in going to and in leaving the actual place of work.”
  3. “There may be some reasonable extension in both time and place and a workman may be regarded as in the course of his employment even though he had not reached or had left his employer's premises.”
  4. “The facts and circumstances of each case will have to be examined very carefully in order to determine whether the accident arose out of and in the course of the employment of a workman keeping in view at all times this theory of notional extension.”

Facts & result: workmen of the salt works at Porbandar had to cross a creek; they used a public ferry boat (not provided by the employer) which capsized. Held: the accident did not occur in the course of employment — once they were on a public boat used by all, they were in the same position as the public; the notional extension did not stretch to a public ferry. Saurashtra Salt thus accepted the theory but denied relief on facts — an important nuance for answers.

Why it matters: every later case (Agnes, Francis De Costa, Daivshala) begins with these propositions; the Nov-Dec 2025 paper reproduced them verbatim and asked for their application.

Step 3 — B.E.S.T. Undertaking v. Agnes (1964) 3 SCR 930: adapting the doctrine to a city bus fleet

Majority (Subba Rao J.): “the doctrine necessarily will have to be adapted to meet its peculiar requirements… in the case of a city transport service, by analogy, the entire fleet of buses forming the service would be the ‘premises’… They are given that facility not as members of the public but as employees; not as a grace but as of right because efficiency of the service demands it.” Dissent (Raghubar Dayal J.): free travel was a mere concession; once the driver left his own bus he was off duty and under no control. [Full analysis in Part I.]

Step 4 — Statutory recognition and the Law Commission

  • ESI §51C (Act 44 of 1966, w.e.f. 28 Jan 1968): accident while travelling as a passenger in employer-provided (non-public) transport with express/implied permission deemed to arise out of and in the course of employment even without obligation — “a law which came to be laid down in Agnes… was given effect by a statutory recognition in the ESI Act” (Daivshala, para 39). Now Code §34(4).
  • 62nd Law Commission Report (Oct 1974), para 3.3: adopt §51C into the WC Act “with modification that it should not be necessary that the transport be provided by the employer if the workman is travelling directly to or from the place of employment” — after surveying foreign law and the ILO Convention of 1964 (C-121). Parliament did not act on it for the EC Act.

Step 5 — The restrictive turn: Regional Director, ESIC v. Francis De Costa (1996) 6 SCC 1 (3 judges)

Employee cycling to the factory was hit by the employer's lorry about 1 km from the factory, 15 minutes before the shift. Held: not an employment injury — a road accident on a public road while going to work, in one's own conveyance, is not caused by the employment; §51C applies only to employer's transport; notional extension cannot be stretched to the public highway. The Court doubted the Gujarat High Court's liberal view in Sadgunaben Amrutlal v. ESIC 1981 Lab IC 1653 (employee killed at a public bus stop while boarding a bus to work — covered, treating the theory as “an elastic and flexible formula to be applied in a purposeful manner”).

Step 6 — Legislative response: ESI §51E (2010) → and judicial completion: Daivshala (2025)

§51E ESI (ESI (Amendment) Act, 2010): “An accident occurring to an employee while commuting from his residence to the place of employment for duty or from the place of employment to his residence after performing duty, shall be deemed to have arisen out of and in the course of employment if nexus between the circumstances, time and place in which the accident occurred and the employment is established.” Now Code §34(3) (ESI) and — for the first time for non-ESI employees — Code §74(4).

Daivshala v. Oriental Insurance Co. 2025 INSC 904 (K.V. Viswanathan J.): watchman on the 3 a.m. shift killed ~5 km from the factory while riding to work in April 2003 — before §51E and long before the Code. Held: EC Act and ESI Act are pari materia; §51E is clarificatory and therefore applies retrospectively; the phrase in §3 EC Act includes commuting accidents on the nexus test; the night watchman “dutifully proceeding to his workplace to be well on time” satisfied it. Award of 26 June 2009 restored.

Distinguishing the strands (for a “critically examine” answer)
  • Judicial notional extension (Saurashtra Salt/Agnes): flexible, fact-specific, limited to employer-controlled or obligatory access, means, time.
  • §34(4)/§51C (employer's transport): a deeming rule; requires employer-operated/arranged, non-public-transport vehicle and permission; no need for obligation.
  • §34(3)/§51E/§74(4) (commute): a deeming rule for any mode (own bike, public bus, on foot) — but conditioned on the nexus of circumstances, time and place; excludes detours, private errands, unusual hours.
  • After the Code: for accidents on/after 21 Nov 2025 cite §74(4) directly; for earlier accidents cite Daivshala (retrospective reading of §3). Francis De Costa survives only as a caution against claims lacking nexus.

C.7 Defences, exceptions and the no-contracting-out rule

The three statutory defences — §74(1)(b) [old §3(1)(b)]

  • Available only where the injury does not result in death or permanent total disablement; the accident must be directly attributable to (i) drink or drugs; (ii) wilful disobedience of an order expressly given or a rule expressly framed for securing safety; or (iii) wilful removal or disregard of a safety guard/device known to be provided for safety.
  • “Wilful” = deliberate, not mere negligence, thoughtlessness or a momentary lapse; the burden is on the employer. R.B. Moondra & Co. v. Bhanwari AIR 1970 Raj 111 — driver told to check a petrol-tank leak entered the tank with a lighted match and died: covered (act done for the employer's purpose; no express safety rule breached; in any case death removes the defence). Padam Debi v. Raghunath Rai AIR 1950 Ori 207 — defence unavailable in death cases.
  • The employee's contributory negligence, rashness or breach of a general instruction is no defence — the statute is no-fault. §74(2) goes further: breach of law/orders for the employer's business is deemed within employment.

Other exceptions / bars

  • Three-day rule — §74(1)(a): no liability where disablement does not exceed three days (a “waiting period” also reflected in §76(4)).
  • Election of remedy — §74(7) [old §3(5)]: no compensation if the employee has sued for damages in a civil court; no civil suit if he has claimed compensation or agreed to receive it. (Under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, §167 gives the injured an option between the Claims Tribunal and the EC forum — not both.)
  • Chapter IV excludes Chapter VII (First Schedule) — an insured person claims ESI benefits (bar formerly in ESI §53).
No contracting out — §161 [old EC §17; MB §27; PG §14]

Any award, agreement or contract of service inconsistent with the Code is overridden, save more favourable terms. Roshan Deen v. Preeti Lal (2002) 1 SCC 100: a workman's “compromise” accepting Rs 24,000 against a statutory entitlement of more than double could not be enforced; the High Court erred in relegating him to it — the protective purpose of the Act cannot be defeated by a settlement (§89 requires registration of lump-sum agreements and lets the competent authority refuse inadequate ones).

Example — “safety guard” defence

A power-press operator removes the interlock guard to speed up output and loses two fingers (PPD 20% — Fourth Schedule Part II item 9). The employer proves (a) the guard was provided for safety, (b) the worker knew it, (c) removal was deliberate. Defence succeeds — no compensation. If the same act had led to death, the widow recovers in full; if it had led to loss of both hands (PTD), the worker recovers in full.

D.2 Principles for classifying disablement PYQ: short notes CLC 2001, 2002, 2004, 2005

  1. Earning capacity, not actual earnings. The test is the reduction of the worker's capacity to earn in the labour market — not whether the employer continued to pay the same wage out of sympathy or on light duty (Jain: Balasaheb v. Mohanlal 1983 (Bom)). Conversely, a worker who somehow earns more after injury can still be “disabled”.
  2. “Every employment he was capable of undertaking” v. “all work he was capable of performing”. PPD looks at the general market of employments open to him; PTD asks whether he can do any of the work he could do before. Hence a trained carpenter who loses his left hand is totally (100%) disabled though the Schedule lists the injury at 60%: Pratap Narain Singh Deo v. Srinivas Sabata (1976) 1 SCC 289 — the Schedule percentages are for deemed partial disablement; where the evidence shows the worker cannot do the work he was doing or any similar work, the disablement is total. Similarly a truck driver whose leg is amputated cannot drive heavy vehicles — 100% loss of earning capacity (K. Janardhan v. United India Insurance (2008) 8 SCC 518).
  3. Schedule percentages are a floor, not a ceiling for the assessment of functional loss (Jain: Samir Parikh v. Sikander AIR 1984 SC); for scheduled injuries the deeming is conclusive as to the nature (partial) and the minimum percentage; the competent authority may find total disablement on evidence.
  4. Unscheduled injuries (spinal injury, head injury, loss of grip): loss of earning capacity “as assessed by the medical practitioner” having due regard to Schedule percentages (§76(1)(c)(ii) and Expl. 2). Golla Rajanna v. Divisional Manager (2017) 1 SCC 45: the assessment is a finding of fact for the Commissioner; the High Court cannot substitute its own view without a substantial question of law. Distinguish physical disability from functional (earning) disability (the MV Act analogue: Raj Kumar v. Ajay Kumar (2011) 1 SCC 343).
  5. Aggregation. Multiple injuries in one accident are aggregated but cannot exceed the PTD amount (§76(1)(c) Expl. 1); Part II injuries adding to 100% are deemed PTD (§2(56) proviso).
  6. Temporary → permanent. On review, half-monthly payments may be converted into the lump sum for permanent disablement, less amounts already paid (§79(2)).

E.1 Liability provisions (§§73–75)

§73 · Reports of fatal accidents and serious bodily injuries old §10B
  • Where any law (e.g., Factories/OSH Code, Mines Act) requires notice of an accident in the employer's premises causing death or serious bodily injury, the person required to give it must within seven days send a report to the competent authority stating the circumstances (or, if the State so specifies, to the authority to whom the notice is due).
  • “Serious bodily injury” = injury involving (or in all probability involving) permanent loss of use of / permanent injury to any limb, permanent loss of or injury to sight or hearing, fracture of any limb, or enforced absence from work exceeding twenty days.
  • State Government may extend the duty to other classes of premises (§73(2)). Not applicable to establishments covered by ESI (§73(3)).
  • Purpose: enables the competent authority to trigger §88 (calling for the employer's statement and informing dependants) — the State's proactive role in fatal cases.
Example

In the Mundka (Delhi) commercial-building fire of 13 May 2022, 27 persons, mostly workers of a CCTV/router assembling unit, died. For a unit outside ESI, the occupier would owe a §73 report within seven days; the competent authority could then issue §88 notices requiring the employer to state whether it accepts liability to deposit compensation for each deceased employee within 30 days.

§74 · Employer's liability for compensation old §3

Fully analysed in Part C. Sub-section map: (1) liability + three-day rule + three defences; (2) breach of law/orders for employer's business deemed within employment; (3) occupational diseases (Second Schedule employment + Third Schedule disease + six months' service where required) deemed injury by accident, presumed to arise out of employment unless contrary proved; (4) commuting accidents on the nexus test; (5) Government may modify/add employments and diseases by notification after three months' notice; (6) other diseases only if directly attributable to a specific injury by accident; (7) bar on double remedy (civil suit v. compensation claim).

§75 · Compensation for death or injury by collapse of a house in a plantation new to the EC scheme

If death or injury is caused to a plantation worker or a member of his family by the collapse of a house provided by the employer in a plantation, and the collapse is not solely and directly attributable to a fault of any occupant or to a natural calamity, the employer must pay compensation under §76 and the Sixth Schedule “so far as may be applicable”. “Worker” here is specially defined (persons employed for hire/reward, incl. contract workers for > 60 days; excluding medical officers, high-wage staff, managerial staff and temporary construction workers). Drawn from the plantation labour law it extends no-fault liability to a non-work event and to family members — a unique feature.

§77 · Compensation to be paid when due; interest and damages for default old §4A
  • Compensation “shall be paid as soon as it falls due” (§77(1)) — it falls due on the date of the accident, not on adjudication: Pratap Narain Singh Deo (1976) 1 SCC 289; reaffirmed by a three-judge bench in Oriental Insurance Co. v. Siby George (2012) 12 SCC 540 (interest runs from the date of accident).
  • If the employer disputes the extent of liability he must make a provisional payment of the admitted amount (§77(2)).
  • Default beyond one month: the competent authority shall direct interest at the prescribed rate (the 1923 Act fixed 12% p.a.; under the Code the rate is prescribed by the Central Government) and, if there is no justification for the delay, may impose damages up to 50% of the arrears after a show-cause opportunity (§77(3)). Both go to the employee/dependant (§77(4)).
  • Insurer's position: under a policy covering EC liability the insurer must pay the compensation and the interest, but the penalty/damages for the employer's default is the employer's personal liability (Ved Prakash Garg v. Premi Devi (1997) 8 SCC 1).
§78 · Method of calculating monthly wages old §5
  • (a) Service of ≥ 12 months with the liable employer: monthly wages = 1/12 of the total wages that fell due in the last 12 months.
  • (b) Service of < 1 month: the average monthly earnings, during the preceding 12 months, of an employee on the same work with the same employer (or similar work in the locality).
  • (c) Other cases (1–12 months, or where (b) cannot be applied): 30 × total wages for the last continuous period ÷ number of days in that period. A period of service is continuous unless interrupted by absence exceeding 14 days.
  • “Wages” = §2(88) (basic + DA + retaining allowance, with the 50% add-back). Then apply the §76(3) ceiling.
§79 Review · §80 Commutation of half-monthly payments old §§6, 7
  • Review (§79): a half-monthly payment (by agreement or order) may be reviewed on application by employer or employee with a medical certificate of change in condition (or without certificate under State rules); it may be continued, increased, decreased or ended, or converted into the lump sum for permanent disablement, less amounts already paid.
  • Commutation (§80): by agreement, or — if payments have continued for ≥ 6 months — on application by either party, the competent authority may redeem half-monthly payments for a lump sum.
§81 · Distribution of compensation — compulsory deposit old §8
  • No direct payment of death compensation, or of a lump sum to a woman or a person under legal disability, is valid: it must be deposited with the competent authority; a direct payment “shall not be deemed to be a payment of compensation” (§81(1)). Exception: the employer may advance up to three months' wages to a dependant, to be adjusted.
  • Other sums ≥ Rs 5,000 may be deposited; the authority's receipt is a sufficient discharge (§81(2)–(3)).
  • On deposit for a deceased employee, notice to dependants; inquiry; if no dependant exists, refund to employer; apportionment among dependants “in such proportion as the competent authority thinks fit” or wholly to one — after hearing and with recorded reasons (§81(4)–(5)).
  • Sums for women/persons under disability may be invested or applied for their benefit (§81(7)); orders may be varied for neglect of children or changed circumstances (§81(8)); recovery of amounts obtained by fraud/impersonation as arrears of land revenue (§81(9)–(10)).
Why it matters

If the employer hands the widow a cheque privately, it does not discharge him; the dependants can still claim and the employer can only seek adjustment. The rule protects illiterate dependants from under-settlements (read with §89 and Roshan Deen).

§82 · Notice and claim (limitation two years) old §10
  • Notice of the accident “as soon as practicable” + claim within two years of the accident or death. For occupational diseases the accident is deemed to occur on the first day of continuous absence (or when notice of disablement is given, or when symptoms are first detected within two years after leaving a Second Schedule employment).
  • Want/defect of notice is no bar if (a) death occurred on the employer's premises/place of work or the worker died without leaving the vicinity, or (b) the employer had knowledge from any source; and the authority may condone delay in notice or claim for sufficient cause (proviso) — construed liberally for illiterate claimants.
  • Notice contents; service on the employer/manager; notice-book may be prescribed; service by registered post, electronically or by entry in the notice-book.
§83 · Accidents outside India — seamen, aircrew, employees of Indian companies abroad, drivers/helpers sent abroad old §§15, 15A, 15B
  • Notice/claim may be served on the master of the ship, captain of the aircraft, or the local agent. No notice needed if the accident happened on board.
  • Claim periods: one year after news of death; eighteen months where the ship/aircraft is lost with all hands; condonation for sufficient cause.
  • Depositions by a Judge/Magistrate/Consular Officer admissible if authenticated and cross-examination was possible; no half-monthly payment while the shipowner is liable to maintain the seaman under merchant shipping law.
§84 · Medical examination old §11

After notice, the employee must submit to free medical examination offered within three days (and periodically while receiving half-monthly payments — frequency limited by State rules). Refusal/obstruction, or leaving the vicinity before examination, suspends the right to compensation (revivable; condonable by the medical practitioner for circumstances beyond control). Unreasonable refusal of treatment that aggravates the injury → disablement assessed as if treatment had been followed (§84(6)). Death during suspension: authority may still pay dependants (§84(4)).

§85 Contracting · §86 Remedies against stranger · §87 Insolvency old §§12, 13, 14
  • §85: where an employer contracts out work “ordinarily part of the trade or business” to a contractor, the principal employer is liable to the contractor's employees as if he employed them directly (wages reckoned as under the contractor), with a right of indemnity against the contractor; the employee may instead sue the contractor; applies only to accidents “on, in or about the premises” where the principal undertakes the work or which are under his control (§85(4)). Aim: prevent evasion through sham contractors.
  • §86: an employer (or indemnifier) who paid compensation for an injury caused by a third party's tort is entitled to be indemnified by that tortfeasor (e.g., the negligent lorry owner in Agnes-type facts).
  • §87: on the employer's insolvency/winding up, the employer's rights against his insurer vest in the employee; compensation liabilities rank as priority debts under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 and Companies Act, 2013; half-monthly payments valued at their commuted lump sum.
§88 Fatal-accident statements · §89 Registration of agreements old §§10A, 28–29
  • §88: on information of a death, the competent authority may require the employer (by registered post/electronically) to state within 30 days the circumstances and whether he accepts liability; if he accepts, deposit within 30 days; if he disclaims, the authority may inform dependants of their right to claim and may even provide an advocate from the State panel to a dependant unable to engage one (§88(5) — new).
  • §89: lump-sum settlements (and any compensation settled for a woman or person under disability) must be sent as a memorandum for registration; the authority may refuse to register for inadequacy, fraud or undue influence and make just orders; an unregistered agreement leaves the employer liable for the full statutory amount and unable to deduct more than half of what he paid (§89(3)). Registered agreements are enforceable notwithstanding the Contract Act.

ESI touch-points — coverage, casual workers and the Turf Club litigation

Chapter IV of the Code (ESI Act, 1948) as it bears on Unit 8: who is an “employee”, what is a “shop”, and why Royal Western India Turf Club is in the syllabus

F.1 The ESI scheme in one view

  • Applicability (First Schedule): every establishment with 10+ persons (except seasonal factories); notified hazardous occupations even with one employee; plantations by option. Under the 1948 Act, non-factory establishments (shops, hotels, cinemas, road transport, newspapers, educational and medical institutions) were covered only by State notifications under §1(5) — the source of the “shop” controversy.
  • Employee (old §2(9); now §2(26) with a wage ceiling notified under §2(89) — Rs 21,000/month; Rs 25,000 for persons with disability): includes persons employed through an immediate employer/contractor, on work incidental or preliminary to the establishment's work, whether in the premises or elsewhere, and administrative/purchase/sales staff.
  • Contributions (§29): employer 3.25%, employee 0.75% of wages (employees below the notified daily wage exempt from their share); contribution period ≤ 6 months (Rule 2(2A) of the 1950 Rules, cited in Turf Club) and corresponding benefit period.
  • Benefits (§32): sickness, maternity, disablement (temporary/permanent), dependants', medical, funeral; plus unemployment allowance (Rajiv Gandhi Shramik Kalyan Yojana / Atal Beemit Vyakti Kalyan Yojana) by regulation.
  • Employment injury deeming: §§34–36 (presumption; emergency; commuting; employer's transport; breach of law; occupational disease) — see Part C.6.
  • Adjudication: Employees' Insurance Court (§§48–51); appeal to High Court on a substantial question of law within 60 days (§52).
Welfare construction — the interpretive engine

The Supreme Court has repeatedly read the ESI Act expansively: Regional Director, ESIC v. South India Flour Mills AIR 1986 SC 1686 (casual workers building a factory are employees, overruling Gnanambikai Mills (Mad)); Hindu Jea Band v. RD, ESIC (1987) 2 SCC 101 (a band shop selling music services is a “shop”); Cochin Shipping Co. v. ESIC (1992) 4 SCC 245 (clearing & forwarding agents — “namely” in a notification is illustrative); ESIC v. R.K. Swamy (1994) 1 SCC 445 (advertising agency = shop); Transport Corpn. of India v. ESIC (2000) 1 SCC 332; Bombay Anand Bhavan Restaurant v. Dy. Director, ESIC (2009) 9 SCC 61 (courts may “strain” language to advance the object). Turf Club (2016) sits in this line: “The Act is a welfare legislation and is required to be interpreted so as to ensure extension of benefits to the employees and not to deprive them.”

Under the Code

The “shop” question is now largely historical: Chapter IV applies to every establishment with ten or more persons irrespective of nomenclature (First Schedule), and §2(76) defines “shop” by reference to the State Shops Act only where the word is used. But the casual-worker holding remains fully alive: §2(26) covers anyone “employed on wages… whether the terms of employment be express or implied”, and the contribution/wage-period machinery in §29 and the Regulations continues to accommodate employment for part of a wage period. The anti-consent principle (a statutory notification cannot be contracted out of) is reinforced by §161.

Gratuity — Chapter V, §§53–58

Definition & scope, eligibility, computation, continuous service, nomination, determination and disputes, compulsory insurance, competent authority [Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972]

G.1 Concept, definition and scope

Gratuity (from gratuitas — a gift) began as a voluntary payment by a grateful employer; industrial adjudication converted it into a claimable retiral benefit — “a reward for long and meritorious service” payable in addition to provident fund (Indian Hume Pipe Co. v. Workmen AIR 1960 SC 251; Garment Cleaning Works v. Workmen AIR 1962 SC 673 — forfeiture only to the extent of loss caused; Delhi Cloth & General Mills v. Workmen AIR 1970 SC 919 — principles for gratuity schemes). The Kerala (1970) and West Bengal (1971) Acts led to the central Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, now re-enacted as Chapter V. It is a statutory right, a form of deferred wage / social security for old age, not dependent on the employer's profits (unlike bonus) and not a matter of contract (Bakshish Singh v. Darshan Engineering Works (1994) 1 SCC 9 — payable even on resignation; a contract cannot take it away).

Definition under the Code: the Code does not define “gratuity” as a term; §53 defines it operationally — a sum payable by the employer at 15 days' wages for every completed year of service (or part in excess of six months) on the termination of employment after five years' continuous service (with exceptions). “Wages” for gratuity = §2(88) (basic + DA + retaining allowance, with the 50% add-back rule); “completed year of service” = continuous service for twelve months (§2(17)); “superannuation” = the age fixed in the contract/conditions of service (§2(82)); “retirement” = termination otherwise than on superannuation (§2(70)).

Scope (First Schedule, Chapter V) and who is an “employee”
  • (a) every factory, mine, oilfield, plantation, port and railway company; (b) every shop or establishment with ten or more employees on any day of the preceding twelve months; and notified shops/establishments (educational institutions were notified under the 1972 Act by S.O. 1080 of 3 Apr 1997). Once applicable, continues even if numbers fall (§1(8)).
  • Employee = §2(26) — every category of work “manual… managerial, administrative, technical, clerical or any other work”; no wage ceiling (removed from the PG Act in 1994). Teachers are employees: Birla Institute of Technology v. State of Jharkhand (2019), following the 2009 retrospective amendment of §2(e) PG Act (see Part I).
  • Excluded (Expl. 1 to §53): persons holding posts under the Central/State Government governed by any other Act or rules providing for gratuity (e.g., CCS (Pension) Rules); apprentices under the Apprentices Act (§2(26)).
  • Contract labour: gratuity is the contractor's liability as employer (labour.gov.in FAQ, 16 Mar 2026); the principal employer's liability arises only under the contract labour provisions of the OSH Code if the contractor defaults.

G.2 §53 — Payment of gratuity (eligibility, rate, ceiling, forfeiture) old §4

§53(1) — When payable

On termination of employment after continuous service of not less than five years — (a) on superannuation; (b) on retirement or resignation; (c) on death or disablement due to accident or disease; (d) on termination of the contract period under fixed term employment; (e) on any other notified event.

  • Working journalists: “five years” read as three years (first proviso).
  • Five years not necessary where termination is due to death, disablement, expiry of fixed-term employment or a notified event (second proviso).
  • Death: paid to the nominee, or if none, to heirs; a minor's share is deposited with the competent authority to be invested for the minor (third proviso).
  • “Disablement” (Expl. 2): such disablement as incapacitates an employee for the work he was capable of performing before the accident or disease.

§53(2) — Rate

  • 15 days' wages (or such number of days as notified) for every completed year of service or part thereof in excess of six months, at the rate of wages last drawn.
  • Monthly-rated employee: 15 days' wages = monthly wages ÷ 26 × 15 (Expl. 3) — i.e., wages × 15/26 × years.
  • Piece-rated: daily wages = average of total wages for the three months preceding termination, excluding overtime.
  • Seasonal establishment (employee not employed throughout the year): seven days' wages per season.
  • Fixed-term employees and deceased employees: gratuity on a pro-rata basis (third proviso) — a Code innovation.

§53(3)–(5) — Ceiling, reduced wages, better terms

  • Ceiling: “such amount as may be notified” — currently Rs 20 lakh (notification of 29 Mar 2018 under the 1972 Act, saved by §164(2)); Government employees' ceiling is Rs 25 lakh under pension rules.
  • Employee re-employed after disablement on reduced wages: pre-disablement service at pre-disablement wages; post-disablement service at reduced wages (§53(4)).
  • Better terms under any award, agreement or contract are saved (§53(5)); read with §161.

§53(6) — Forfeiture (notwithstanding §53(1))

  • (a) Services terminated for any act, wilful omission or negligence causing damage or loss to the employer's property → forfeited to the extent of the damage or loss.
  • (b) May be wholly or partially forfeited if services terminated for (i) riotous or disorderly conduct or any other act of violence, or (ii) an act constituting an offence involving moral turpitude committed in the course of employment.
  • Conditions: there must be a termination for the misconduct (not merely a finding after superannuation — Jaswant Singh Gill v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. (2007) 1 SCC 663) and the loss must be quantified; a speaking order after hearing is required. On whether a criminal conviction is necessary for “moral turpitude”: Union Bank of India v. C.G. Ajay Babu (2018) 9 SCC 529 said the offence must be established in a court of law; a 2025 bench in Western Coal Fields Ltd. v. Manohar Govinda Fulzele (2025 INSC 233) held that a disciplinary finding of misconduct amounting to an offence involving moral turpitude (e.g., securing employment on a forged date-of-birth certificate) suffices — conviction is not a pre-condition.
Computation examples
  • Clerk, last drawn wages (basic + DA) Rs 30,000, service 12 years 7 months → 13 years × 30,000 × 15/26 = Rs 2,25,000.
  • Fixed-term data analyst, one-year contract, wages Rs 52,000 → pro rata: 52,000 × 15/26 × 1 = Rs 30,000 on expiry of the contract (no five-year requirement).
  • Worker dies after 2 years 8 months, wages Rs 20,000 → 3 years (part > 6 months) × 20,000 × 15/26 = Rs 34,615 to the nominee; five-year rule inapplicable.
  • Seasonal sugar-mill worker, 10 seasons, daily wage Rs 600 → 7 × 600 × 10 = Rs 42,000.

G.3 §54 Continuous service · §55 Nomination old §§2A, 6

§54 — Continuous service

  • (A) Uninterrupted service, including interruptions by sickness, accident, leave, absence without leave (unless treated as a break under standing orders/rules), lay-off, strike, lock-out or cessation of work not due to the employee's fault — before or after the Code.
  • (B) Deemed continuous service (non-seasonal): for one year if the employee has actually worked ≥ 190 days (below-ground mine / establishment working < 6 days a week) or ≥ 240 days (others) in the preceding 12 months; for six months, ≥ 95 / 120 days. Days “actually worked” include lay-off days, leave with full wages, absence due to temporary disablement from an employment injury, and maternity leave up to 26 weeks.
  • (C) Seasonal establishments: ≥ 75% of the days the establishment operated.
  • Lalappa Lingappa v. Laxmi Vishnu Textile Mills (1981) 2 SCC 238: “actually employed/worked” means days on which the employee worked or is deemed to have worked — badli workers with sporadic attendance fell short; voluntary absence is excluded. Netram Sahu v. State of Chhattisgarh (2018) 5 SCC 430: service as a daily wager before regularisation counts.
  • “4 years and 240 days”: several High Courts (e.g., Madras in Mettur Beardsell (1998)) treat 240 days in the fifth year as completing five years under the deeming clause; the point is not finally settled by the Supreme Court — mention both views in an answer.

§55 — Nomination

  • Every employee who has completed one year of service must nominate in the prescribed form/time; may distribute among several nominees.
  • If the employee has a family, nomination must be in favour of family members — a nomination in favour of an outsider is void; if no family, anyone may be nominated but the nomination becomes invalid on acquiring a family and a fresh nomination must be made.
  • Modification at any time by written intimation; if a nominee predeceases, the interest reverts to the employee who nominates afresh; the employer keeps nominations in safe custody.
  • “Family” = §2(33): spouse, minor/dependent children, dependent parents (including in-laws of a woman employee), and for unmarried employees whose parents are dead, dependent minor siblings.

Maternity Benefit — Chapter VI, §§59–72

Every provision of the Chapter [Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 as amended in 2017], with the case law that gives it teeth

H.1 Object, constitutional basis and key definitions

The Chapter protects the dignity of motherhood and the health of mother and child by (i) prohibiting work during a period around childbirth, (ii) securing paid absence (“maternity benefit” at the average daily wage), (iii) granting ancillary rights (medical bonus, leave for miscarriage/illness, nursing breaks, crèche), and (iv) protecting employment against dismissal or adverse variation. It implements Art. 42 (“just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief”), Arts. 14, 15(3), 21, 39(e)/(f), and India's obligations under CEDAW Art. 11 and the ILO Maternity Protection Conventions (C-3/1919, C-103/1952, C-183/2000). The Supreme Court treats maternity benefits as part of the right to reproductive choice and dignity under Art. 21 (Deepika Singh v. CAT (2022) 7 SCC 1; K. Umadevi v. Govt. of Tamil Nadu 2025 INSC 781).

Applicability (First Schedule): (a) every establishment being a factory, mine or plantation, including Government establishments; (b) every shop or establishment with ten or more employees; (c) other notified establishments. §61: a woman remains entitled under this Chapter notwithstanding ESI coverage of her establishment until she qualifies for ESI maternity benefit under §32 — so no woman falls between the two schemes.

Definitions (§2)
  • “woman” §2(91): a woman employed, directly or through a contractor, for wages in any establishment — no qualifying category or wage ceiling; for Chapter IV, “insured woman” includes a commissioning mother and a woman adopting a child up to three months old.
  • “child” §2(12): includes a still-born child; “delivery” §2(23): birth of a child; “confinement” §2(18): labour resulting in a living child, or labour after 26 weeks resulting in a child alive or dead.
  • “miscarriage” §2(48): expulsion of the contents of a pregnant uterus before or during the 26th week (excluding miscarriage punishable under the IPC/BNS); “medical termination of pregnancy” §2(45): termination permissible under the MTP Act, 1971.
  • “commissioning mother” §2(13): a biological mother who uses her egg to create an embryo implanted in another woman (surrogacy).
  • “maternity benefit” §2(43): the payment referred to in §60(1).

H.2 The core rights — §§59–63

§59 · Employment of, or work by, women prohibited during certain periods old §4
  • Employer shall not knowingly employ, and no woman shall work, in any establishment during the six weeks immediately following delivery, miscarriage or MTP (§59(1)–(2)) — an absolute prohibition, reflecting the ILO's compulsory post-natal leave.
  • On request, a pregnant woman shall not be required to do arduous work, work involving long hours of standing, or work likely to interfere with pregnancy/foetal development or cause miscarriage/harm — during (a) the one month preceding the six weeks before expected delivery and (b) any part of those six weeks she does not take as leave (§59(3)–(4)). “Arduous” = strenuous, difficult and tiring (Expl.). No wage cut for the lighter assignment (§69).
§60 · Right to payment of maternity benefit old §5
  • Entitlement and liability (§60(1)): every woman is entitled to, and her employer is liable for, maternity benefit at the average daily wage for the period of her actual absence — the period immediately preceding delivery, the day of delivery and the period following. Average daily wage = average of wages payable for the days worked in the three calendar months preceding the absence, subject to the minimum wage under the Code on Wages, 2019.
  • Qualifying condition (§60(2)): actual work for not less than 80 days in the 12 months immediately preceding the expected date of delivery, in the establishment of the employer from whom benefit is claimed. Days of lay-off and paid holidays count (Expl.). The medical bonus (§64) follows the entitlement — no separate qualifying condition.
  • Maximum period (§60(3)): 26 weeks, of which not more than 8 weeks may precede the expected delivery; for a woman with two or more surviving children: 12 weeks, not more than 6 before delivery.
  • Death: if the woman dies during the period, benefit only up to the day of death; but if she dies during or after delivery leaving the child, the employer is liable for the entire period (till the child's death if the child also dies) — the last proviso relied on in Dr. Kavita Yadav to show that benefits are not tied to continued employment.
  • Adoptive and commissioning mothers (§60(4)): 12 weeks from the date the child (below three months, for adoption) is handed over.
  • Work from home (§60(5)): after availing the benefit, if the nature of work permits, on mutually agreed terms.

§68 · Dismissal for absence during pregnancy old §12
  • While a woman is absent in accordance with the Chapter, it is unlawful to discharge or dismiss her during or on account of the absence, to give notice expiring during it, or to vary her conditions of service to her disadvantage (§68(1)).

Overriding effect — §161 [old §27]

The Chapter prevails over any inconsistent law, award, agreement or contract of service, saving more favourable terms. This is why a fixed-term clause cannot cut short the 26 weeks (Kavita Yadav, para 14–15) and why a university resolution excluding contractual teachers from maternity leave could not stand (Manisha Priyadarshini v. 1. General Manager, B.E.S.T. Undertaking, Bombay v. Mrs. Agnes

(1964) 3 SCR 930 · AIR 1964 SC 193 · decided 10 May 1963 · Supreme Court, three judges · majority: K. Subba Rao J.; dissent: Raghubar Dayal J. · Workmen's Compensation Act, 1923, §3(1) [now Code §74(1)]
ClaimantMrs. Agnes, widow of Nanu Raman, bus driver of the B.E.S.T. Undertaking (Bombay Municipal Corporation)ClaimRs 3,500 under §3(1) WC Act before the Commissioner for Workmen's Compensation, BombayCourse of litigationCommissioner dismissed → Bombay High Court allowed the appeal → Supreme Court (by majority) affirmed the High Court
Facts

Nanu Raman was a driver in the Undertaking's city bus service. On 20 July 1957 he finished his shift at about 7.45 p.m. at the Jogeshwari depot. To go home to Santa Cruz he boarded another B.E.S.T. bus, travelling under a facility given to the Undertaking's staff. Near Erla Bridge, Andheri, the bus collided with a stationary lorry; Raman was thrown out, sustained serious injuries and died in hospital on 26 July 1957. The Undertaking's Standing Orders/Rules for traffic staff were in evidence — inter alia Rule 19 (drivers and conductors in uniform may travel free in the Undertaking's buses, with limits on the number of staff who may travel in one bus), and rules showing that drivers were posted to various depots across Greater Bombay, had split duties and could be called to duty from home, and that crews were expected to reach depots punctually even when far from their residence. The Commissioner held the accident did not arise in the course of employment; the High Court reversed.

Issue

Whether the death of a bus driver, who after finishing his duty was travelling home in another bus of the employer under a free-travel facility, was caused by an accident “arising out of and in the course of his employment” within §3(1) — i.e., did the course of employment extend to the return journey in the employer's own bus?

Arguments
Appellant (B.E.S.T. Undertaking)
  • The employment ended when the driver handed over his bus at the depot; thereafter he was a member of the travelling public availing a concession.
  • The English House of Lords' “obligation” test governs: Cremins v. Guest, Keen & Nettlefolds (1908) 1 KB 469; St. Helens Colliery Co. v. Hewitson [1924] AC 59; Alderman v. Great Western Rly [1937] AC 454 — a workman travelling to or from work is not in the course of employment unless he is contractually bound to use that means of transport; a mere privilege of free travel is not enough.
  • Rule 19 is a bare concession; it does not oblige the driver to travel by bus; he was under no control of the employer while travelling; there was no duty to be performed on that bus.
  • Notional extension applies only to the employer's premises and their means of access, not to the public streets of Bombay.
Respondent (widow) & amicus curiae (Mr. Ganapati Iyer)
  • The test is not contractual obligation but nexus: was the workman's presence at the place of the accident so related to his employment that he was acting within its scope? (Weaver v. Tredegar Iron & Coal Co. [1940] 3 All ER 157, HL; Evershed MR's formulation.)
  • The Supreme Court has already accepted the theory of notional extension of the employer's premises in time and space (Saurashtra Salt Mfg. Co. v. Bai Valu Raja AIR 1958 SC 881) — what counts as “premises” must be adapted to the nature of the undertaking.
  • For a city transport service the whole fleet is the premises; free travel is provided in the interest of the service (long distances, split shifts, punctuality) — a proved necessity generating an implied obligation to use it; the driver used it as an employee, not as a member of the public.
  • The Act is a beneficial statute to be liberally construed.
Decision — majority (Subba Rao J.)
  • Reviewed the English authorities and accepted the wider nexus test of Weaver; applied Saurashtra Salt's four propositions.
  • Para 12: “the employment does not necessarily end when the ‘down tool’ signal is given or when the workman leaves the actual workshop… There is a notional extension of both the entry and exit by time and space. The scope of such extension must necessarily depend on the circumstances of a given case.”
  • Para 14: the doctrine developed for factories and harbours “equally applies to such a bus service, [but] the doctrine necessarily will have to be adapted to meet its peculiar requirements… in the case of a city transport service, by analogy, the entire fleet of buses forming the service would be the ‘premises’.” The free transport was provided “in the interest of service, having regard to the long distance a driver has to traverse… the user of the said buses is a proved necessity giving rise to an implied obligation on his part to travel in the said buses as a part of his duty. He is not exercising the right as a member of the public, but only as one belonging to a service.” The illustration of a staff bus: “what difference would it make if the employer, instead of providing a separate bus, throws open his entire fleet… They are given that facility not as members of the public but as employees; not as a grace but as of right because efficiency of the service demands it.”
  • Held: “when a driver when going home from the depot or coming to the depot uses the bus, any accident that happens to him is an accident in the course of his employment.” Appeal dismissed; the widow entitled to compensation (quantum not challenged).
Dissent (Raghubar Dayal J.)
  • Rule 19 created only a concession, hedged by limits (a fixed number of staff per bus; no guaranteed seat) and extended to others such as councillors and police — it was not a term of the contract of service and imposed no obligation.
  • Applying Hewitson (Lord Buckmaster: the workman “was under no control… nor bound in any way either to use the train or… to obey directions; though he was where he was in consequence of his employment, I do not think it was in its course that the accident occurred”), Raman was off duty once he left the bus he had driven; in the second bus he was under no control and had no duty to perform; the accident was “in consequence of” but not “in the course of” employment. He would have allowed the appeal.
Principle evolved. (1) The course of employment is subject to a notional extension in time and space; its scope is a question of fact in each case. (2) The extension is adapted to the nature of the undertaking: for a city transport service the whole fleet is the “premises”. (3) A facility provided in the interest of the service and used as of right creates an implied obligation; the English “contractual obligation” test is not the touchstone in India — the nexus between the employment and the presence at the place of accident is. (4) Beneficial legislation is construed liberally. — Agnes was later given statutory recognition in ESI §51C (1968; now Code §34(4)) and treated in Daivshala as the fountainhead of the commuting-accident rule.
Exam angles
  • “Which view is more persuasive?” — majority: purposive, recognises the realities of a metropolitan bus service and the employer's interest in punctual, rested crews; dissent: doctrinally tidy but would leave most transit workers unprotected and rests on an English test that Parliament itself displaced (ESI §51C). Note the dissent's fair point that unlimited extension makes liability uncertain — answered today by the statutory nexus filter (§74(4)).
  • Distinguish Agnes from Jain's ticket-checker problem (personal court case — purpose foreign to service) and from Saurashtra Salt (public ferry, not the employer's).

2. Daivshala & Others v. Oriental Insurance Company Ltd. & Another

2025 INSC 904 · 2025 SCC OnLine SC 1534 · Supreme Court · judgment by K.V. Viswanathan J. · Employee's Compensation Act, 1923, §3(1) [now Code §74(1), (4)]
DeceasedShahu Sampatrao Jadhavar, night watchman at a sugar factory (respondent No. 2), Osmanabad district, MaharashtraAccident22 April 2003, while proceeding to the factory for the 3 a.m.–11 a.m. shift; spot about 5 km from the workplaceCourse of litigationCommissioner for Workmen's Compensation & Civil Judge (Sr. Dn.), Osmanabad — WC Application No. 28/2005 allowed on 26 June 2009 → Bombay High Court (Aurangabad Bench), First Appeal No. 2015/2011, reversed on 1 Dec 2011 → Supreme Court allowed the dependants' appeal and restored the award
Facts

The deceased was employed as a watchman — a Second Schedule employment [item (xxxiii)]. His duty hours on 22 April 2003 were 3 a.m. to 11 a.m. While he was on his way to the factory to report in time, he met with a road accident at a place about five kilometres from the factory and died. His widow Daivshala and other dependants claimed compensation under the EC Act against the employer and its insurer, Oriental Insurance. The Commissioner allowed the claim; the High Court, in the insurer's first appeal, held that an accident on a public road five kilometres away, before the commencement of duty, could not be said to arise out of and in the course of employment, relying on the line of cases that restricted notional extension (notably Francis De Costa).

Issues
  1. Whether an accident occurring to an employee while commuting from residence to the place of work (or back) can be an accident “arising out of and in the course of his employment” under §3(1) of the EC Act.
  2. Whether the principle in §51E of the ESI Act (inserted in 2010) — deeming commuting accidents to be employment injuries where a nexus of circumstances, time and place exists — can inform the interpretation of the EC Act, and whether §51E is clarificatory (retrospective).
Arguments
Appellants (dependants)
  • The theory of notional extension (Saurashtra Salt; Agnes) covers a watchman travelling at an unearthly hour solely to reach his post on time; the employment “as such — its nature, conditions, obligations and incidents” (Mackinnon Mackenzie) placed him on the road at 2–3 a.m.
  • The EC Act and the ESI Act are cognate social-security statutes in pari materia; ESI §51C (1968) codified Agnes, and §51E (2010) codified the commuting rule; both should guide the reading of §3 EC Act, as the 62nd Law Commission had recommended.
  • Beneficial legislation must be construed to advance the remedy; the Commissioner's finding of nexus was one of fact.
Respondents (insurer / employer)
  • The three-judge decision in Regional Director, ESIC v. Francis De Costa (1996) 6 SCC 1 holds that an accident on a public road while going to work, away from the premises, is not an employment injury; the High Court rightly followed it.
  • §51E is confined to the ESI Act and is prospective (2010); Parliament chose not to amend the EC Act; courts cannot legislate a deeming provision into §3.
  • Five kilometres from the workplace, before duty commenced, on a public road, in the employee's own conveyance — there was no causal connection with the employment; notional extension cannot be stretched so far.
Decision and reasoning
  • The need for “deeming” and its Indian source (paras 34–39): quoting Lord Denning in Vandyke v. Fender (commuting accidents, even in the employer's transport, need a statutory deeming provision), the Court noted that ESI §51C, introduced w.e.f. 28 Jan 1968, was precisely such a provision — and that it gave “statutory recognition” to the law laid down in Agnes (10 May 1963). This demonstrated “the cognate nature of the EC Act and the ESI Act. Both the statutes seek to ameliorate the conditions of workmen and provide them social security benefits.” Mackinnon Mackenzie's formulation (nature, conditions, obligations, incidents) was reaffirmed.
  • Law Commission and ILO (paras 40–41): the 62nd Report (Oct 1974, Gajendragadkar, CJ (retd.)) recommended adopting §51C into the WC Act without insisting on employer-provided transport for direct journeys, after considering the ILO Convention of 1964 on commuting accidents.
  • The conflicting High Court decisions (paras 42–44): Sadgunaben Amrutlal v. ESIC 1981 Lab IC 1653 (Guj) (bus-stop death covered; theory “an elastic and flexible formula to be applied in a purposeful manner”) — doubted in Francis De Costa; Bhagubai v. Central Railway (1954) 2 LLJ 403 (stabbing in railway premises en route — covered, not disapproved in Francis De Costa). This “parade of case law” showed “considerable doubt and ambiguity” about commuting accidents. “It was to clarify and put beyond doubt the meaning of the phrase… that Section 51E was enacted… we have no manner of doubt that the said amendment is clarificatory in character and will have retrospective effect.”
  • “Deemed to have” (para 45 ff.): in §51E the words are not used to create a legal fiction but “to put beyond doubt a particular construction that might otherwise be uncertain”.
  • Pari materia (paras 52–55): citing Bennion, R v. Wheatley, State of Assam v. Deva Prasad Barua (1969) 1 SCR 698 and Viscount Simonds in AG v. Prince Ernest Augustus of Hanover, the Court held that decisions and definitions under one of two statutes in pari materia may be applied to the other. “Applying the above principle, we interpret the phrase ‘accident arising out of and in the course of his employment’ occurring in Section 3 of the EC Act to include accident occurring to an employee while commuting from his residence to the place of employment for duty or from the place of employment to his residence after performing duty, provided the nexus between the circumstances, time and place in which the accident occurred and the employment is established.”
  • Application (para 56): undisputed facts — employment as watchman; duty 3 a.m.–11 a.m.; proceeding to the workplace when the accident occurred ~5 km away. “Considering that the deceased was a night watchman and was dutifully proceeding to his workplace to be well on time, there was a clear nexus between the circumstances, time and place in which the accident occurred and his employment as watchman.” Appeal allowed; High Court judgment set aside; Commissioner's award of 26 June 2009 restored; no costs (para 57).
Principle evolved. (1) The EC Act and ESI Act are in pari materia; the deeming clauses of the ESI Act (§§51C, 51E) inform the meaning of “arising out of and in the course of employment” in the EC Act. (2) §51E is clarificatory and retrospective. (3) Commuting accidents (residence ↔ workplace, before or after duty) fall within §3 EC Act if the nexus of circumstances, time and place with the employment is established — a fact-sensitive filter that excludes detours, private errands and unconnected timings. (4) Francis De Costa is thereby confined; the liberal Sadgunaben approach is vindicated. (5) The rule is now statutory for all employees: Code §74(4) (EC) and §34(3) (ESI).
Exam angles
  • Cite as the latest and capstone authority on notional extension, after Saurashtra Salt and Agnes; quote the nexus formula exactly.
  • Cornell's predicted problem (“watchman 3 km from the factory”) and the Dec 2024 “heart attack en route to hospital” question both turn on the time–place–circumstances nexus.
  • Critique: does the “nexus” test risk uncertainty? Answer: it mirrors §51E/§74(4), tracks ILO C-121, and the burden remains on the claimant; the doctrine is a shield for genuine work-connected journeys, not a general accident insurance.

3. Royal Western India Turf Club Ltd. v. Employees' State Insurance Corporation & Others

(2016) 4 SCC 521 · AIR 2016 SC 1143 · Civil Appeal No. 49 of 2006 (with CA 1575/2006, 3421–3422/2012) · decided 29 Feb 2016 · V. Gopala Gowda & Arun Mishra JJ (judgment by Arun Mishra J.) · ESI Act, 1948, §§1(5), 2(9), 2(22), 2(23), 39, 42; ESI (Central) Rules, 1950, r. 2(2A); ESI (General) Regulations, 1950, regs. 26–31, 36
AppellantRoyal Western India Turf Club Ltd. — conducts horse-racing at the Mahalaxmi Race Course (Mumbai) and PunePrior roundThree-judge bench, 31 July 2014 (Bangalore Turf Club Ltd. v. RD, ESIC (2014) 9 SCC 657): a turf club is a “shop” within the Maharashtra notification under §1(5) — ESI Act applies; remaining issues remitted to a Division BenchPeriod in dispute1978-79 to 1982-83 and subsequent years; High Court had limited recovery to the period from 1987
Facts

Several departments of the Club (workshop, garage, security, carpentry, personnel, accounts) had been covered under the ESI Act from 28 January 1968. In ESI Application No. 16 of 1976 before the ESI Court, Bombay, the Club and the Corporation filed consent terms (based on an Inspection Report of 29 Nov 1975 and a Memorandum of 14 Apr 1976) recording that those departments were and would remain covered, while “the employees of Racing Administration Department, casual labour engaged on race track, temporary staff engaged on race days for issue of tickets/dividends were not covered.” On 18 September 1978 the Government of Maharashtra issued a fresh notification under §1(5) extending the Act to the remaining departments of the Club. The Corporation demanded contributions in respect of the casual/temporary race-day staff from 1978-79. The ESI Court and, in part, the Bombay High Court (21 Oct 2005) upheld coverage of temporary race-day staff but the High Court directed that no recovery be made for the period before 1987. Both sides appealed.

Issues
  1. Whether casual workers — temporary staff engaged on race days to issue tickets and pay dividends, and casual labour on the race track — are “employees” under §2(9) of the ESI Act.
  2. Whether the Corporation was bound by the consent terms of 1976 excluding such staff.
  3. From which date the Club was liable — 1978-79 (date of the notification) or 1987 as directed by the High Court.
Arguments
Turf Club (Mr. J.P. Cama, Sr. Adv.)
  • Race-day staff work only on racing days for a few hours; such sporadic engagement does not make them “employees” employed “for wages in or in connection with the work” of the establishment.
  • The consent terms in Application No. 16/1976 specifically excluded casual race-track labour and temporary ticket staff; the Corporation is bound by them.
  • Rule 2(2A) (contribution period not exceeding six consecutive months) and Regulations 29 and 31 make it impracticable to compute contributions for persons working a few days; the scheme presupposes regular employment.
  • The High Court's direction not to recover contributions before 1987 should stand — ESIC v. Hyderabad Race Club (2004) 6 SCC 191 waived arrears for a period of bona fide doubt.
  • ESIC v. Premier Clay Products 1994 Supp (3) SCC 567: coolies who work for several employers on the same day are not employees.
ESI Corporation
  • §2(9) is deliberately wide: any person employed for wages on any work of, or incidental or preliminary to, or connected with the work of the establishment — race-day staff do the Club's core work.
  • The notification of 18 Sept 1978 specifically covered the departments in question; the consent terms related to an earlier period and a different notification (26 Feb 1976); they even acknowledged coverage of the Club since 1968.
  • §39(4) and §42(3) expressly contemplate employees employed for part of a wage period — i.e., casual employees; the Regulations (reg. 36) fix when their contribution falls due.
  • Welfare legislation — South India Flour Mills (casual construction workers covered), Davangere Cotton Mills, Oswal Woollen Mills.
Decision and reasoning
  • Casual workers are employees (paras 5–12): “The definition of ‘employee’ is very wide… The Act is a welfare legislation and is required to be interpreted so as to ensure extension of benefits to the employees and not to deprive them.” §39(4) shows that “an employee who is employed for a part of the wage period is also covered for the purposes of contribution”; §42(2) does not prescribe any minimum period of work; §§2(22)–(23) (wages, wage period) “does not exclude the wages payable to casual workers”; Rule 2(2A) prescribes a maximum, not a minimum, contribution period; Regulation 36 fixes the due date for part-period employment — “the intendment of regulation is clear to cover work rendered in part of wage period.” Following South India Flour Mills (which overruled Gnanambikai Mills and held that §§39(4) and 42(3) “clearly envisage the case of casual employees… it is the intention of the Legislature that the casual employees should also be brought within the purview of the Act”), the Club's submission was “too tenuous for its acceptance.” Premier Clay Products was distinguished: there the work was sporadic and the coolies served several employers on the same day; here “the employees' work for the day of racing which is perennial activity of Royal Turf Club.”
  • Consent terms (para 13): the submission was “factually incorrect, misconceived, legally untenable and otherwise also devoid of the substance” — the 1976 terms concerned a prior period; after the 1978 notification the left-out departments were specifically covered; “Notification has statutory force and agreement cannot supersede it.”
  • Period of liability (para 14–15): the 1978 notification was clear; Hyderabad Race Club “turned on its own different factual matrix” (there was genuine doubt whether the Act applied at all); here the Club had complied since 1968 for other departments. High Court erred in quashing the demand for 1978–1987. The Club must pay contributions per the 1978 notification with interest till actual payment, within three months; the Corporation's appeals allowed; the Club's appeals dismissed with costs of Rs 2 lakh.
Principle evolved. (1) “Employee” in social-security law is not confined to regular or full-time workers: casual, temporary and part-wage-period workers employed on the establishment's regular activity are employees; the machinery provisions (contribution and wage periods) are adapted to them, not used to exclude them. (2) Sporadic engagement by multiple principals (Premier Clay Products) is distinguished from recurring casual engagement on a perennial activity. (3) A statutory notification cannot be contracted out of — consent terms or agreements do not bind the Corporation against the statute (cf. Code §161). (4) Liability for contributions runs from the date of coverage, with interest; relief from arrears is confined to cases of genuine doubt as to applicability. (5) Reaffirms the liberal-construction canon for the ESI Act — the same canon that drives Agnes, Daivshala and Kavita Yadav.
Exam angles
  • Use with the 2014 reference decision to explain how ESI coverage was extended to “shops” (services) and why the Code's establishment-based threshold (10+) ends that debate.
  • Link to Chapter VII: once ESI applies, race-day employees' injuries (a fall while handling horses — Second Schedule item (xxxvi)) are ESI employment injuries; an uncovered club would be liable under §74.
  • Gig-economy analogy: the “perennial activity/recurring engagement” reasoning is the strongest doctrinal bridge for arguing that platform workers who work regularly for one aggregator are employees — though §2(35)/(61) currently defines them as outside the employer–employee relationship.

4. Dr. Kavita Yadav v. Secretary, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare Department & Others

(2024) 1 SCC 421 · decided 17 Aug 2023 · Supreme Court, three judges: Aniruddha Bose, Sanjay Kumar & S.V.N. Bhatti JJ · Maternity Benefit Act, 1961, §§5, 8, 12(2)(a), 27 [now Code §§60, 64, 68, 161]
AppellantDr. Kavita Yadav, pathologist, Senior Resident (Pathology), Janakpuri Super Speciality Hospital (autonomous institute under the Government of NCT of Delhi)CounselMr. Sourabh Gupta (appellant); Ms. Rachita Garg (respondent employer)Course of litigationEmployer allowed benefit only up to contract expiry → Central Administrative Tribunal rejected her claim → Delhi High Court dismissed the writ (19 Aug 2019) → Supreme Court allowed the appeal
Facts

The appellant was appointed on 6 June 2014 on a purely temporary, residency-scheme basis for one year, extendable annually to a maximum of three years. Her tenure was extended twice, the final term ending on 11 June 2017. On 24 May 2017 she applied for maternity benefit commencing 1 June 2017. The employer granted benefit only for 11 days (1–11 June 2017), treating her entitlement as ending with her contract. During her earlier pregnancy the same employer had extended maternity benefits to her as a contractual employee, and it was undisputed that she had worked more than 80 days in the twelve months preceding her expected delivery (§5(2)). The Tribunal and the High Court accepted the employer's stand that “actual absence” under §5(1) presupposes a subsisting obligation to attend, which ceases with the contract; granting benefit beyond expiry would amount to an unintended extension of the contract.

Issue

“Whether the maternity benefits, as contemplated in the 1961 Act, would apply to a lady employee appointed on contract if the period for which she claims such benefits overshoots the contractual period” (para 6).

Arguments
Appellant
  • Once the prerequisite in §5(2) (80 days' work in the preceding 12 months) is fulfilled, a contractual employee is entitled to the full benefit under §5(3) (26 weeks); entitlement of contractual employees as such was not in dispute (the employer had paid during her first pregnancy).
  • §12(2)(a) shows that the benefit survives discharge/dismissal during pregnancy; §27 gives the Act overriding effect over any contract of service.
  • The Act is a welfare statute implementing Art. 42; MCD v. Female Workers (Muster Roll) extended benefits to daily wagers.
Respondent employer
  • “Once the term or tenure of the contract ends, there cannot be a notional extension of the same by giving the employee the benefits of the 1961 Act in full”; benefits must be confined within the contractual period.
  • “Actual absence” connotes absence from a subsisting employment; after 11 June 2017 there was no employment to be absent from.
  • Female Workers (Muster Roll) is distinguishable — it arose from an Industrial Tribunal award and the muster-roll women had worked for a long period.
Decision and reasoning
  • Para 8: §12(2)(a) “contemplates entitlement to the benefits… even for an employee who is dismissed or discharged at any time during her pregnancy… Thus, continuation of maternity benefits is in-built in the statute itself, where the benefits would survive and continue despite the cessation of employment… such benefits can travel beyond the term of employment also. It is not coterminous with the employment tenure.”
  • Paras 9–11: MCD v. Female Workers (Muster Roll) (2000) 3 SCC 224 — Art. 42 and the Universal Declaration/CEDAW; the Act's benefits available to casual/muster-roll women; Deepika Singh v. CAT — the object is to enable women to participate in the workforce as mothers and workers; the Court read the statute purposively.
  • Para 12: having regard to §27 (overriding effect), “the High Court erred in law in holding that the appellant was not entitled to maternity benefits beyond 11-6-2017.” Para 13: daily wagers too lack unlimited continuity, yet their tenure “stood notionally extended so far as application of maternity benefits… was concerned.”
  • Para 14: the Act speaks of “maternity benefits” (§3(h)), not “leave”; §5(2) fixes accrual, §5(3) the maximum; the last proviso to §5(3) pays the full period even if the woman dies after delivery; “the expression ‘discharge’ is of wide import, and it would include ‘discharge on conclusion of the contractual period’.”
  • Para 15: “once the appellant fulfilled the entitlement criteria specified in Section 5(2)…, she would be eligible for full maternity benefits even if such benefits exceed the duration of her contract. Any attempt to enforce the contract duration term within such period by the employer would constitute ‘discharge’ and attract the embargo specified in Section 12(2)(a)… The law creates a fiction in such a case by treating her to be in employment for the sole purpose of availing maternity benefits under the 1961 Act.”
  • Para 16: High Court and Tribunal orders set aside; employer directed to extend benefits under §§5 and 8 (less sums already paid) within three months; rejection orders quashed; no costs.
Principle evolved. (1) Maternity benefit is a statutory entitlement that accrues on fulfilling §5(2) [§60(2)] and is not coterminous with the tenure of employment. (2) “Discharge” in §12(2)(a) [§68(1) proviso] includes the automatic expiry of a fixed-term contract; the employer cannot invoke the contract to truncate the benefit. (3) A legal fiction deems the woman in employment solely for availing the benefit — no general extension of service, seniority or other rights. (4) The overriding clause (§27 → §161) subordinates contractual tenure clauses. (5) Contractual, ad-hoc and daily-wage women stand on the same footing as regular employees for Chapter VI.
Exam angles
  • Template for “Dr. Dolly” / “Sheetal”-type problems: (i) is she a “woman” employed in a covered establishment? (ii) 80 days in 12 months before expected delivery? (iii) entitlement = 26 weeks (12 if two surviving children) from the date chosen (≤ 8 weeks pre-natal); (iv) contract expiry mid-way is irrelevant — Kavita Yadav; (v) remedies: Inspector-cum-Facilitator (§72), competent authority appeal (§68(2)), writ; (vi) note she cannot demand renewal of the contract itself (contrast Manisha Priyadarshini, where non-renewal was because of maternity and hence discriminatory).
  • Policy critique: the Code's §2(34)(b) (fixed-term employees get all statutory benefits proportionately) plus §60 now make the result explicit; the remaining gap is women outside any employer relationship (gig/platform), addressed only through §114 schemes.

5. Birla Institute of Technology v. State of Jharkhand & Others

AIR 2019 SC 1309 · Civil Appeal No. 2530 of 2012 · orders of 7 Jan 2019 (recalled) and 7 Mar 2019 · Abhay Manohar Sapre & Indu Malhotra JJ · Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, §2(e) as amended by Act 47 of 2009 [now Code §2(26), Chapter V]
AppellantBirla Institute of Technology (BIT), Mesra, Ranchi — “a premier technical educational institute”Respondent No. 4Assistant Professor, joined 16 Sept 1971, superannuated 30 Nov 2001Course of litigationControlling authority (7 Sept 2002): gratuity Rs 3,38,796 with 10% interest → appellate authority dismissed BIT's appeal (15 Apr 2005) → Jharkhand HC, Single Judge dismissed writ (12 Jan 2007) → Division Bench dismissed LPA → Supreme Court
Facts

On superannuation the teacher claimed gratuity under the 1972 Act; BIT refused, contending that a teacher is not an “employee” under §2(e). The authorities and the High Court upheld the claim. In the Supreme Court, the Bench initially (order of 7 January 2019) allowed BIT's appeal on the strength of Ahmedabad Pvt. Primary Teachers Association v. Administrative Officer (2004) 1 SCC 755, which had held that teachers, whose principal function is imparting education, do not perform “skilled, semi-skilled or unskilled, manual, supervisory, technical or clerical work” and hence were not employees. Neither counsel had brought to the Court's notice that Parliament had, by the Payment of Gratuity (Amendment) Act, 2009 (Act 47 of 2009), published on 31 Dec 2009, amended §2(e) retrospectively from 3 April 1997 — the date on which the Act had been extended to educational institutions employing ten or more persons (S.O. 1080, 3 Apr 1997). Realising the error, the Court suo motu recalled its order and re-heard the matter.

Issue

Whether a teacher of an educational institution is an “employee” entitled to gratuity under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 — in the light of the amended §2(e).

Arguments
BIT (Mr. Shambo Nandy)
  • Ahmedabad Pvt. Primary Teachers Association squarely holds that teachers are not employees under §2(e); the authorities below erred in ignoring it.
  • Applying noscitur a sociis, the enumerated categories (manual, supervisory, technical, clerical) do not encompass the intellectual vocation of teaching; Parliament had used wide words (“any kind of work, manual or otherwise”) in the EPF Act but not in the Gratuity Act.
Respondents (State; teacher — Mr. Sunil Roy)
  • The 2004 decision has been legislatively superseded: the Statement of Objects and Reasons of the 2009 Bill expressly refers to Ahmedabad Pvt. Primary Teachers and states that the amendment is “to widen the definition of ‘employee’… in order to extend the benefit of gratuity to the teachers”, retrospectively from 3 Apr 1997.
  • The teacher, having served 30 years and retired in 2001 (after 1997), falls within the amended definition; the award of Rs 3,38,796 with interest is correct.
Decision and reasoning
  • The Court reproduced the unamended §2(e) (“skilled, semi-skilled, or unskilled, manual, supervisory, technical or clerical work”) and the reasoning of the 2004 decision, including its observation that it was for the legislature to consider teachers' gratuity.
  • It then set out the Statement of Objects and Reasons of the 2009 Bill (introduced 24 Feb 2009; re-introduced after lapse; signed by Mallikarjun Kharge, 12 Nov 2009) and the amended §2(e): “‘employee’ means any person (other than an apprentice) who is employed for wages, whether the terms of such employment are express or implied, in any kind of work, manual or otherwise, in or in connection with the work of a factory, mine, oilfield, plantation, port, railway company, shop or other establishment…, but does not include any such person who holds a post under the Central Government or a State Government and is governed by any other Act or by any rules providing for payment of gratuity.”
  • Held: the amendment, being retrospective from 3 Apr 1997, brings teachers within the Act; “the decision rendered in Ahmedabad Pvt. Primary Teachers Association has lost its binding effect”; reliance on it was “wholly misplaced”. The earlier order of 7 Jan 2019 was recalled; the appeal was dismissed; BIT directed to pay the gratuity as determined with interest, with costs of Rs 25,000 to the teacher.
Principle evolved. (1) Teachers are “employees” entitled to gratuity, retrospectively from 3 April 1997. (2) A judicial interpretation of a definition can be legislatively overridden with retrospective effect; the earlier precedent then ceases to bind. (3) Courts may recall their own orders passed per incuriam (in ignorance of a statutory amendment) to prevent injustice. (4) Under the Code, §2(26) (“any other work”) makes the point explicit; educational institutions with ten or more employees are covered by Chapter V through the First Schedule/notifications.
Exam angles
  • Nov-Dec 2025 Q.8(b) (“Gratuity Act and teachers”): trace Ahmedabad Pvt. Primary Teachers (2004) → 2009 amendment (retrospective to 3 Apr 1997) → BIT (2019) → Code §2(26).
  • Note the exclusion of Government post-holders governed by other gratuity rules (Expl. 1 to §53) — e.g., Kendriya Vidyalaya teachers under CCS (Pension) Rules.

Supporting cases (cite around the five)

Saurashtra Salt Manufacturing Co. v. Bai Valu Raja, AIR 1958 SC 881 (Imam J.)

Facts: workmen of the salt works at Porbandar crossed a creek by a public ferry boat to reach the works; the boat capsized and several drowned. Held: the theory of notional extension is accepted (four propositions — Part C.5), but the accident occurred on a public ferry available to all, beyond the area of notional extension; claims dismissed. Principle: the extension covers the area a workman “passes and repasses” under the employer's control or necessity, not public transport used as a member of the public.

Mackinnon Mackenzie & Co. (P) Ltd. v. Ibrahim Mahmmed Issak, (1969) 2 SCC 607 (Ramaswami J.)

Facts: a seaman on the appellant's ship was found missing at sea; his dependants claimed compensation. Held: laid down the classic tests — “in the course of” = in the course of the work he is employed to do and what is incidental to it; “arising out of” = injury from a risk incidental to the duties of the service which he would not otherwise have suffered; employment “as such — its nature, its conditions, its obligations and its incidents”; “zone of special danger”; “added peril” exception. On the facts (as summarised in Jain) the unexplained disappearance did not establish that death arose out of employment. Quoted in Daivshala (para 37).

Regional Director, ESIC v. Francis De Costa, (1996) 6 SCC 1 (three judges)

Facts: employee cycling to the factory was hit by the employer's lorry about 1 km away, 15 minutes before duty. Held: not an employment injury — no causal connection; §51C confined to employer's transport; notional extension cannot cover public roads generally; Sadgunaben doubted. Status: confined by Daivshala and overtaken by ESI §51E/Code §§34(3), 74(4); still useful for the proposition that a bare commute without nexus is not covered.

National Iron & Steel Co. v. Manorama Dassi, AIR 1953 Cal 143; Works Manager, E.I. Railway v. Mahabir, AIR 1954 All 132; Bhagubai v. Central Railway, (1954) 2 LLJ 403 (Bom)

Manorama Dassi: a tea-boy sent out by the factory was shot during police firing near the gate — the employment brought him to the locality of danger (street/locality risk; Dennis v. White, Thom v. Sinclair, Brooker v. Borthwick applied) — compensation awarded. Mahabir: a railway workman crossing lines to board the workmen's train home was killed — within the notionally extended premises/means of egress — awarded. Bhagubai: railway employee stabbed in railway premises while going to duty — covered (cited with approval in Daivshala, para 43).

Pratap Narain Singh Deo v. Srinivas Sabata, (1976) 1 SCC 289

Facts: carpenter's left arm amputated above the elbow after a fall at work; employer offered a paltry sum, disputing “total” disablement. Held: a carpenter cannot work with one hand — permanent total disablement (not the scheduled 60%); compensation falls due on the date of the accident, so penalty/interest for default were rightly imposed. Use: Part D (classification) and §77.

Indian Rare Earths Ltd. v. Subaida Beevi, (1981) 2 LLJ 293 (SC); Dedhiben v. New Jehangir Vakil Mills, AIR 1976 SC 1250; State of Rajasthan v. Ram Prasad, (2001) 1 LLJ 177 (SC)

Subaida Beevi: worker cycling to the factory (with a cycle subsidy from the employer) killed while crossing the road at the permitted point near the gate — within the course of employment; no added peril. Dedhiben: worker a few feet from the mill gate about ten minutes before the shift — notional extension applied (as noted in Jain). Ram Prasad: worker killed by lightning at the work site — arose out of employment because the work exposed her to the natural peril.

Manisha Priyadarshini v. Aurobindo College-Evening & Ors. (Delhi High Court, 2019)

Facts: ad-hoc Assistant Professor at a Delhi University college availed maternity leave (14 Jan–24 May 2019); on rejoining she was told her ad-hoc appointment stood terminated (29 May 2019) and was not extended, the College relying on University Resolution 120(8) excluding contractual staff from maternity leave. Held: termination quashed and reinstatement directed — denying continuation because of maternity penalises motherhood and violates Arts. 14, 16 and 21; the MB Act and Art. 42 apply to ad-hoc teachers. Use: Dec 2024 Q.4(c) (ad-hoc employees).

MCD v. Female Workers (Muster Roll), (2000) 3 SCC 224; Deepika Singh v. CAT, (2022) 7 SCC 1

Female Workers: muster-roll women of the Delhi Municipal Corporation held entitled to maternity benefit — the Act is not confined to regular employees; Art. 42, Universal Declaration Art. 25(2), CEDAW Art. 11 invoked; the Tribunal's award upheld. Deepika Singh: a nurse at PGIMER, Chandigarh, denied maternity leave for her first biological child because her husband's two children from an earlier marriage were counted; the Court granted leave, reading the rule purposively in light of Arts. 14, 15, 39, 42, 43 and the reality of atypical families; maternity benefits enable women “to participate in the workforce as both mothers and workers.”

Dec 2024 · Q.6Employees' Compensation — heart attack while proceeding to hospital after a workplace incident
Reconstructed problem (the paper's exact wording is summarised in the Cornell PYQ analysis): A workman meets with an incident at the workplace (injury/acute distress) and is being taken to a hospital; on the way he suffers a heart attack and dies. His dependants claim compensation. The employer contends that death was due to a natural cause (cardiac failure) unconnected with employment and that it occurred outside the premises after work had stopped. Decide.
Plan: §74(1) conditions → “accident” & “personal injury” include internal injuries → causation (workplace incident as the proximate/contributory cause; aggravation) → “in the course of”: journey to hospital is incidental/notional extension → heart-attack case law (Param Pal Singh; Sujatha; contrast Jyothi Ademma, Shakuntala) → burden of proof/inference → defences none → relief.

Issue. Whether the death by heart attack, occurring while the workman was being taken to hospital after a workplace incident, is a “personal injury caused… by accident arising out of and in the course of his employment” under §74(1) of the Code on Social Security, 2020 [§3(1), Employee's Compensation Act, 1923], making the employer liable under §76.

Law. (i) “Accident” means an unlooked-for mishap or untoward event not expected or designed (Fenton v. Thorley); injury includes internal physiological injury such as cardiac failure precipitated by a work event or strain. (ii) “Arising out of” requires a causal connection with the employment “as such — its nature, its conditions, its obligations and its incidents” (Mackinnon Mackenzie v. Ibrahim); a pre-existing weakness does not defeat the claim if the employment contributed to or accelerated the fatal event (Lord Sumner's test in Highley). (iii) “In the course of” is subject to notional extension in time and space (Saurashtra Salt; Agnes) — acts reasonably incidental to employment, including obtaining treatment for an injury sustained at work under the employer's arrangement, remain within the course. (iv) Heart-attack precedents: compensation denied where there was no work strain and the death merely coincided with the workplace (Jyothi Ademma v. Plant Engineer (2006) 5 SCC 513; Shakuntala Chandrakant Shreshti (2007) 11 SCC 668); granted where the stress and strain of the work caused or accelerated the attack (Param Pal Singh v. National Insurance (2013) 3 SCC 409 — long-distance truck driver; NEKRTC v. Sujatha (2019) 20 SCC 297 — bus driver on duty). (v) The competent authority may draw reasonable inferences; the statute is no-fault and beneficially construed (Daivshala, 2025).

Application. Two features distinguish this problem from Jyothi Ademma. First, there is an identifiable workplace incident — an “accident” in the course of employment — which set the chain of events in motion; the cardiac arrest is either its direct sequel (shock, trauma, exertion) or an aggravation of a latent condition by that event. The causa causans is the employment incident; the employer takes the workman as he finds him. Secondly, the journey to hospital was not a private frolic: the workman was where he was because of the workplace incident and (typically) under the employer's direction or arrangement — squarely within the notional extension recognised in Agnes (“the employment does not necessarily end when the ‘down tool’ signal is given”) and consistent with Daivshala's insistence on a nexus of circumstances, time and place. That the death occurred outside the premises is therefore immaterial. The employer's “natural cause” plea would succeed only if medical evidence showed the cardiac event to be wholly independent of the incident (no strain, no shock — the Jyothi Ademma situation). No §74(1)(b) defence (drink/drugs; wilful breach of a safety rule; removal of a safety guard) arises, and in any event those defences are unavailable where death results.

Conclusion. The dependants (§2(24)) are entitled to compensation: 50% of monthly wages (capped per §76(3) notification, Rs 15,000) × the age factor (Sixth Schedule) or the notified minimum, whichever is more; plus funeral expenses (≥ Rs 15,000) and reimbursement of medical expenses (§76(5), (7)); interest from the date of the accident and up to 50% damages if the employer delays without justification (§77; Pratap Narain Singh Deo; Siby George). The amount must be deposited with the competent authority for apportionment (§81). If the establishment were ESI-covered, the claim would instead lie for dependants' benefit, aided by the statutory presumption in §34(1).

Dec 2024 · Q.4(c)Short note — Maternity Benefit Act and ad-hoc employees
Does the Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 (now Chapter VI of the Code on Social Security, 2020) apply to a woman employed on an ad-hoc/contractual basis? Discuss with reference to decided cases.

Answer. Yes. The statute is status-blind: “woman” means a woman employed, whether directly or through any contractor, for wages in any establishment (§3(o) MB Act; §2(91) Code) — there is no requirement of permanency, no wage ceiling and no minimum tenure other than the qualifying condition of 80 days' actual work in the twelve months preceding the expected delivery (§5(2); §60(2)). Three lines of authority settle the point: (1) MCD v. Female Workers (Muster Roll) (2000) 3 SCC 224 — daily-wage muster-roll women are entitled; the Court relied on Art. 42, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and CEDAW, holding that the Act's benefits are not confined to regular employees and that women engaged on casual or muster-roll basis stand on the same footing. (2) Manisha Priyadarshini v. Aurobindo College-Evening (Del HC 2019) — an ad-hoc Assistant Professor of Delhi University denied continuation after maternity leave on the strength of a University resolution excluding contractual staff was reinstated: penalising motherhood violates Arts. 14, 16 and 21; the MB Act applies to ad-hoc teachers. (3) Dr. Kavita Yadav v. Secretary, MoHFW (2024) 1 SCC 421 — a contractual Senior Resident whose three-year tenure expired eleven days into her maternity leave was held entitled to the full benefit: the entitlement accrues on satisfying §5(2), is “not coterminous with the employment tenure”, “discharge” in §12(2)(a) includes discharge on conclusion of the contract, and the law creates a fiction of continued employment for the sole purpose of the benefit; §27 overrides the contract. The Code codifies this: §2(34)(b) (fixed-term employees get all statutory benefits proportionately without the qualifying period), §60 (26 weeks; 12 for two or more surviving children, adoption or commissioning), §68 (no dismissal/variation during absence), §161 (overriding effect). Remedies: complaint to the Inspector-cum-Facilitator (§72), appeal to the competent authority (§68(2)), writ jurisdiction against public employers. Caveat: the ad-hoc woman cannot insist on renewal of her contract as such — unless non-renewal is because of maternity, which is discriminatory (Manisha Priyadarshini; Air India v. Nergesh Meerza).

May–June 2025 · Q.5Doctrine of notional extension — accident to a workman (problem)
The paper set a fact situation of an accident to a workman away from the actual work-spot (on the way to/from work or on the employer's transport) and asked whether the dependants could claim compensation, applying the doctrine of notional extension.
Plan: §74(1) → Saurashtra Salt's four propositions → Agnes (fleet = premises; “as of right”) → Mackinnon test → Francis De Costa (limit) → ESI §§51C/51E; Law Commission → Daivshala (nexus test; pari materia; retrospective) → §74(4) → apply to the three variables: time (was he going directly to/from duty at the reporting hour?), place (usual route/employer's conveyance?), circumstances (purpose connected with employment; no detour/frolic; mode of transport) → conclusion and relief.

Model answer skeleton (adapt to the facts given).

  1. Rule. Under §74(1) [§3(1) EC Act] liability arises only if the accident arose “out of and in the course of employment”. Ordinarily the course of employment begins when the employee reaches the place of work and ends when he leaves it — “the journey to and from the place of employment being excluded” — but this is subject to the theory of notional extension of the employer's premises “in both time and place” (Saurashtra Salt v. Bai Valu Raja, AIR 1958 SC 881 — four propositions).
  2. Development. (a) Agnes (1964): the extension must be adapted to the nature of the undertaking; for a city bus service the whole fleet is the premises; a facility used “not as a grace but as of right because efficiency of the service demands it” generates an implied obligation; the driver's death on the employer's bus going home was in the course of employment (Raghubar Dayal J. dissenting on the “obligation” test). (b) Mackinnon Mackenzie (1969): nature, conditions, obligations and incidents of employment; zone of special danger; added peril. (c) Parliament codified Agnes in ESI §51C (1968) and, after the restrictive three-judge ruling in Francis De Costa (1996) (cyclist hit 1 km from the factory — not covered), enacted ESI §51E (2010) deeming commuting accidents covered “if nexus between the circumstances, time and place… and the employment is established”. (d) Daivshala v. Oriental Insurance (2025 INSC 904): the EC and ESI Acts are pari materia; §51E is clarificatory and retrospective; a night watchman killed ~5 km from the factory on his way to the 3 a.m. shift was covered — “dutifully proceeding to his workplace to be well on time” established the nexus. (e) The Code now says so expressly for all employees: §74(4) (and §34(3)–(4) for ESI).
  3. Application. Test the facts against the three variables. If the workman was travelling directly between residence and workplace at the appropriate time, by his normal route/means (or in the employer's transport), for the purpose of reporting for or returning from duty → nexus established → claim succeeds (Daivshala; Agnes). If he had deviated for a private purpose (personal court case; shopping; visiting relatives), was travelling at a time unconnected with his duty, or exposed himself to an added peril (crossing at a prohibited place; riding on the footboard against rules) → outside the extension (Saurashtra Salt on facts; Koduri; Kanchanbala). Public transport used as a member of the public is not per se excluded after Daivshala/§74(4), but the nexus must still be shown.
  4. Conclusion and relief. State the result; compute under §76 (death: 50% × wages × factor or notified minimum; PTD: 60%); funeral ≥ Rs 15,000; interest and up to 50% damages for default (§77); deposit and apportionment (§81); limitation two years (§82); competent authority (§§90–93); appeal (§99). If the facts pre-date 21 Nov 2025, apply §3 EC Act as interpreted in Daivshala; the result is the same.
May–June 2025 · Q.8(e)Short note — salient features of the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 (now Chapter V of the Code)
Write a short note on the salient features of the law relating to payment of gratuity.

Answer. Gratuity is a statutory retiral benefit — “a reward for long and meritorious service” — payable by the employer irrespective of profits, now governed by Chapter V (§§53–58) of the Code on Social Security, 2020, which re-enacts the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 (a central law that followed the Kerala and West Bengal Acts). Features: (1) Applicability — factories, mines, oilfields, plantations, ports, railway companies, and shops/establishments with ten or more employees (once covered, always covered — §1(8)); educational institutions covered since 3 Apr 1997. (2) Employee — every category of work including managerial, administrative and teaching (§2(26); BIT v. Jharkhand, 2019); no wage ceiling; Government servants governed by other gratuity rules excluded. (3) Eligibility — termination after five years' continuous service on superannuation, retirement, resignation, death, disablement, expiry of fixed-term employment or a notified event; five years not required for death, disablement or fixed-term expiry; three years for working journalists (§53(1)). (4) Continuous service — uninterrupted service including sickness, leave, lay-off, strike, lock-out; deemed continuity on 240/190 days a year (120/95 for six months); 75% of working days for seasonal establishments (§54; Lalappa Lingappa). (5) Rate — 15 days' wages (basic + DA + retaining allowance, §2(88)) for every completed year or part exceeding six months, at wages last drawn; monthly wages ÷ 26 × 15; seven days per season; three-month average for piece-rated; pro rata for fixed-term and deceased employees (§53(2)). (6) Ceiling — as notified (Rs 20 lakh); better terms saved (§53(3), (5)). (7) Forfeiture — to the extent of loss caused by wilful omission/negligence; wholly/partly for riotous conduct, violence or an offence involving moral turpitude in the course of employment (§53(6); Jaswant Singh Gill; C.G. Ajay Babu; Western Coal Fields 2025). (8) Nomination after one year, in favour of family (§55). (9) Determination — employer must determine and pay within 30 days; simple interest for delay; disputes to the competent authority with deposit of the admitted amount; appeal in 60 days with pre-deposit by the employer (§56). (10) Compulsory insurance or an approved gratuity fund (§57). (11) Protection — non-attachable (§151), overriding effect (§161), penalties (§133). (12) Adjudication by the competent authority (§58) — civil suits excluded.

Nov–Dec 2025 · Q.5Critically examine the doctrine of notional extension with reference to decided cases, including the most recent Supreme Court pronouncement (the question set out the Saurashtra Salt propositions and asked for their application)
“As a rule, the employment of a workman does not commence until he has reached the place of employment and does not continue when he has left the place of employment… subject to the theory of notional extension…” Examine the doctrine in the light of this statement and the case law, and its present statutory position.

Introduction. Section 74(1) of the Code on Social Security, 2020 (§3(1) EC Act, 1923) makes the employer liable for personal injury by accident “arising out of and in the course of employment”. The phrase “in the course of” fixes the temporal and spatial boundaries of the employer's liability. Because a rigid boundary at the factory gate would leave unprotected the many accidents that occur while workers use means of access, employer-provided transport or travel at unusual hours for the employer's business, courts developed the doctrine of notional extension of the employer's premises — later partly codified by Parliament.

The foundational statement — Saurashtra Salt Mfg. Co. v. Bai Valu Raja (AIR 1958 SC 881). Imam J. laid down four propositions: (i) employment does not commence until the workman reaches the place of employment and does not continue after he leaves it, the journey being excluded; (ii) this is subject to the theory of notional extension of the employer's premises to include the area which the workman “passes and repasses” in going to and leaving the actual place of work; (iii) there may be a reasonable extension in both time and place; (iv) each case turns on its facts. On the facts — workmen drowned when a public ferry boat capsized — the Court held the extension did not reach a public conveyance used as members of the public.

Adaptation — B.E.S.T. Undertaking v. Agnes (1964) 3 SCR 930. A bus driver returning home from the depot in another bus of the Undertaking under a staff travel facility was killed. Rejecting the English “contractual obligation” test (Hewitson, Alderman) in favour of the “nexus” approach (Weaver v. Tredegar), Subba Rao J. held that the doctrine “necessarily will have to be adapted to meet [the] peculiar requirements” of a city transport service: the entire fleet is the “premises”; the free travel was “a proved necessity giving rise to an implied obligation”, enjoyed “not as a grace but as of right because efficiency of the service demands it” — so the accident was in the course of employment. Raghubar Dayal J. dissented: the concession created no obligation; once the driver left his own bus he was off duty and under no control. The majority view has prevailed and, as Daivshala notes, received statutory recognition in ESI §51C (1968).

The causal limb — Mackinnon Mackenzie v. Ibrahim (1969) 2 SCC 607. “Arising out of” looks to employment “as such — its nature, its conditions, its obligations and its incidents”; if these bring the workman within the “zone of special danger” the injury arises out of employment, unless he has exposed himself to an “added peril”. Illustrations: Manorama Dassi (police firing near the gate — covered); Mahabir (crossing railway lines to the workmen's train — covered); Indian Rare Earths v. Subaida Beevi (cyclist crossing at the permitted point — covered); Koduri (climbing a tree for a rabbit — added peril).

The restrictive phase — ESIC v. Francis De Costa (1996) 6 SCC 1. A three-judge bench refused to extend the doctrine to a cyclist struck 1 km from the factory before duty: a commuting accident on a public road lacks causal connection; §51C is confined to employer's transport; Sadgunaben (Guj) doubted. The decision exposed a gap that the 62nd Law Commission Report (1974) had already flagged, recommending — in line with ILO Convention No. 121 (1964) — that commuting accidents be covered even without employer-provided transport.

Legislative and judicial completion — ESI §51E (2010) and Daivshala v. Oriental Insurance Co. (2025 INSC 904). §51E deemed commuting accidents to be employment injuries “if nexus between the circumstances, time and place… and the employment is established.” In Daivshala a night watchman killed about 5 km from the sugar factory while going for his 3 a.m. shift was denied compensation by the Bombay High Court on the Francis De Costa logic. The Supreme Court (K.V. Viswanathan J.) reversed: the EC and ESI Acts are in pari materia; §51C was the statutory recognition of Agnes; §51E was enacted to remove doubt and is clarificatory and retrospective; therefore §3 EC Act itself “include[s] accident occurring to an employee while commuting from his residence to the place of employment for duty or from the place of employment to his residence after performing duty, provided the nexus between the circumstances, time and place in which the accident occurred and the employment is established.” The watchman “dutifully proceeding to his workplace to be well on time” satisfied the test. The Code on Social Security, 2020 — in force from 21 Nov 2025 — now enacts the rule for all employees in §74(4) (and §34(3)–(4) for insured persons).

Critical evaluation. (1) The doctrine converts a spatial rule into a relational one — what matters is the connection between the employee's presence at the spot and the employer's business; this is faithful to the no-fault, risk-distribution purpose of the statute and to the constitutional mandate of Arts. 39(e), 41 and 42. (2) The nexus filter answers the dissent's fear of open-ended liability: detours, private errands, unusual timings and added perils remain excluded (Saurashtra Salt on facts; Jain's ticket-checker problem). (3) Daivshala's pari materia reasoning and its treatment of §51E as clarificatory are doctrinally bold but defensible: both statutes address the same mischief, and the Law Commission had recommended parity half a century earlier; the result avoids an arbitrary distinction between insured and uninsured workers doing identical journeys. (4) Remaining issues: the doctrine presupposes an employee — gig and platform workers, defined as outside the employer–employee relationship (§2(35), (61)), fall back on §114 schemes; and the burden of proving nexus still rests on dependants who are often illiterate — the competent authority's power to draw inferences and the State's legal-aid duty under §88(5) mitigate this.

Conclusion. From Saurashtra Salt's cautious “reasonable extension in time and place” to Agnes's fleet-as-premises and Daivshala's nexus test — now written into §74(4) — the doctrine has matured into a coherent, purposive rule: an employee travelling directly to or from duty, at the appropriate time, for the purpose of the employment, is within the course of employment; the employer, as the enterprise that benefits from the journey, bears the risk.

Nov–Dec 2025 · Q.7Maternity benefit — fixed-term contract expiring during the benefit period (Dr. Dolly)
Dr. Dolly's one-year fixed-term contract with a research institute runs from 15 January 2024 to 14 January 2025. She applied for maternity leave commencing 25 December 2024. The institute informed her that only 20 days of maternity benefit can be granted since her tenure ends on 14 January 2025. Advise Dr. Dolly with reference to statutory provisions and leading cases.
Plan: Coverage (establishment; “woman”) → qualifying 80 days → entitlement 26 weeks (12 if two surviving children) → issue: tenure expiry → §68 first proviso [§12(2)(a)] + §161 [§27] → Kavita Yadav ratio (accrual; not coterminous; “discharge” includes contract expiry; legal fiction) → supporting cases → application with dates → relief and forum → caveats.

Issue. Whether a woman employee's maternity benefit, having accrued under §60 of the Code on Social Security, 2020 [§5 MB Act, 1961], can be curtailed because her fixed-term contract expires during the benefit period.

Law. (1) Chapter VI applies to every factory, mine, plantation and to every shop/establishment employing ten or more persons (First Schedule); a research institute so staffed is covered. “Woman” (§2(91)) means any woman employed for wages in an establishment — contractual and fixed-term employees included (MCD v. Female Workers (Muster Roll) (2000) 3 SCC 224). (2) §60(2): the only qualifying condition is 80 days' actual work in the twelve months preceding the expected delivery. (3) §60(3): maximum 26 weeks, not more than eight before the expected delivery (12 weeks if she has two or more surviving children). (4) §68(1) and its first proviso [§12(2)(a)]: discharge or dismissal during pregnancy shall not deprive her of the maternity benefit or medical bonus; §161 [§27]: the Code overrides any inconsistent contract of service. (5) §2(34)(b): a fixed-term employee is eligible for all statutory benefits available to a permanent employee, proportionately, even without the qualifying period. (6) Dr. Kavita Yadav v. Secretary, MoHFW (2024) 1 SCC 421: a contractual Senior Resident whose tenure ended on 11 June 2017 was given only eleven days' benefit; the Supreme Court held that the benefit “accrues on fulfilment of the conditions specified in Section 5(2)… and such benefits can travel beyond the term of employment also. It is not coterminous with the employment tenure”; “discharge” includes “discharge on conclusion of the contractual period”; enforcing the tenure clause within the benefit period would be a prohibited discharge; “the law creates a fiction… by treating her to be in employment for the sole purpose of availing maternity benefits.” The employer was directed to pay the full benefit under §§5 and 8 within three months. Also Deepika Singh v. CAT (2022) 7 SCC 1 (purposive reading; Arts. 14, 15, 39, 42, 43); Manisha Priyadarshini (Del HC 2019) (ad-hoc teacher).

Application. Dr. Dolly worked from 15 January 2024 — far more than 80 days in the twelve months before her expected delivery; the entitlement to 26 weeks (or 12 weeks if she already has two surviving children) therefore accrued when she gave notice for leave from 25 December 2024 (a date within eight weeks of expected delivery). The institute's stand — 20 days only, up to 14 January 2025 — is precisely the “coterminous” argument rejected in Kavita Yadav; her situation (one-year fixed term in a research institute) is indistinguishable from the appellant's three-year residency there. By the statutory fiction she is deemed in employment till the end of the benefit period (26 weeks from 25 December 2024, i.e., up to about 25 June 2025) for the sole purpose of the benefit; the automatic expiry of her contract on 14 January 2025 is a “discharge” that cannot reduce the benefit (§68 proviso), and the tenure clause yields to §161. She is also entitled to the medical bonus of Rs 3,500 if no free pre-/post-natal care was provided (§64), to leave for pregnancy-related illness up to one month (§65(3)) and, had she continued in service, to nursing breaks and crèche access (§§66–67).

Advice. (i) Serve a written notice under §62 (if not already done) specifying the date of absence and nominee; (ii) demand payment in advance of the pre-natal portion and the balance within 48 hours of proof of delivery (§62(5)); (iii) on refusal, complain to the Inspector-cum-Facilitator under §72 (who may direct payment) and/or appeal against the deprivation order to the competent authority within 60 days (§68(2)); for a Government/autonomous institute a writ petition (Arts. 14, 21, 42) also lies — the route taken in Kavita Yadav; (iv) non-payment is an offence under §133. (v) Caveat: Kavita Yadav does not entitle her to renewal of the contract or to salary for work beyond 14 January 2025 for other purposes; if the institute refuses renewal because of her maternity, that is separately challengeable as discriminatory (Manisha Priyadarshini; Nergesh Meerza).

Conclusion. Dr. Dolly is entitled to the full maternity benefit (26/12 weeks) at her average daily wage notwithstanding the expiry of her contract on 14 January 2025; the institute's 20-day limitation is legally unsustainable.

Nov–Dec 2025 · Q.8(b)Short note — applicability of the Payment of Gratuity Act to teachers
Are teachers entitled to gratuity? Trace the legal position.

Answer. Yes — retrospectively from 3 April 1997. Step 1 — coverage of institutions: by S.O. 1080 dated 3 April 1997 the Central Government extended the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 to educational institutions employing ten or more persons (§1(3)(c)). Step 2 — the setback: in Ahmedabad Pvt. Primary Teachers Association v. Administrative Officer (2004) 1 SCC 755 the Supreme Court held that teachers were not “employees” under §2(e) as it then stood (“skilled, semi-skilled or unskilled, manual, supervisory, technical or clerical work”): applying noscitur a sociis, imparting education was none of these; had Parliament intended universal coverage it would have used the EPF Act's words “any kind of work, manual or otherwise”; the Court left it to the legislature. Step 3 — the legislative response: the Payment of Gratuity (Amendment) Act, 2009 (Act 47 of 2009, published 31 Dec 2009), whose Statement of Objects and Reasons expressly refers to the 2004 decision, substituted §2(e) to cover any person employed for wages “in any kind of work, manual or otherwise”, with retrospective effect from 3 April 1997. Step 4 — judicial confirmation: in Birla Institute of Technology v. State of Jharkhand (AIR 2019 SC 1309) the Supreme Court, having first allowed the institute's appeal on 7 Jan 2019 in ignorance of the amendment, recalled that order suo motu and, on 7 March 2019, dismissed the appeal: the 2004 decision “has lost its binding effect”; an Assistant Professor who served from 1971 to 2001 was entitled to gratuity of Rs 3,38,796 with 10% interest. Step 5 — the Code: §2(26) defines “employee” to include “any other work”; Chapter V applies to shops/establishments with ten or more employees and notified establishments; Explanation 1 to §53 excludes only Government post-holders governed by other gratuity rules. Hence teachers in private schools, colleges and universities are entitled to gratuity at 15 days' wages per completed year after five years' continuous service (no five-year requirement on death/disablement/fixed-term expiry), subject to the Rs 20 lakh ceiling.

J.3 Older DU LL.B. questions (2000–2008, from Jain's DU-oriented text) — concise model answers

Short note: contract of service v. contract for service [LC-I 2008]

Compensation is payable only to an “employee” — a person employed under a contract of service. Distinguish from an independent contractor under a contract for service using (i) the control test — control over the manner of work (Dharangadhara Chemical Works AIR 1957 SC 264), (ii) the integration test — is the work an integral part of the business (Silver Jubilee Tailoring House (1974) 3 SCC 498), (iii) the economic-reality/multiple-factor test — who owns the tools, bears risk, pays wages, can dismiss, “economic control” (Hussainbhai v. Alath Factory (1978) 4 SCC 257; Ram Singh v. UT Chandigarh (2004) 1 SCC 126). Under the Code, §2(26) covers persons employed on wages “either directly or through a contractor… whether the terms of employment be express or implied”; casual and part-period employment suffices (Royal Western India Turf Club (2016) 4 SCC 521); the principal employer answers for a contractor's employees under §85. Examples: a plumber engaged for a job — contract for service; race-day ticket clerks — contract of service.

Short note: total and partial disablement; distinction [CLC 2001, 2002, 2004, 2005]

Define §2(56) PTD (incapacity for all work he was capable of performing; Part I injuries; Part II combinations = 100%), §2(55) PPD (reduction of earning capacity in every employment he could undertake; Part II injuries deemed), §2(83) temporary disablement (temporary incapacity for the work he was doing). Points of distinction: (a) degree — total = incapacity for all work; partial = reduction of earning capacity; (b) duration — permanent v. temporary; (c) measure — 60% × wages × factor v. percentage thereof v. 25% half-monthly; (d) test — loss of earning capacity, not of earnings (Balasaheb); scheduled percentages are minima; a carpenter losing a hand is totally disabled though the Schedule says 60% (Pratap Narain Singh Deo (1976) 1 SCC 289); driver losing a leg — 100% (K. Janardhan); unscheduled injuries assessed by a medical practitioner (Golla Rajanna). Add the 2×2 matrix and one example each (Part D).

Essay: when does the employer become liable to pay compensation? Conditions for a claim, with cases [CLC 2002, 2008; LC-I 2002, 2004, 2005; LC-II 2000]

Structure: (1) Employee under Chapter VII (Second Schedule) in an establishment outside ESI; (2) personal injury — external or internal (heart cases); (3) by accident (Fenton v. Thorley) or a scheduled occupational disease (§74(3)); (4) arising out of employment — causal tests (Mackinnon Mackenzie; Lord Sumner; street/locality risks — Manorama Dassi, Salamabegum; natural perils — Ram Prasad; added peril — Koduri; disease without strain — Jyothi Ademma); (5) in the course of employment — time/place/incidental acts; notional extension (Saurashtra Salt, Agnes, Mahabir, Daivshala; §74(4)); (6) result — death or disablement exceeding three days; (7) no defence under §74(1)(b) (drink/drugs; wilful breach of express safety order; removal of safety guard — never in death/PTD cases; Moondra; Padam Debi), and no election of civil remedy (§74(7)); (8) procedure — notice and two-year limitation (§82), deposit (§81), competent authority (§§90–93). Conclude with quantum (§76) and the no-fault character.

Essay: theory of notional extension with cases [LC-I 2001, 2003]

Use the answer to Nov–Dec 2025 Q.5 (J.2.5), adding the older illustrations that Jain highlights: Mahabir (AIR 1954 All 132 — crossing railway lines to the workmen's train, “environmental accidents”), Manorama Dassi (AIR 1953 Cal 143), Dedhiben (AIR 1976 SC 1250), Subaida Beevi, Kanchanbala (circuitous route — no), J.F. Pareira v. Eastern Watch Co. (Bom 1985), and the English roots (Hewitson, Weaver).

Problem: Peon ‘A’ (office work + outside mail delivery) killed while commuting to office — widow's claim [CLC 2001]

Old analysis (Jain): claim succeeds — A's duties took him on to the streets, so street risks were incidents of his employment and the notional extension covered his journey to the office at the reporting hour. Current analysis: even without relying on the outdoor nature of his duties, §74(4)/Daivshala covers a direct commute at the appropriate time and place; the peon “dutifully proceeding to his workplace” establishes the nexus. Check that a peon is a Chapter VII employee (office attached to a factory/establishment in the Second Schedule or a State extension saved by item (l); otherwise ESI). Relief: 50% × wages × factor (or notified minimum) + funeral; deposit with the competent authority.

Problem: Driver ‘A’, given a rent-free house by the Manager for domestic work, killed while taking the Manager's children to school in the company vehicle [LC-II 2004]

The employer's liability extends only to accidents connected with the employer's trade or business. Driving the Manager's children to school was a private service to the Manager under a separate arrangement (rent-free house), not the company's work; it was neither the driver's duty nor incidental to it; §74(2) (acts in breach of orders) helps only where the act is “for the purpose of and in connection with the employer's trade or business”. No notional extension applies (Saurashtra Salt proposition (i); contrast Agnes where the facility served the efficiency of the service). Claim against the company fails; the dependants may pursue the Manager as employer of a domestic driver (if covered by a State extension of the Schedule) or a tort/MV Act claim against the vehicle's owner/insurer. If, however, the company had knowledge of and sanctioned the practice as a perquisite of the Manager's service, the act could be treated as within the employer's business — argue in the alternative.

Problem: Fisherman ‘A’, Great Nicobar, swept away by the tsunami ten metres before the work-spot after reporting for muster — daughter's claim [CLC 2005]

Sea-fishing is a Second Schedule employment (item (xxxiv)). A had reported for muster — employment had commenced; he was within the notionally extended workplace, ten metres from the boat on the shore where the employment required him to be at that hour (Dedhiben; Mahabir). The tsunami is vis major, but that is no defence per se: the question is whether the employment exposed him to the natural peril more than the public (Ram Prasad — lightning; Thom v. Sinclair); a fisherman on the shore at dawn for his employer's work plainly was. The accident arose out of and in the course of employment. The daughter is a dependant under §2(24)(a) if unmarried (legitimate/adopted) — no proof of dependence needed; if married and a minor/widowed and a minor, under (c) on proof of partial dependence. Compensation: 50% × wages × factor (or notified minimum); funeral; deposit and apportionment (§81). Note that the employer could not plead the §74(1)(b) defences in a death case.

Problem on the facts of Agnes [CLC 2002]

Reproduce the case card (Part I.1): identify the issue (course of employment on the return journey in the employer's bus), state Saurashtra Salt's propositions, apply Agnes's majority reasoning (fleet = premises; facility as of right; implied obligation), note the dissent, add Daivshala/§74(4) as confirming the result today (even a journey on public transport would qualify on the nexus test), and conclude that the widow succeeds; quantum under §76; if the undertaking were ESI-covered, dependants' benefit with §34(4)'s deeming for employer's transport.

Problem: Ticket-checker ‘A’ with free State Roadways travel leaves office to attend his personal court case and is killed in a Roadways bus accident [LC 2003]

Distinguish Agnes: there the driver was travelling home from duty, using a facility provided for the efficiency of the service. Here A was travelling during duty hours for a purpose wholly personal (his own litigation) — a “frolic of his own”; the free-travel facility does not convert every journey into the course of employment; the nexus of circumstances with the employment is absent even if time and place coincide (§74(4); Daivshala). No compensation under Chapter VII; the dependants' remedy is against the Roadways as carrier/insurer under the Motor Vehicles Act. (If A had been travelling on the bus to check tickets, the accident would clearly arise out of and in the course of employment.)

Problem: employee attending the employer's Silver Jubilee function killed while crossing the public road outside the gate to board a bus [CLC 2006]

Two questions: (i) was attendance at the function “employment”? If attendance was required or expected as part of service (roll call, wages paid, official function on the premises), the function is within the course of employment; a purely voluntary social event is not. (ii) Even so, the accident occurred on the public road outside the gate — beyond the premises and their means of access. Under the older law (Saurashtra Salt; Francis De Costa) the claim would fail unless the employer's arranged transport was involved (Agnes) or the crossing was the only means of egress (Subaida Beevi; Mahabir). Under §74(4)/Daivshala, a journey home directly after an obligatory function at the appropriate time on the normal route has the necessary nexus and would be covered; a voluntary attendee has no such nexus. Jain's hint (“added peril”) applies if he crossed negligently at a dangerous, prohibited point — though negligence as such is no defence, an added peril takes the accident outside the employment risk (Koduri; contrast Subaida Beevi).

Predicted problem — watchman injured 3 km from the factory on the way to the night shift (Cornell)

Apply Daivshala squarely (identical category — Second Schedule item (xxxiii)); §74(4) if after 21 Nov 2025; nexus of time (night shift), place (direct route), circumstances (going to duty). Insurer's Francis De Costa plea fails. Relief under §76 (PTD/PPD as the case may be) + §77 interest.

Predicted essay — significance of ESI §51C (now §34(4)) for interpreting EC §3/§74

Codified Agnes; shows Parliament's acceptance of the majority view; proves the cognate nature of the two Acts (pari materia) → foundation of Daivshala's reading of §51E into the EC Act; contrast: §51C needs employer-operated, non-public transport and permission; §74(4) needs only nexus; neither requires contractual obligation.

Predicted — Agnes majority v. dissent; which is persuasive?

Use Part I.1 “Exam angles”: majority = purposive adaptation to the undertaking; dissent = obligation/control test, later abandoned by Parliament itself (§51C) and the Supreme Court (Daivshala); the modern nexus filter meets the dissent's concern about limits.

Predicted — would notional extension extend to gig/platform workers under the Code?

No “employee” → no §74 liability; §2(35)/(61) define them outside the employer–employee relationship; protection only via §114 schemes (accident insurance, life/disability cover) funded by aggregator contributions (1–2% of turnover, capped at 5% of payouts) and the Social Security Fund (§141); State laws (Rajasthan Platform Based Gig Workers (Registration and Welfare) Act, 2023; Karnataka's 2025 law) add welfare boards/cess; critique: rights v. schemes; the Turf Club “perennial activity” logic and the multiple-factor test could support reclassification of some platform workers as employees; compare the UK Supreme Court's Uber BV v. K.3 Real-life illustrations (how the provisions bite)

1 · Sugar-factory watchman (Daivshala facts, Osmanabad, 2003)

Second Schedule item (xxxiii) → Chapter VII employee; 3 a.m. shift → commute at an hour dictated by employment; accident 5 km away → nexus of time, place, circumstances → employer/insurer liable (§74(4) today). Had the factory been ESI-covered, ESIC would pay dependants' benefit (90% of wages as pension) under §34(3).

2 · Mundka building fire, Delhi (13 May 2022, 27 dead)

Occupier's §73 report within seven days (if outside ESI); §88 notices; death compensation at 50% × wages (≤ Rs 15,000) × age factor — for a 25-year-old on Rs 12,000: 6,000 × 216.91 = Rs 13,01,460 + funeral; deposit with the competent authority (§81); ex-gratia from Governments is in addition. Absence of fire-safety compliance is irrelevant to the no-fault liability but relevant to prosecution under the OSH Code/BNS.

3 · Silkyara–Barkot tunnel collapse, Uttarkashi (Nov 2023; 41 workers rescued after 17 days)

Tunnel construction is Second Schedule item (viii)(c)/(x); workers were largely inter-State migrants (§2(41)). Any disablement (physical or psychological — e.g., PTSD, if medically established as an injury by the accident) → §76; temporary disablement → half-monthly payments; medical reimbursement §76(5); BOCW welfare board assistance in addition (Chapter VIII).

4 · Heat-stroke deaths of outdoor workers (summer heat-waves)

“Diseases due to effect of heat in extreme hot climate” is a Third Schedule Part B disease for “all work involving exposure to the risk” — with six months' employment, deemed injury by accident (§74(3)); otherwise argue an internal accident caused by work exposure (Ram Prasad logic for natural perils). State heat-action plans and ESI benefits complement Chapter VII.

5 · Sewer/septic-tank deaths

Item (xxxviii) makes sewer cleaners Chapter VII employees even when engaged casually through contractors (§85 principal-employer liability — often a municipality). Compensation under §76 coexists with the Supreme Court's directions (Rs 30 lakh per death) and the 2013 Act's rehabilitation duties.

6 · Food-delivery rider injured in a road crash

If engaged “outside traditional employer-employee relationship” (§2(35)/(61)), no §74 claim against the aggregator; remedies: aggregator's insurance under the §114 scheme, Motor Vehicles Act claim against the tortfeasor, PMSBY. If facts show control, integration and economic dependence on one platform (cf. Turf Club's “perennial activity”; Hussainbhai), reclassification as an employee (and Second Schedule item (l)/State notification for motor-vehicle drivers) could open Chapter VII.

7 · Contractual Senior Resident / ad-hoc lecturer expecting a child near contract expiry

Kavita Yadav + §68 + §161: full 26 weeks regardless of tenure; §2(34)(b) for fixed-term staff; non-renewal because of maternity is discriminatory (Manisha Priyadarshini). A hospital in an ESI area with wages ≤ Rs 21,000 → ESIC pays; otherwise the employer.

8 · Private-school teacher retiring after 30 years

Gratuity = last drawn (basic + DA) × 15/26 × 30, capped at Rs 20 lakh; payable within 30 days with interest thereafter (§56); teacher's status settled by BIT v. Jharkhand; disputes to the competent authority (§58), not civil courts.
