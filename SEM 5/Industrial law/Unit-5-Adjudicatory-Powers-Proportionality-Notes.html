0Orientation — What This Unit Is Really About

The one-sentence answer

Unit 4 established how an employer may punish (the managerial prerogative and the domestic enquiry); Unit 5 asks what happens once the dispute reaches an adjudicator — how far may a Labour Court, Industrial Tribunal or National Industrial Tribunal go in (i) re-deciding whether the misconduct itself was proved, and (ii) re-writing the punishment the employer chose. The answer is: since 15 December 1971 the adjudicator has both powers (§11-A, IDA; now §50, IRC 2020), but the second power is deliberately fenced — it may be used only where the punishment is grossly disproportionate / shocks the conscience. That fence is called the doctrine of proportionality.

0.1 The syllabus heading decoded

Words in the syllabusWhat they mean in law
“Powers of the Adjudicatory Authorities”The powers of the Labour Court, the Industrial Tribunal and the National Industrial Tribunal when an industrial dispute relating to discharge, dismissal or termination of an individual workman is referred to them under §10 (the reference being competent because of the deeming provision in §2-A).
“in cases of Discharge/Dismissal”The section applies only to termination disputes — not to transfers, suspensions, non-promotions, or other service grievances (those travel under other heads, e.g., Schedule II item 1, or are dealt with under §33/IRC §90). A “discharge” is a termination simpliciter; a “dismissal” is a punitive termination for misconduct; both are governed.
“Application of Doctrine of Proportionality in awarding Punishments”The adjudicator’s power to substitute a lesser punishment — “the punishment must fit the misconduct”. Its outer limit, as settled in Hombe Gowda (2006), is that the Tribunal may interfere with quantum only where the punishment is “grossly disproportionate” and “no reasonable person could inflict such punishment”.
“(section 50; 56)”The provisions of the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 that replace, respectively, §11-A IDA (substantive remedial power over discharge/dismissal — §50, with a new sub-section (2) conferring interim relief) and §17-B IDA (payment of full wages last drawn to a workman who has won reinstatement, while the employer’s challenge is pending in the High Court/Supreme Court — §56). The syllabus deliberately asks you to know both the old and the new statute.

The four questions every Unit-5 answer must be able to answer
  1. What exactly did §11-A change? — two new powers (reappraisal of the finding; modification of the punishment) and nothing else.
  2. What did it not change? — the employer’s right to lead evidence for the first time where the enquiry was absent/defective; the rule that a domestic enquiry is expected but not a statutory precondition to a valid dismissal.
  3. How far may the Tribunal go in reducing the punishment? — only to the extent the punishment is grossly disproportionate / shocks the conscience; the power is “limited”, not plenary (Hombe Gowda).
  4. What is the position under the IRC, 2020 and the Constitution? — §50 (with interim relief under §50(2)) and §56 replace §11-A and §17-B; and proportionality is now also an Article 14/21 standard (Om Kumar; Raghubir Singh; Olga Tellis).

AThe Statutory Framework — §11-A, IDA 1947 (now §50, IRC 2020)

A.1 The pre-1971 position — why Parliament had to intervene

The regime before §11-A: Indian Iron & Steel Co. Ltd. v. Their Workmen, AIR 1958 SC 130

Where a proper domestic enquiry had been held and the employer’s conclusion was plausible on the evidence, the Tribunal had no jurisdiction to substitute its own judgment. It could interfere with the dismissal only on four narrow grounds — (i) want of good faith; (ii) victimisation or unfair labour practice; (iii) basic error or violation of the principles of natural justice; and (iv) a finding perverse or based on no evidence. And — the point that Unit 5 is built on — even where the misconduct was proved, the Tribunal had no power to touch the punishment. Its only choice was to uphold the dismissal entirely or set it aside entirely (which meant full reinstatement). The employer’s power to choose the punishment was treated as an aspect of “managerial functions”.

The result was binary justice: a Tribunal that thought dismissal was too harsh had no middle course available to it. It either confirmed a crushing punishment or awarded the workman the windfall of full reinstatement with back wages.

The International Labour Organisation trigger

ILO Recommendation No. 119 concerning termination of employment at the initiative of the employer, adopted in June 1963, recommended that a worker aggrieved by termination “should be entitled to appeal against the termination, among others, to a neutral body such as an arbitrator, a court, an arbitration committee or a similar body”, and that the neutral body “should be empowered to examine the reasons given in the termination of employment and the other circumstances relating to the case and to render a decision on the justification of the termination”. It further recommended that the neutral body be empowered, if it found the termination unjustified, to order that the worker “unless reinstated with unpaid wages, should be paid adequate compensation or afforded some other relief”. (Quoted in Firestone, para 3, and reproduced in Raghubir Singh para 34.)

The Statement of Objects and Reasons (1971)

“In accordance with these recommendations, it is considered that the Tribunal’s power in an adjudication proceeding relating to discharge or dismissal of a workman should not be limited and that the Tribunal should have the power, in cases wherever necessary, to set aside the order of discharge or dismissal and direct reinstatement of the workman on such terms and conditions, if any, as it thinks fit or give such other relief to the workman including the award of any lesser punishment in lieu of discharge or dismissal as the circumstances of the case may require. For this purpose, a new section 11-A is proposed to be inserted in the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947….”

The Statement expressly refers to the limitations on the powers of an Industrial Tribunal laid down in Indian Iron & Steel Co. — legislative history that Firestone used to identify the mischief being cured.

A.2 The section — text, and every element explained

§11-A, Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (inserted by Act 45 of 1971, w.e.f. 15 December 1971)

“Powers of Labour Courts, Tribunals and National Tribunals to give appropriate relief in case of discharge or dismissal of workmen.—Where an industrial dispute relating to the discharge or dismissal of a workman has been referred to a Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal for adjudication and, in the course of the adjudication proceedings, the Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal, as the case may be, is satisfied that the order of discharge or dismissal was not justified, it may, by its award, set aside the order of discharge or dismissal and direct reinstatement of the workman on such terms and conditions, if any, as it thinks fit, or give such other relief to the workman including the award of any lesser punishment in lieu of discharge or dismissal as the circumstances of the case may require:

Provided that in any proceeding under this section the Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal, as the case may be, shall rely only on the materials on record and shall not take any fresh evidence in relation to the matter.”

Element-by-element breakdown — write these headings in the exam
Words of the sectionMeaning, authority and exam use
“Where an industrial dispute relating to the discharge or dismissal of a workman…”The section is confined to termination disputes — discharge, dismissal (and, by the deeming provision of §2-A, retrenchment/termination). It does not apply to transfer, suspension, denial of promotion, or other disciplinary action short of termination. An individual workman’s dismissal is an “industrial dispute” because of §2-A.
“…has been referred to a Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal for adjudication”The power is exercisable only in adjudication upon a reference (under §10, or on a §33-A complaint, or under a §2-A deeming). It is not a free-standing jurisdiction; there must be a reference pending. Under the IRC, 2020 the trigger is §53(6) — the worker’s direct application — or a reference to the National Industrial Tribunal.
“…is satisfied that the order of discharge or dismissal was not justified”This is the key phrase. The test is no longer “was the employer’s conclusion perverse?” but “is the Tribunal satisfied that the order was justified?” — the Tribunal’s own satisfaction, on the record, decides. Firestone: “what was once largely in the realm of the satisfaction of the employer, has ceased to be so; and now it is the satisfaction of the Tribunal that finally decides the matter.”
“…may, by its award, set aside the order … and direct reinstatement … on such terms and conditions … as it thinks fit”Reinstatement remains the primary relief, now expressly on terms and conditions — which is the source of the Tribunal’s power to award reinstatement with full, partial or no back wages (Scooters India: reinstatement with 75% back wages).
“…or give such other relief … including the award of any lesser punishment in lieu of discharge or dismissal…”The radical new power. The Tribunal may accept the misconduct and yet substitute (say) censure, withholding of increments, suspension without pay for a period, or a fine, for dismissal. This power did not exist at all before 1971 (pre-1971, once misconduct was validly proved the Tribunal had no power over quantum).
“…as the circumstances of the case may require”Discretion, but a judicial discretion — it must be reasoned and considered, not sentimental or compassionate (Scooters India upheld; Hombe Gowda struck down an unreasoned/extreme reduction; Bharat Forge: “such an order cannot be passed on an irrational or extraneous factor and certainly not on a compassionate ground”).
Proviso — “shall rely only on the materials on record and shall not take any fresh evidence”Confines the Tribunal to the composite record properly before it and prevents it from behaving like an ordinary appellate court calling for evidence of its own. It does not bar the employer’s pre-existing right to lead evidence for the first time where no enquiry/defective enquiry — see A.5 below.

A.4 The Proviso — what “materials on record” really means

The workmen in Firestone argued that “materials on record” could only mean the material produced at the domestic enquiry, and that “fresh evidence” was exactly the employer’s first-time evidence — so that the proviso abolished the employer’s right to lead evidence before the Tribunal. The Supreme Court rejected this reading. The expression, held the Court, refers to the material before the Tribunal, and comprises:

The three categories (verbatim from Firestone, para 49)
  1. “the evidence taken by the management at the enquiry and the proceedings of the enquiry; or
  2. the above evidence and, in addition, any further evidence led before the Tribunal; or
  3. evidence placed before the Tribunal for the first time in support of the action taken by an employer as well as the evidence adduced by the workmen contra.”

“We are not inclined to limit that expression as meaning only that material that has been placed in a domestic enquiry. The Proviso only confines the Tribunal to the materials on record before it as specified above… It also prohibits the Tribunal from taking any fresh evidence either for satisfying itself regarding the misconduct or for altering the punishment… From the Proviso it is not certainly possible to come to the conclusion that when once it is held that an enquiry has not been held or is found to be defective, an order reinstating the workman will have to be made by the Tribunal. Nor does it follow that the Proviso deprives an employer of his right to adduce evidence for the first time before the Tribunal.”

Why the proviso exists (para 50 of the judgment)

Because “such wide powers” had been conferred on Tribunals — to differ on the finding and to alter the punishment — the legislature thought it necessary to impose a restriction on what materials could be taken into account. The Tribunal “cannot call for further or fresh evidence, as an appellate authority may normally do under a particular statute”. The balance: wide power, but on a closed record.

A.5 The preserved right — the employer’s “first-time evidence”

The rule

Where no enquiry was held, or the enquiry held is found defective, the employer may still justify the punishment by leading all relevant evidence before the Tribunal for the first time; the workman is entitled to lead evidence contra; and the Tribunal decides for itself whether the misconduct is proved. A defective enquiry stands on the same footing as no enquiry (Motipur Sugar Factory).

The two reasons given in Firestone

  1. No express or implied abrogation: “If the intention of the legislature was to do away with such a right, which has been recognised over a long period of years … the section would have been differently worded.”
  2. To avoid “incongruous” and “startling” results: a Tribunal acting under §33 may itself permit an employer to lead evidence for the first time even where no domestic enquiry was held and then grant permission/approval. If the Tribunal adjudicating the main dispute were then bound to order reinstatement merely because no enquiry had been held, the Act would contradict itself.
The small-employer illustration (para 52) — a favourite exam point

“There may be other instances where an employer with a limited number of workmen may himself be a witness to a misconduct committed by a workman. He will be disabled from conducting an enquiry against the workman because he cannot both be an enquiry officer and also a witness in the proceedings. Any enquiry held by him will not be in keeping with the principles of natural justice. But he will certainly be entitled to take disciplinary action for which purpose he can serve a charge-sheet and, after calling for explanation, impose the necessary punishment without holding any enquiry. This will be a case where no enquiry at all has been held by an employer. But the employer will have sufficient material available with him which could be produced before any Tribunal to satisfy it about the justification for the action taken.” (Cf. Unit 4 — Associated Cement: the eyewitness officer should not hold the enquiry.)

A.6 Retrospectivity — Firestone’s second question

Argument on the pointHow the Court dealt with it
Workmen’s case: §11-A is procedural — it is placed in Chapter IV (“Procedure, Powers and Duties of Authorities”), and procedural laws apply to pending proceedings; and the words “has been referred” (present perfect) are wide enough to cover references already made. Hence it applies to all disputes pending on 15.12.1971.The words “has been referred” are “no doubt capable of being interpreted” that way — but they “cannot be isolated from the context”. Two decisive indications go the other way: (i) the proviso speaks of “in any proceeding under this section” — and there cannot be a proceeding under a section before the section itself came into force; and (ii) the section was brought into force by a notification on 14.12.1971, after the Amendment Act received assent (8.12.1971).
Employers’ case: retrospective operation is not to be given to a statute so as to impair an existing right unless the intention appears clearly or by necessary intendment; §11-A abridges the employer’s managerial rights recognised in Indian Iron and the long line of cases.Accepted. The Court held that §11-A “has the effect of altering the law by abridging the rights of the employer inasmuch as it gives power to the Tribunal for the first time to differ both on a finding of misconduct arrived at by an employer as well as the punishment imposed by him”; for such a provision to reach pre-existing references there must be a “clear, express and manifest indication”, which is absent.
The holding — and how to state it

“Section 11-A has no application to disputes referred prior to December 15, 1971. The section applies only to disputes which are referred for adjudication on or after December 15, 1971. Such disputes have to be dealt with according to the decisions of this Court already referred to.” Consequently Civil Appeal No. 1461 of 1972 (where the Tribunal had held §11-A inapplicable) was dismissed; and the three appeals in which the Bombay Labour Court had applied §11-A to pre-1971 references were allowed to that extent and the matters remanded to be adjudicated “in accordance with the views expressed in this judgment”.

Two precision guards
  • The date is 15 December 1971. Some judgments/notes (including a stray reference in Raghubir Singh) print “15.12.1972” — that is a typographical slip; the notification under §1(2) brought the Amendment Act into force on 15 December 1971, and Firestone itself uses 1971 throughout.
  • A “dismissal order passed before 15.12.1971” does not matter; what matters is the date of the reference. A faint argument to the contrary was rightly not pursued even by the employers’ counsel.
  • Today the point is largely academic (no live reference can pre-date 1971), but it remains the classic illustration of the presumption against retrospectivity for provisions that abridge substantive rights — a favourite “short note”.

A.7 Does §11-A make a domestic enquiry compulsory? — No, and here is why

The argument rejected

It was argued that because standing orders certified under the IESO Act, 1946 are “statutory terms and conditions of service”, an order of dismissal passed without the enquiry that the standing orders require is illegal; an illegal order can have no effect; and therefore the Tribunal must order reinstatement without anything more, the employer’s “second chance” of leading fresh evidence having been taken away by the legislature.

The Court’s reasons for rejecting it

  1. “There is no provision either in this statute or in the Act which states that an order of dismissal or discharge is illegal if it is not preceded by a proper and valid domestic enquiry.”
  2. The IESO Act applies only to establishments covered by §1(3), while the IDA “applies to employers who may have no standing orders at all”; the argument would make the Act operate differently on the two classes — “certainly not the scheme of the Act”.
  3. The very contention had been rejected in Motipur Sugar Factory.
  4. Accepting it would produce “incongruous results” with §33 (see A.5 above).
But — and this is the sentence examiners look for (para 53)

“We should not be understood as laying down that there is no obligation whatsoever on the part of an employer to hold an enquiry before passing an order of discharge or dismissal. This Court has consistently been holding that an employer is expected to hold a proper enquiry according to the Standing Orders and principles of natural justice. It has also been emphasised that such an enquiry should not be an empty formality. If a proper enquiry is conducted by an employer and a correct finding arrived at regarding the misconduct, the Tribunal, even though it has now power to differ from the conclusions arrived at by the management, will have to give very cogent reasons for not accepting the view of the employer.” And by holding a proper enquiry the employer “will also escape the charge of having acted arbitrarily or mala fide”; a proper enquiry “will conduce to harmonious and healthy relationship between him and the workmen and … serve the cause of industrial peace”.

Exam take-away: the enquiry is not a condition of validity but it is the employer’s best insurance — it disciplines the record, secures deference, and converts the Tribunal’s power to differ into a power that must be exercised with “very cogent reasons”.

A.8 §11-A and §33 — deliberately kept distinct (para 51)

§33 (Unit 6 / IRC §90)

An application for permission (§33(1)/(3)) or approval (§33(2)) “has still to be dealt with according to the principles laid down by this Court in its various decisions. No change has been effected in that section by the Amendment Act.” Even a dismissal passed with permission or approval “can form the subject of a dispute and as such [be] referred for adjudication”; the §33 proceedings then “form part of the materials on record before the Tribunal”.

The consequence — and the “startling results” warning

Because a §33 Tribunal may allow an employer to lead evidence for the first time even where no domestic enquiry was held, a main-dispute Tribunal that straightaway ordered reinstatement for want of an enquiry “would lead to very startling results”. Hence “an attempt must be made to construe Section 11-A in a reasonable manner” — which is a further reason for holding that the right to adduce first-time evidence survives.

⑯ The Workmen of M/s Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. of India P. Ltd. v. The Management CASE No. 16 · Unit 5

AIR 1973 SC 1227 · (1973) 1 SCC 813 · (1973) 3 SCR 587 · Four connected Civil Appeals by special leave (Nos. 1461, 1995, 1996 and 2386 of 1972) · Bench: C.A. Vaidialingam, J. · The definitive interpretation of §11-A
Context — why this is the case of the unit

Section 11-A was inserted in December 1971. Within eighteen months the Supreme Court had to decide what it meant. Firestone answers two questions: (i) the proper interpretation of §11-A — whether it enlarged the Tribunal’s power over the finding of misconduct, over the punishment, and whether it abrogated the employer’s pre-existing right to lead evidence for the first time; and (ii) whether §11-A applies to disputes that had already been referred before 15 December 1971. Everything in this unit, and much of Unit 4, is downstream of this judgment.

Facts
  1. Four connected appeals by special leave raised common questions. In all four, the reference relating to the dismissal/discharge had been made long before §11-A came into force, and the industrial disputes were pending adjudication on 15 December 1971.
  2. Different Tribunals/Labour Courts in Maharashtra had taken divergent views — some holding that §11-A applied retrospectively to all pending references (the section being “merely procedural”), and some holding that it did not (the section abridging substantive managerial rights). They also differed on the scope of the new power: was the Tribunal confined to revising the punishment after accepting the finding of misconduct, or could it reappraise the finding itself? And did the employer’s right to lead fresh evidence survive?
  3. The cases thus came to the Supreme Court as a test of the whole new provision — the Court itself described the exercise as an exhaustive review of the pre-1971 case law (Indian Iron & Steel; Punjab National Bank; Bharat Sugar Mills; Ritz Theatre; Khardah Co.; Motipur Sugar Factory; State Bank of India v. R.K. Jain; DCM v. Ludh Budh Singh).
Issues
  1. What is the true scope and effect of §11-A? In particular — (a) can the Tribunal reappraise and differ from the finding of misconduct recorded in a proper domestic enquiry; (b) can it interfere with the punishment and award a lesser punishment; (c) does §11-A abolish the employer’s right to adduce evidence for the first time before the Tribunal where no enquiry was held or the enquiry is defective; and (d) does §11-A make the holding of a domestic enquiry mandatory, so that its absence renders the dismissal illegal and compels reinstatement?
  2. Does §11-A operate retrospectively so as to apply to industrial disputes already referred and pending on 15 December 1971?
  3. What is the effect of the proviso — “shall rely only on the materials on record and shall not take any fresh evidence”?
Arguments
For the workmen — Mr. Deshmukh, Miss Indra Jai Singh and others
  1. §11-A has completely re-written the law: the limitations on the Tribunal’s jurisdiction laid down in Indian Iron & Steel Co. are wholly superseded.
  2. Where the domestic enquiry is found defective or none was held, the order of discharge/dismissal becomes, “without anything more”, unjustified — the Tribunal has no option but to order reinstatement; the employer cannot take advantage of his own illegality.
  3. The employer’s right to adduce fresh evidence before the Tribunal has been abrogated: the legislature intended that employers who fail to conduct a proper enquiry should not get a “second chance”.
  4. The proviso supports this reading: “materials on record” must mean only the material available at the domestic enquiry, and “fresh evidence” precisely refers to the employer’s first-time evidence, now prohibited.
  5. Standing orders certified under the IESO Act, 1946 are statutory terms and conditions of service; a dismissal passed in breach of them is illegal, and an illegal act cannot be made legal — the analogy being that just as an illegal strike cannot be lawful, so too an employer’s illegal omission of an enquiry cannot be cured.
  6. On retrospectivity: §11-A sits in Chapter IV (“Procedure, Powers and Duties of Authorities”); it is procedural, and procedural provisions apply to pending proceedings. The words “has been referred” (present perfect) show that the section speaks of a reference already made. No vested right of the employer has been taken away.
For the employers — Mr. Damania, Mr. M.G. Setalvad, Mr. Tarkunde
  1. §11-A makes only a narrow, specific change: it gives the Tribunal power to alter the punishment after it has accepted that the misconduct is proved — i.e., only at the remedy stage; it does not disturb the Tribunal’s inability to reappraise the finding itself where a proper enquiry was held.
  2. Pre-existing employer rights are untouched — to rely on a proper enquiry’s finding, and to adduce fresh evidence where the enquiry was absent/defective. There are no express words abrogating them, and a right recognised over decades is not taken away by implication.
  3. “Materials on record” must be read broadly — all evidence properly before the Tribunal, from the enquiry and any additional evidence properly led before it; “fresh evidence” means only evidence not properly on this composite record (i.e., evidence sought to be introduced at a later, improper stage).
  4. §11-A should not apply retrospectively: it affects substantive rights (the managerial prerogative over dismissal), not mere procedure; retrospective operation requires clear, express words or a necessary intendment, both absent. The proviso’s reference to “any proceeding under this section” itself shows that a proceeding can only exist from the date the section came into force.
  5. A faint further argument — that even the order of discharge/dismissal should have been passed on or after 15.12.1971 — was (rightly) not pursued.
Decision — Part A: the interpretation of §11-A
  • (1) Where a proper domestic enquiry was held: the Tribunal may now reappraise the evidence for itself and satisfy itself whether the finding of misconduct is correct — it is no longer confined to the four narrow grounds: “What was once largely in the realm of the satisfaction of the employer, has ceased to be so; and now it is the satisfaction of the Tribunal that finally decides the matter.” The limitations of Indian Iron “can no longer be invoked by an employer”.
  • (2) Where no enquiry was held, or the enquiry was defective: the employer’s right to adduce evidence for the first time before the Tribunal “has not been taken away”. The issue about the merits of the dismissal is “at large” before the Tribunal, which must decide the misconduct for itself; “in such a case, the exercise of managerial functions does not arise at all”.
  • (3) The proviso: “materials on record” means the composite record — (i) the enquiry evidence and proceedings; (ii) that evidence plus further evidence led before the Tribunal; (iii) evidence placed before the Tribunal for the first time by the employer and the workman’s rebuttal evidence. The proviso merely confines the Tribunal to that record and forbids it from taking fresh evidence of its own motion “as an appellate authority may normally do”.
  • (4) No mandatory-enquiry rule: the contention that a dismissal without a proper enquiry is illegal and must be followed by automatic reinstatement was rejected — there is no such provision in the IESO Act or the IDA; the IESO Act applies only to establishments under its §1(3) whereas the IDA applies to employers with no standing orders at all; the contention had already been rejected in Motipur; and accepting it would produce “incongruous” and “startling” results with §33.
  • (5) The net change (paras 50–51): power for the first time (a) to satisfy itself whether the misconduct is proved, even against a finding in a properly held enquiry; and (b) to interfere with the punishment imposed by the employer; with the proviso restricting the Tribunal to the materials on record.
  • (6) The advice that survives (para 53): a proper enquiry is still expected, “should not be an empty formality”, and where one has been properly held and a correct finding arrived at, the Tribunal “will have to give very cogent reasons for not accepting the view of the employer”; such an enquiry also lets the employer “escape the charge of having acted arbitrarily or mala fide” and conduces to “harmonious and healthy relationship” and industrial peace.
Decision — Part B: retrospectivity
  • “Section 11-A has no application to disputes referred prior to December 15, 1971. The section applies only to disputes which are referred for adjudication on or after December 15, 1971.”
  • Reasons: (i) the words “has been referred” cannot be isolated from context; (ii) the proviso’s phrase “in any proceeding under this section” — there cannot be a proceeding under a section before the section came into force; (iii) the section was brought into force by notification only on 15.12.1971, after the Amendment Act received assent on 8.12.1971; and (iv) §11-A abridges recognised managerial rights, so retrospective operation would require a clear, express and manifest indication, which is absent.
  • Result: the appeal in which the Tribunal had held §11-A inapplicable (C.A. No. 1461 of 1972) was dismissed; the three appeals in which the Bombay Labour Court had applied §11-A to pre-1971 references were allowed to that extent and the matters remitted for adjudication in accordance with the judgment.
The ten principles of paragraph 32 — the pre-1971 law, restated
#Principle (as summarised in the judgment and repeated in the LB-503 material)
1The right to take disciplinary action and to decide the quantum of punishment are mainly managerial functions, but if a dispute is referred to a Tribunal, the latter has power to see if the action of the employer is justified.
2Before imposing punishment, an employer is expected to conduct a proper enquiry in accordance with the standing orders (if applicable) and the principles of natural justice; the enquiry “should not be an empty formality”.
3Where a proper enquiry has been held and the finding of misconduct is a plausible conclusion from the evidence, the Tribunal has no jurisdiction to sit in judgment as an appellate body; interference is justified only where the findings are perverse or the management is guilty of victimisation, unfair labour practice or mala fides.
4Even if no enquiry has been held, or the enquiry is defective, the Tribunal, to satisfy itself about the legality and validity of the order, must give an opportunity to the employer and the employee to adduce evidence; the employer may lead evidence for the first time and the employee may lead evidence contra.
5Where no enquiry was held, the Tribunal is not confined to asking whether there was a prima facie case: the merits are at large and the Tribunal decides for itself whether the misconduct is proved; “a case of defective enquiry stands on the same footing as no enquiry”.
6The Tribunal gets jurisdiction to consider evidence placed before it for the first time only if no enquiry was held or the enquiry is found defective.
7It has never been recognised that the Tribunal should straightaway, without anything more, direct reinstatement merely because no domestic enquiry was held or the enquiry was defective.
8An employer who wants the opportunity of adducing evidence for the first time should ask for it at the appropriate stage; if asked for, the Tribunal has no power to refuse — the opportunity is in the interest of both the management and the employee and enables the Tribunal to satisfy itself about the alleged misconduct.
9Once the misconduct is proved — whether in the employer’s enquiry or by evidence before the Tribunal for the first time — the punishment cannot be interfered with except where it is so harsh as to suggest victimisation. (This is the seed of the proportionality rule in this unit.)
10After setting aside the order of dismissal, whether the workman should be reinstated or paid compensation is, as held in Management of Panitole Tea Estate v. The Workmen, within the judicial discretion of the Labour Court or Tribunal.

Note for the exam: paragraphs 32(1)–(9) are commonly referred to as the “nine principles” because principle (10), on reinstatement-versus-compensation, is sometimes treated as an addendum drawn from Panitole Tea Estate. Say “nine principles (paragraph 32)” — that is the form used by the Bar and in the Unit-4 material.

Ratio Decidendi — in five lines
  1. Two new powers: §11-A empowers the Tribunal (a) to reappraise the evidence and differ from the employer’s finding of misconduct even after a proper domestic enquiry; and (b) to modify the punishment, awarding a lesser punishment even where the misconduct is proved.
  2. One preserved right: §11-A does not abolish the employer’s right to adduce evidence for the first time where no enquiry was held or the enquiry is defective; the proviso restricts the Tribunal’s record, not the parties’ rights to make that record.
  3. No mandatory enquiry: the absence of a domestic enquiry does not render the dismissal illegal or make reinstatement automatic.
  4. Prospective operation: §11-A applies only to disputes referred on or after 15 December 1971.
  5. Composite record: the Tribunal may act on all materials properly placed before it, but may not call for fresh evidence of its own.
How to use Firestone
  • Any question on §11-A’s scope: state the two new powers + the preserved right + the retrospective holding, and quote “the satisfaction of the Tribunal … finally decides the matter”.
  • Predicted question: “Trace the evolution of the Tribunal’s power over dismissal disputes from Indian Iron & Steel to Firestone” — use Part A.1 (pre-1971), paragraph 32 (the nine/ten principles) and paragraphs 36–41 (what §11-A changed).
  • “Does §11-A abolish the employer’s right to lead fresh evidence?” — this is the single most commonly set “trap”; the answer is No, with the two reasons (no abrogation; avoiding incongruity with §33) and the small-employer illustration.
  • “Is §11-A retrospective?” — No; the proviso’s phrase “in any proceeding under this section” is the clinching textual point.
  • Link to Unit 4: the same judgment supplies principles 4–8 on defective enquiries and the “not an empty formality” requirement.
Precision guards
  • Cite it as (1973) 1 SCC 813 : AIR 1973 SC 1227. The judgment’s operative paragraphs on §11-A are 33–53; the retrospectivity holding is at paras 54–67; the ten principles are at para 32.
  • Do not say §11-A gave the Tribunal “unlimited” power. The Tribunal’s power to differ on the finding is broad; its power over the punishment is fenced by proportionality (Hombe Gowda, Part B.2).
  • Do not say the pre-1971 law is entirely irrelevant. Paragraph 32 remains the standard summary of the pre-1971 jurisprudence and of the employer’s fresh-evidence right.
  • The employer’s right survives only where the enquiry was absent or defective — not where a proper enquiry was held (then the employer relies on the enquiry record).

⑰ Hombe Gowda Educational Trust v. State of Karnataka CASE No. 17 · Unit 5

(2006) 1 SCC 430 · Civil Appeals arising out of SLP (C) Nos. … of 2003, decided 2 December 2005 · Bench: S.B. Sinha, J. · The outer limit on the power to modify punishment
Context — the case that put a fence around §11-A

Firestone said the Tribunal may award a lesser punishment. Hombe Gowda says how far it may go: the jurisdiction is a “limited jurisdiction” and it may be exercised “only when … it is found to be grossly disproportionate” — a threshold captured in the formula that interference must rest on a finding that “no reasonable person could inflict such punishment”. It is also the leading authority for the proposition that provocation is mitigating, not exculpatory, and that an assault on a superior is “gross indiscipline”. The case is studied here although the employer was a grant-in-aid college, because the Tribunal’s jurisdiction under the Karnataka Act was expressly held to be “akin to one under Section 11-A of the Industrial Disputes Act”.

Facts
  1. The parties: the appellant No. 1 was the Hombe Gowda Educational Trust; appellant No. 2, Kuvempu Mahavidyalaya, a private educational institution receiving grant-in-aid from the State of Karnataka under the Grant-in-Aid Code of the Karnataka Collegiate Education Department; the institution is governed by the Karnataka Private Educational Institutions (Discipline and Control) Act, 1975. Respondent No. 3, Venkappa Gowda, was a lecturer in that college.
  2. The incident (18 September 1987): the Principal (RW-1) refused to permit the lecturer to sign the attendance register in the morning (because he had not signed the afternoon register). There was a verbal altercation which “turned into a heated argument”. According to the Principal the lecturer abused him in vulgar language; the Principal pushed him; the lecturer caught hold of the Principal’s collar; the Principal pushed him down, causing him to fall; the lecturer got up and hit the Principal with a chappal.
  3. The disciplinary action: the lecturer was charge-sheeted for (i) unauthorised absence and conduct unbecoming of a lecturer, and (ii) misbehaviour and physical assault on the Principal; the domestic enquiry found him guilty and he was dismissed from service.
  4. Before the Educational Appellate Tribunal (constituted under §§8 and 10 of the 1975 Act; its proceedings are by a legal fiction treated as judicial proceedings): a preliminary issue was framed whether the departmental proceedings were in consonance with Rule 14(2) of the CCS (CCA) Rules; the Tribunal held the departmental proceeding to be invalid in law. The management therefore adduced evidence before the Tribunal to prove the charges (an exact parallel to the Firestone “first-time evidence” situation).
  5. The Tribunal’s findings: it framed three questions — (1) whether unauthorised absence and conduct unbecoming of a lecturer were proved; (2) whether the evidence established misbehaviour on 18.9.1987 and physical assault on the Principal; and (3) if so, whether dismissal was justified or a lesser punishment deserved. It held Charge 1 NOT satisfactorily proved; on Charge 2 it held the assault proved, but added: “the incident would not have occurred had the Principal employed restraint upon his words and action… even the act of the appellant in using chappal to assault the Principal cannot under any circumstances be justified… the Principal and the appellant have acted in an undesirable manner and unbecoming of academicians.”
  6. The substituted punishment: instead of dismissal, the Tribunal ordered withholding of three increments, with reinstatement and full pecuniary benefits retrospectively from the date of dismissal minus the withheld increments, the management being made liable to pay and the State being held vicariously liable.
  7. The High Court held that “when the action of the petitioner in assaulting the Principal with chappal stands proved by the evidence of RWs 1 to 5, whatever may be the provocation for such a conduct, the said conduct of the petitioner cannot be justified under any circumstances”; since the lecturer had in the meantime reached the age of superannuation within three months of the Tribunal’s order, the High Court reduced back wages to 60%. All three parties — the Trust, the State and the lecturer — went up to the Supreme Court.
Issues
  1. Whether the Tribunal was justified in interfering with the punishment of dismissal and substituting a lesser punishment (withholding of three increments) despite a concurrent finding of a grave misconduct (assault on the head of the institution) by the Tribunal and the High Court.
  2. Whether the doctrine of proportionality applies to the substitution of punishment in such a case — and, if so, what is the threshold for interference.
  3. Whether grave provocation by the Principal, and the management’s failure to proceed against the Principal, could justify or mitigate the misconduct so as to reduce the punishment.
  4. Whether, the management having once sought and obtained time to comply with the High Court’s order, it was precluded from maintaining the appeal.
Arguments
For the management (Hombe Gowda Educational Trust / Kuvempu Mahavidyalaya) — Mr. R.S. Hegde and Mr. S.R. Hegde
  1. A finding of fact of a grave misconduct had been arrived at both by the Tribunal and by the High Court — the lecturer had assaulted the Principal, the head of the institution.
  2. Given such a concurrently established, grave misconduct, there was “absolutely no justification” in directing payment of 60% back wages after setting aside the punishment of dismissal — let alone in reducing dismissal to a mere withholding of three increments.
  3. Discipline in educational institutions is paramount; assaulting a superior is “gross indiscipline” warranting the severest sanction, and a teacher’s conduct must be an example to the pupils.
For the lecturer (Respondent 3) — Mr. S.N. Bhat
  1. The Principal himself provoked the incident: RW-1 admitted that he did not permit the lecturer to sign the attendance register, that there was a verbal altercation, and (per the witness Dhanpal) that the Principal told him he would not permit him to sign even the morning registers. The Tribunal’s finding was that “all was not well between the Principal and the appellant”.
  2. Both were at fault, yet only the lecturer was proceeded against. Since the Principal was equally guilty, it was obligatory on the management to initiate a departmental proceeding against him too; the management was guilty of being “selectively vindictive” — a fit case for the Supreme Court not to exercise its discretionary jurisdiction under Article 136.
  3. Charge 1 was not proved at all, a point not given due weight.
  4. The management had sought time to comply with the High Court’s order; having obtained that indulgence it was estopped and precluded from maintaining the appeal.
  5. The concurrent findings of the Tribunal (reducing the punishment) and of the High Court (partly affirming it) should not be disturbed under Article 136.
Decision (Holding)
  • Appeals allowed; the impugned judgments of the Tribunal and the High Court were set aside — i.e., the reduction of dismissal to withholding of three increments (and the consequential back-wage award) was not sustainable.
  • On estoppel (para 15): “It is now well settled that by seeking extension of time to comply with the order of the High Court by itself does not preclude a party aggrieved to question the correctness or otherwise of the order of the High Court as thereby a party to a lis does not waive his right to file an appeal before this Court.”
  • On provocation and the unpunished Principal (para 16): it may be true that no departmental proceeding was initiated against the Principal, “but the same by itself would not be a relevant fact for imposing a minor punishment upon the respondent”; and although the lecturer acted under grave provocation, both the Tribunal and the High Court had held the charges established.
  • THE CENTRAL DOCTRINAL HOLDING (para 17): “The Tribunal’s jurisdiction is akin to one under Section 11-A of the Industrial Disputes Act. While exercising such discretionary jurisdiction, no doubt it is open to the Tribunal to substitute one punishment by another; but it is also trite that the Tribunal exercises a limited jurisdiction in this behalf. The jurisdiction to interfere with the quantum of punishment could be exercised only when, inter alia, it is found to be grossly disproportionate.”
  • The threshold (para 18): “This Court repeatedly has laid down the law that such interference at the hands of the Tribunal should be inter alia on arriving at a finding that no reasonable person could inflict such punishment. The Tribunal may furthermore exercise its jurisdiction when relevant facts are not taken into consideration by the management which would have direct bearing on the question of quantum of punishment.”
  • On the misconduct (paras 19–20): “Assaulting a superior at a workplace amounts to an act of gross indiscipline. The respondent is a teacher. Even under grave provocation a teacher is not expected to abuse the head of the institution in filthy language and assault him with a chappal. Punishment of dismissal from services, therefore, cannot be said to be wholly disproportionate so as to shock one’s conscience. … A person, when dismissed from service, is put to a great hardship but that would not mean that a grave misconduct should go unpunished. Although the doctrine of proportionality may be applicable in such matters, but a punishment of dismissal from service for such a misconduct cannot be said to be unheard of. Maintenance of discipline of an institution is equally important.”
  • On the unpunished Principal (para 29): “Indiscipline in an educational institution should not be tolerated. Only because the Principal of the institution had not been proceeded against, the same by itself cannot be a ground for not exercising the discretionary jurisdiction by us. It may or may not be that the management was selectively vindictive but no management can ignore a serious lapse on the part of a teacher whose conduct should be an example to the pupils.”
  • Binding effect of precedent (paras 30–32): the Court spoke of the “change in economic policy of the country” and that “it may not now be proper to allow the employees to break the discipline with impunity”; “[l]aw declared by this Court in terms of Article 141 … categorically demonstrates that the Tribunal would not normally interfere with the quantum of punishment imposed by the employers unless an appropriate case is made out therefor. The Tribunal being inferior to this Court was bound to follow the decisions of this Court”. Citing Dwarikesh Sugar Industries Ltd. v. Prem Heavy Engg. Works, it deprecated “judicial adventurism” by subordinate courts in ignoring settled law; and in D. Navinchandra & Co. v. Union of India noted that legal positions laid down by the Court bind all concerned.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. The Tribunal’s jurisdiction to modify punishment under §11-A and its analogues is limited and discretionary — not an appellate power to re-do the punishment it would itself have chosen.
  2. Interference with the quantum of punishment is warranted only where the punishment is “grossly disproportionate” — the test being that “no reasonable person could inflict such punishment”; additionally, where relevant facts bearing on quantum were not considered by the management.
  3. Provocation is a mitigating, not an exculpatory, factor: it does not render dismissal disproportionate for a serious act such as physical assault on a superior.
  4. Assault on a superior at the workplace is “gross indiscipline”, ordinarily warranting the severest sanction; the Tribunal may also interfere where relevant facts bearing on quantum were not considered by the management.
  5. Selective non-action against another culpable party is not, by itself, a ground to reduce the punishment of the offender who has been proceeded against.
  6. Organisational discipline is a value co-equal with worker protection — “discipline at the workplace/industrial undertakings” must not be allowed to “receive a setback”.

Precision guards
  • This is not an IDA case: the statutory source of the Tribunal’s power was the Karnataka Private Educational Institutions (Discipline and Control) Act, 1975.   • The Court did not say that dismissal is mandatory for every assault; it said dismissal for this misconduct was not “wholly disproportionate” or “unheard of” — proportionality remains a fact-based inquiry.
  • Charge 1 (unauthorised absence) was not proved; the case therefore turned entirely on the assault charge.

⑱ Scooters India Ltd. v. Labour Court CASE No. 18 · Unit 5

AIR 1989 SC 149 — Special Leave Petition (Civil) directed against the Allahabad High Court’s dismissal of W.P. No. 2305 of 1986 · Bench: R.S. Pathak, C.J. and S. Natarajan, J. · Decided on the U.P. analogue of §11-A, §6(2-A) of the U.P. Industrial Disputes Act, 1947
Context — the power working, and being upheld

The value of Scooters India in this unit is that it shows the other side of the coin from Hombe Gowda: a Labour Court which accepted the enquiry as fair and the misconduct as proved, and yet set aside the dismissal and ordered reinstatement with 75% back wages. The Supreme Court refused to interfere, because the discretion had been exercised judicially — with reasons appearing on the face of the award — and because “justice must be tempered with mercy”. Read with Hombe Gowda, it gives the rule of degree: courts defer to a reasoned, non-extreme exercise of the §11-A power, but strike down an extreme or unreasoned one.

Facts
  1. The respondent-workman of Scooters India Ltd. (a public sector undertaking) was issued three separate charge memos — dated 23 March 1981, 30 April 1981 and 21 July 1981 — and separate domestic enquiries were held in respect of the charges in each of them.
  2. The charges pertained to acts of major misconduct — including, according to the management, the distribution of an offensive pamphlet (on 19.3.1981) and other “subversive” activity, with the stand-off between the workman and the management having become bitter and personal.
  3. All the charges were held proved in the respective enquiries, and the Presiding Officer of the Labour Court found that the enquiries “conformed to the statutory prescriptions and the principles of natural justice and were not vitiated in any manner”, so that the findings rendered by the Inquiry Officer and accepted by the Disciplinary Authority “were not open to challenge”.
  4. On those findings the workman’s services were terminated. A reference was made to the Labour Court under §4(k) of the U.P. Industrial Disputes Act, 1947.
  5. Even so, the Presiding Officer held: “Having regard to all these circumstances and the reasons given above I would hold that the order of termination was not justified in the circumstances of this case. I would therefore set aside the order of termination of service and direct that the workman shall be reinstated within one month of the award becoming enforceable.” He then added the important reasoning on back wages: “The workman has unfortunately to blame himself for much of the bad blood which has developed between him and the management and therefore his conduct, motivated by ideals which are not relevant, has been far from satisfactory, insofar as it was rough, bordering on rudeness and with highly exaggerated sense of his duties. In these circumstances it will meet the ends of justice if back wages to the extent of 75 per cent are allowed to the workman.”
  6. Both sides challenged the award in the Allahabad High Court: the company by W.P. No. 2305 of 1986, and the workman by W.P. No. 6769 of 1986 (seeking full back wages instead of 75%). The High Court dismissed both petitions, adverting to §6(2-A) of the U.P. Act, “analogous to Section 11-A of the Industrial Disputes Act”, which confers wide powers on the Labour Court to set aside a discharge/dismissal and to substitute any lesser punishment.
  7. Only the company came to the Supreme Court by special leave; the workman did not challenge the dismissal of his own writ petition (and hence did not press for full back wages).
Issues
  1. Whether the Labour Court could interfere with the punishment of dismissal even though it had upheld the domestic enquiries as fair and lawful and the misconduct as proved.
  2. Whether, in doing so, it had exercised its powers under §6(2-A) in a judicial manner or in an arbitrary manner.
  3. Whether the award of 75% back wages (rather than full back wages or none) was a permissible exercise of the Labour Court’s discretion.
Arguments
For the company (petitioner) — Mr. Manoj Swarup
  1. Three orders, not one: the High Court proceeded on the basis that there was only one order of termination passed in respect of all three enquiries, whereas a separate order of termination had been passed on the findings in each enquiry.
  2. Wrong standing-order clause: the distribution of an offensive pamphlet had taken place outside the factory premises, so paragraph 14.2(13) of the certified standing orders (which speaks of distribution/exhibition of offensive handbills, pamphlets, etc. inside the factory premises) was not attracted; the conduct would fall, if at all, under 14.2(20) (subversive acts).
  3. Excess of jurisdiction: once the Labour Court itself found the enquiries fair and valid and the misconduct proved, it had exceeded its jurisdiction under §6(2-A) by nevertheless interfering with the punishment — this was an arbitrary, not a judicial, exercise of discretion.
For the workman (respondent) — appeared in person
  1. The section (like §11-A) expressly permits the Labour Court, being satisfied that the termination was not justified, to set aside the order and substitute “any lesser punishment” — the very thing it did.
  2. The Labour Court had weighed the whole history of the dispute, including the workman’s own contribution to the “bad blood”, and had moderated the relief accordingly by awarding only 75% of back wages — a balanced, not a partisan, order.
  3. The High Court had examined all three charge-sheets, the findings and the scope of §6(2-A), and had rightly declined to interfere.
Decision (Holding)
  • The special leave petition was dismissed — the order of the High Court declining to quash the Labour Court’s award did not call for interference. Reinstatement with 75% back wages stood.
  • On the “three orders” contention: rejected on facts — “the order of termination of service refers only to Clause 14.2(13) of the Certified Standing Orders and not to Clause 14.2(20)”, and the High Court had in fact dealt with all three charge-sheets and the findings.
  • On the standing-order mismatch: the High Court “has referred in detail … to all the three charge-sheets and the findings rendered on those charges and as such … cannot be said to have committed a serious error” in its description of the record.
  • THE CENTRAL HOLDING: “It cannot therefore be said that the Labour Court had exercised its powers under Section 6(2-A) of the Act in an arbitrary manner and not in a judicial manner. The Labour Court has taken the view that justice must be tempered with mercy and that the erring workman should be given an opportunity to reform himself and prove to be a loyal and disciplined employee of the petitioner company. It cannot therefore be said that merely because the Labour Court had found the enquiry to be fair and lawful and the findings not to be vitiated in any manner, it ought not to have interfered with the order of termination of service.”
  • Closing observations (para 8): “we hope and trust that the respondent will conduct himself in future in such a manner as to prove himself to be a dedicated and worthy employee of a public sector concern… It will not only be in the interests of the respondent but in the interests of all the workers as well as the petitioner company…” — the rehabilitative philosophy of §11-A relief.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. Under §11-A and its State analogues, the Tribunal may set aside a dismissal and substitute a lesser punishment even where the domestic enquiry was valid and the misconduct proved — the power is not conditioned on any defect in the enquiry.
  2. What the law requires is that the discretion be exercised judicially: the reasons must be reasoned and considered, and must appear on the record; a reasoned, non-extreme exercise will be respected by the higher courts.
  3. “Justice must be tempered with mercy” and the giving of an opportunity to reform are legitimate considerations within the §11-A discretion.
  4. The Tribunal’s remedial discretion is wide and graduated: it may order reinstatement with full, partial (“75%”) or no back wages, or compensation — it is not confined to the binary choice of “dismissal upheld” or “full reinstatement with full back wages”.

⑳ Raghubir Singh v. General Manager, Haryana Roadways, Hissar CASE No. 20 · Unit 5

2014 (6) SLR 6 (SC) · 2014 AIR SCW 5515 · Civil Appeal No. 8434 of 2014 (arising out of SLP (C) No. 22487 of 2012), decided 3 September 2014 · Bench: Sudhansu Jyoti Mukhopadhaya and V. Gopala Gowda, JJ. (judgment by Gopala Gowda J.) · Proportionality as a constitutional standard
Context — why this case closes the unit

Raghubir Singh is the case that shows the doctrine of proportionality operating outside the narrow frame of §11-A — as an Article 14/16/19/21 standard against a statutory public employer (Haryana Roadways, a State undertaking). It is also a rich case on three collateral points the examiner can test: (i) limitation in industrial disputes (there is none — §10(1) says “at any time”, and delay is only a factor in moulding relief); (ii) the duty of the Labour Court to adjudicate on merits under §11-A, and the failure to do so being a “failure to exercise its statutory power coupled with duty”; and (iii) the legal regime applicable to a “workman” — the Model Standing Orders under the IESO Act, 1946, and not the civil-service Punishment and Appeal Rules.

Issues
  1. Whether the Labour Court and the High Court erred in holding the reference barred by limitation — i.e., whether delay in raising an industrial dispute can defeat a reference made under §10(1)(c).
  2. Whether the termination of the workman was legal, he having been terminated without any enquiry and merely by invoking Article 311(2)(b).
  3. Whether a person who is a “workman” under §2(s) of the IDA, employed by a statutory body, is governed by the Model Standing Orders under the IESO Act, 1946 or by the Haryana Civil Services (Punishment and Appeal) Rules, 1987.
  4. Whether the punishment of termination for the alleged unauthorised absence was justified, or was disproportionate to the gravity of the misconduct.
  5. Whether the workman was entitled to reinstatement with back wages and consequential benefits, and from what date.
Arguments
For the workman (appellant) — Mr. … (through Ms. Priya Puri, advocate on record)
  1. The services were illegally terminated; no enquiry was conducted before termination, and the reasons for dispensing with the enquiry were not forthcoming in the termination order — the invocation of Article 311(2)(b) was therefore an abuse.
  2. The principles of natural justice were violated: the charge-sheet was never served (he was in custody/absconding from his village), no evidence was led, and no second show-cause notice on the proposed punishment was given (Ramzan Khan; Karunakar).
  3. The alleged unauthorised absence was explained: he had been falsely implicated in a criminal case filed by the employer itself, had a reasonable apprehension of arrest and was in judicial custody for two months; the employer could have granted leave without wages or extraordinary leave.
  4. The termination was “highly disproportionate to the gravity of misconduct” and hence “shocks the conscience” of the Court; proportionality, as a facet of natural justice and of Articles 14, 16, 19 and 21, required interference.
  5. Being a “workman” under §2(s), he was governed by the Model Standing Orders under the IESO Act, 1946 — certainly not by the civil-service punishment rules invoked by the employer.
For the employer (Haryana Roadways) — Mr. Narender Hooda, learned Additional Advocate General for the State of Haryana
  1. The Labour Court was right in rejecting the reference as barred by limitation, relying on Mohan Lal — delay in raising an industrial dispute is an important circumstance the Labour Court must keep in view, irrespective of whether the objection is raised.
  2. The workman remained continuously absent for more than five months; the charge-sheet was sent to his village residence through a special messenger but he was “neither found in his village residence nor did anyone know of his whereabouts”; notice was then published in the newspaper; he neither joined duty nor replied.
  3. He was “being unresponsive”, and therefore the termination was passed “in the public interest” under Article 311(2)(b) — which permits dispensing with the enquiry where it is not reasonably practicable to hold one.
  4. The High Court had found no illegality or irregularity in the termination, and the concurrent findings should not be disturbed under Article 136.
Decision (Holding)
  • On limitation — the reference is never barred: §10(1) empowers the appropriate Government to refer a dispute “at any time”; the function is administrative (Avon Services Production Agencies v. Industrial Tribunal, Haryana). Delay may cause a dispute to “wane into total eclipse” only where nobody kept it alive; but where it remained alive, “it does not cause the dispute to wane into total eclipse”, and the High Court “should not have quashed the reference merely on the ground of delay” — long delay may be considered by the adjudicator “while moulding its reliefs” (Sapan Kumar Pandit v. U.P. State Electricity Board).
  • Ajaib Singh v. Sirhind Co-operative Marketing Society — Article 137 of the Limitation Act has no application; “relief … cannot be denied to the workman merely on the ground of delay”; the plea of delay “is required to be proved as a matter of fact by showing the real prejudice and not as a merely hypothetical defence”; and even where there is delay, the Tribunal “can appropriately mould the relief by declining to grant back wages … till the date he raised the demand”.
  • S.M. Nilajkar v. Telecom District Manager — delay “would certainly be fatal if it has resulted in material evidence relevant to adjudication being lost and rendered not available”; nothing of that kind had happened.
  • On the facts: there was no delay at all on the part of the workman — the criminal case was pending until his acquittal in 2002, and he approached the conciliation officer immediately after the employer failed to honour the assurance of reinstatement. The decision in Mohan Lal was “not applicable” because there the Labour Court had decided relevant factors, whereas here it had simply rejected the reference without going into the merits.
  • The Labour Court’s duty: having been referred the dispute, it was “the mandatory statutory duty of the Labour Court under Section 11-A … to adjudicate the dispute on merits on the basis of evidence produced on record”; failing to do so — and answering the additional issue on limitation instead — “amounts to failure to exercise its statutory power coupled with duty”.
  • On the enquiry: “it is clear that no enquiry was conducted … to prove the alleged misconduct of unauthorised absence … The reason for dispensing with the enquiry is not at all forthcoming in the order of termination which refers to the aforesaid constitutional provision.”
  • On the applicable legal regime: “With regard to conduct and discipline of its employees the respondent is bound to follow the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946… The appellant being a workman as defined under Section 2(s) of the Act … he will be governed by the Model Standing Orders framed under the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946.” The disciplinary proceedings initiated under the Haryana Civil Services (Punishment and Appeal) Rules, 1987 were “not only untenable in law but also contrary to the legal principles laid down by this Court”.
  • On natural justice — Calcutta Dock Labour Board v. Jaffar Imam: even for detained employees, “it was absolutely essential that the appellant should have held a proper enquiry”, with notice of specific allegations, evidence led and tested, and liberty to lead defence evidence; the detention orders “can afford no justification for not complying with the relevant statutory provision and not following the principles of natural justice. Any attempt to short-circuit the procedure based on considerations of natural justice must … be discouraged if the rule of law has to prevail”.
  • On the second show-cause notice: the workman was dismissed without an enquiry and without a second notice on the proposed punishment, which is “mandatory in law” (Union of India v. Mohd. Ramzan Khan; Managing Director, ECIL v. Karunakar).
  • On the absence: “assuming for the sake of argument that the unauthorised absence of the appellant is a fact, the employer is empowered to grant of leave without wages or extraordinary leave” — a course not considered; the Court therefore deemed it proper to treat the unauthorised absence period as leave without wages.
  • PROPORTIONALITY — the heart of the judgment: “In our view, the termination order is vitiated since it is disproportionate to the gravity of misconduct alleged against him.” The employment was “the source of income for himself and his family members’ livelihood, thereby their liberty and livelihood guaranteed under Article 21 … is denied” (Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation). The respondent, a statutory body under the State Government, is “State” within Article 12; its employment is “a potential employment”; the order of termination violated the workman’s fundamental rights under Articles 14, 16, 19 and 21 and his statutory rights under the IDA (U.P. Warehousing Corporation v. Vijay Narayan Vajpayee — public-sector employment attracts the equality clauses).
  • The proportionality doctrine elaborated: “The principle of ‘Doctrine of Proportionality’ is a well recognised one to ensure that the action of the employer against employees/workmen does not impinge their fundamental and statutory rights. The above said important doctrine has to be followed by the employer/employers at the time of taking disciplinary action … to satisfy the principles of natural justice.” Referring to Om Kumar v. Union of India, the Court adopted the distinction between (i) primary review — where action is challenged as discriminatory under Article 14, the court applies proportionality; and (ii) secondary review — where action is challenged as arbitrary, the court applies the Wednesbury test, asking whether the administrator “acted illegally or has omitted relevant factors … or has taken irrelevant factors into consideration or whether his view is one which no reasonable person could have taken”; and noting Ranjit Thakur (“shockingly disproportionate”), B.C. Chaturvedi (punishment shocking the conscience — ordinarily remit, rarely substitute).
  • Final finding on proportionality: “we have to hold that the order of termination passed is highly disproportionate to the gravity of misconduct and therefore shocks the conscience of this Court. Hence, we hold that the appellant is entitled for the reliefs as prayed by him.”
  • On back wages: applying Deepali Gundu Surwase v. Kranti Junior Adhyapak Mahavidyalaya — reinstatement implies putting the employee back in the position he would have been in but for the illegal action; his family suffers “grave adversities”; if the employer wants to deny back wages it must “specifically plead and prove that during the intervening period the employee was gainfully employed and was getting the same emoluments”; “Denial of back wages to an employee, who has suffered due to an illegal act of the employer would amount to indirectly punishing the concerned employee and rewarding the employer”; and citing Hindustan Tin Works and Surendra Kumar Verma v. Central Government Industrial Tribunal — “the removal of an order terminating the services of workmen must ordinarily lead to the reinstatement … It is as if the order has never been, and so it must ordinarily lead to back wages too.”
  • The relief finally granted (para 43): (i) the Labour Court’s award and the High Court’s judgment/orders are set aside; (ii) the respondent is directed to reinstate the workman with back wages from the date of raising the industrial dispute, i.e. 02.03.2005, till the date of reinstatement, with all consequential benefits — continuity of service, wage revisions and other statutory monetary benefits; and (iii) since he was compelled to litigate to enforce his rights, the order was to be implemented within six weeks. The appeal was allowed. No costs.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. Proportionality is part of the law of disciplinary punishment: a punishment that is “highly disproportionate to the gravity of the misconduct” is vitiated, whether the challenge is under §11-A, under Articles 14/16 or as a facet of natural justice; the employer must apply the doctrine itself when choosing the penalty.
  2. Absence of an enquiry, with an unstated reason for dispensing with it, vitiates the termination — here aggravated by the invocation of Article 311(2)(b) against a person who was not even a civil servant but a “workman” under §2(s) IDA, governed by the Model Standing Orders under the IESO Act, 1946.
  3. Misconduct must be assessed in its full context: an absence caused by a criminal case filed by the employer itself, and by consequent judicial custody, cannot be treated as plain “unauthorised absence” without considering leave without wages/extraordinary leave; the period was accordingly treated as leave without wages.
  4. There is no limitation for a reference: §10(1) permits reference “at any time”; delay is not a bar but a factor that may lead the adjudicator to mould the relief (e.g., by restricting back wages to the date of the demand). A refusal to adjudicate on the merits is a failure to exercise statutory duty.
  5. Reinstatement with back wages is the normal rule where the termination is found illegal; the employer who seeks to deny back wages must plead and prove gainful employment of the workman — the burden is on the employer (Deepali Gundu Surwase; Hindustan Tin Works; Surendra Kumar Verma).
How to use Raghubir Singh
  • Proportionality question: this is your modern, constitutional authority — quote “highly disproportionate to the gravity of misconduct and therefore shocks the conscience of this Court”, and pair it with Om Kumar (primary vs secondary review) and Ranjit Thakur.
  • “Termination for unauthorised absence” problem: apply the case directly — check whether the absence was explained, whether the employer considered leave without wages/extreme leave, and whether an enquiry was held.
  • Limitation/“delay in raising the dispute” question: a ready-made answer — §10(1) “at any time” + Avon Services + Sapan Kumar Pandit + Ajaib Singh + Nilajkar, with the moulding-of-relief rule.
  • Back-wages question: use paras 39–43 (the burden on the employer; the “as if the order had never been” reasoning of Surendra Kumar Verma).
  • Standing orders question: the case is a striking illustration that a statutory body which employs “workmen” must use the IESO Act/Model Standing Orders machinery, not its civil-service rules.
Precision guards
  • The judgment contains a stray reference to §11-A having been inserted “w.e.f. 15.12.1972” — that is a typographical error in the report; the correct date is 15 December 1971.
  • Do not say the Court quashed the reference or awarded full back wages for the entire period from 1994: the relief was reinstatement with back wages from 02.03.2005 (the date the dispute was raised) with consequential benefits — the delay before that date was accounted for by moulding the relief.
  • The termination was held bad on multiple grounds: no enquiry (with no reason for dispensing with it), wrong legal regime, no second show-cause notice, and disproportionate punishment. A good answer states them cumulatively.
  • Raghubir Singh is a 2014 decision and therefore post-dates the IRC debate; cite it for the proportionality principle, and cite §50/§56 IRC for the statutory replacement of §11-A/§17-B.

CDoctrine of Proportionality in Awarding Punishments — the Full Doctrine

C.1 What the doctrine says, in one paragraph and then in pieces

The proposition

Proportionality requires that the punishment bear a reasonable relationship to the gravity of the misconduct proved, judged in the light of the surrounding circumstances. It operates at two levels in Indian industrial/service law: (i) as the statutory test written into §11-A (“may … give such other relief … including the award of any lesser punishment … as the circumstances of the case may require”) and its successor §50 IRC, 2020; and (ii) as a constitutional standard, an element of non-arbitrariness under Article 14 and (for public employment) Articles 15–16 and the right to livelihood under Article 21 — a State employer cannot impose a penalty that is “shockingly disproportionate”, such a penalty being per se arbitrary and therefore unconstitutional. In Firestone the germ of the rule appears as principle 9 of paragraph 32: punishment “cannot be interfered with except where it is so harsh as to suggest victimisation”. In Hombe Gowda it hardens into the “grossly disproportionate / no reasonable person could inflict such punishment” threshold. In Raghubir Singh it becomes a constitutional command binding on the employer before the matter ever reaches a court: a termination for explained absence “shocks the conscience” of the Court and violates Articles 14, 16, 19 and 21.

1. Legitimate aim

The penalty must serve a permissible purpose of employment discipline — deterrence of misconduct, maintenance of workplace order, protection of the employer’s property/confidence — and not an extraneous purpose such as victimisation for union activity, personal animosity, or the settling of scores. A penalty imposed to serve an extraneous purpose is mala fide and fails at the first hurdle.

2. Suitability (rational connection)

The measure must be rationally connected to the aim. Dismissal for conduct that has no nexus with the employment relationship, or dismissal of a person not proved to have done the act, fails. This is where the “no evidence / perversity” limbs of Indian Iron and the charge-proving function of §11-A do their work.

3. Necessity (least restrictive means)

Even a relevant penalty is excessive if a lesser penalty would have sufficed. This is precisely the space in which §11-A’s “any lesser punishment” clause operates: the Tribunal asks — condemning the misconduct in full — would a censure, stoppage of increments, or a period of suspension without pay answer the case?

4. Balancing (proportionality stricto sensu)

Weigh the impact on the workman — loss of livelihood, family hardship, years of service, pensionary consequences (Art 21, Olga Tellis) — against the loss of confidence and the disciplinary needs of the employer (A.T. Mane: “it is the loss of confidence which is the primary factor”). The balance must not be grossly skewed.

5. Reasons on the face of the record

Judicial Indian law adds a procedural overlay: the exercise of discretion “must be cogent and convincing and must appear on the face of the record”; it “is not to be arbitrary, vague and fanciful but legal and regular”. A substitute penalty without reasons is itself a ground of interference (Bharat Forge: not on “compassionate ground”; Surendra Kumar Verma: discretion must be judicial).

6. Degree, not direction

The Indian rule is one of degree. Courts do not ask whether they would have imposed the same penalty; they ask whether the penalty is within the range of reasonable penalties for that misconduct (V. Ramana: “deficiency in the decision-making process and not the decision”). Extreme reductions fail (Hombe Gowda); reasoned, moderate ones survive (Scooters India).

Verma (2006): judiciously, not arbitrarily; Raghubir Singh (2014): constitutional dimension.
Under the IRC, 2020§50(1) carries the doctrine forward: on an application under §53(6), if the Tribunal is satisfied that the discharge, dismissal “or otherwise termination” was not justified, it may set aside the order and direct reinstatement on terms or “give such other relief to the worker including the award of any lesser punishment in lieu of discharge or dismissal…”. Two things change: (i) the relief is now available on the worker’s own application, not only on a government reference; and (ii) the proviso “shall rely only on the materials on record and shall not take any fresh evidence” is retained — so the Firestone jurisprudence on materials and on the employer’s first-time evidence remains the working law.

C.5 The two-stage protection particular to industrial law — Firestone’s architecture

Stage 1 — justification (the finding)

The Tribunal asks whether the order of discharge/dismissal is “justified”: that inquiry covers the existence of the misconduct, its proof on the materials on record, the fairness of the enquiry, and the bona fides of the employer. If the misconduct is not proved, the order falls and relief follows.

Stage 2 — relief (Firestone principle 10)

Even where the dismissal stands (or the misconduct is proved), the Tribunal must still decide what relief to give, i.e., the quantum and the modalities — reinstatement with full or partial back wages, compensation in lieu, or a lesser punishment. This is the stage at which proportionality operates — and it is a statutory duty, not a favour (Raghubir Singh: failure to consider it is “failure to exercise its statutory power coupled with duty”).

C.6 Where proportionality does not operate — the limits

  • The employer’s interest counts too: in Scooters India the Supreme Court, while upholding the award, expressly cautioned that the interests of the employer are equally important and that a Labour Court which thinks a termination unjustified should also consider whether, on reinstatement, the workman would be able to work peacefully in the establishment. The doctrine is therefore not a one-way street — the employer’s loss of confidence and the viability of the employment relationship are equally relevant, as are the interests of the other workers.
  • Not a licence for judicial re-writing: Bharat Forge — the Labour Court “must act within the four corners” of the statute and cannot reduce a penalty on “an irrational or extraneous factor and certainly not on a compassionate ground”.
  • Not an appeal on quantum: V. Ramana; Hombe Gowda — the Tribunal is not to substitute the punishment it would have imposed.
  • Not available on the basis of mercy alone: “justice must be tempered with mercy” (Scooters India) is a reasoned mercy operating on the record; unanchored sympathy is not.
Model paragraph — “Explain the doctrine of proportionality in the award of punishments under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947” (5–6 marks)

Section 11-A of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (inserted by Act 45 of 1971, w.e.f. 15-12-1971) was enacted to give effect to ILO Recommendation No. 119 (1963) and to overcome the decision in Indian Iron & Steel Co. v. Their Workmen, AIR 1958 SC 130, under which a Tribunal could not touch the quantum of punishment once the misconduct was proved. The section now empowers the Tribunal, on being satisfied that the order of discharge or dismissal “was not justified”, to set aside the order and direct reinstatement on such terms as it thinks fit, “or give such other relief … including the award of any lesser punishment … as the circumstances of the case may require”. That last clause is the statutory home of proportionality, whose pre-1971 germ is Firestone’s ninth principle — punishment may be interfered with where it “is so harsh as to suggest victimisation”. The doctrine was then constitutionalised: a punishment “shockingly disproportionate” to the misconduct is arbitrary and violates Article 14, and where it destroys livelihood it engages Article 21 (Ranjit Thakur; B.C. Chaturvedi; Om Kumar; Raghubir Singh). Its judicial threshold is set by Hombe Gowda Educational Trust v. State of Karnataka, (2006) 1 SCC 430 — the Tribunal’s jurisdiction is a “limited” one, exercisable where the punishment is “grossly disproportionate”, i.e. where “no reasonable person could inflict such punishment”. But the doctrine is not a licence for reduction at large: a reasoned and moderate substitution will be respected (Scooters India Ltd. v. Labour Court, AIR 1989 SC 149, reinstatement with 75% back wages upheld), whereas an extreme or unreasoned one will be overturned, and a penalty may never be reduced on an irrelevant or compassionate ground (Bharat Forge). The standard is thus one of degree and reasons, not of judicial taste.

D.2 §50, Industrial Relations Code, 2020 — the operative text

Section 50 — Powers of Tribunal to give appropriate relief in case of discharge or dismissal of workers

“(1) Where an industrial dispute relating to the discharge, dismissal or otherwise termination of a worker has been referred to the Tribunal, or an application has been made by the worker under sub-section (6) of section 53, and in the course of the proceedings, the Tribunal is satisfied that the order of discharge, dismissal or otherwise termination was not justified, it may, by its award, set aside the order of discharge, dismissal or otherwise termination and direct reinstatement of the worker on such terms and conditions, if any, as it thinks fit, or give such other relief to the worker including the award of any lesser punishment in lieu of discharge, dismissal or otherwise termination as the circumstances of the case may require.

(2) Without prejudice to the generality of the foregoing provisions, the Tribunal may, in the interest of justice, grant such interim relief as it may consider necessary to the worker, during the pendency of the proceedings before it.

Provided that in any proceeding under this sub-section, the Tribunal shall rely only on the materials on record and shall not take any fresh evidence in relation to the matter.”

What is new in §50

  • Access: the worker may apply directly under §53(6); he no longer has to persuade a Government officer to refer the dispute.
  • Interim relief: §50(2) — an express statutory power to grant interim relief “in the interest of justice” during the pendency of the proceedings (e.g., subsistence allowance). There was no equivalent in §11-A; it was a gap-filling device of the courts.
  • “or otherwise termination”: widens the relief beyond terminologically pure “discharge” and “dismissal”.

What is retained — and therefore keeps the case law alive

  • The test — “not justified” — unchanged: the Firestone construction governs.
  • The twin reliefs — reinstatement on terms or “any lesser punishment” — unchanged: the proportionality cases (Scooters India, Hombe Gowda, Raghubir Singh) remain directly relevant.
  • The proviso — materials on record / no fresh evidence — unchanged: the entire Firestone-on-the-proviso analysis survives, including the employer’s first-time-evidence right (which Firestone derived from the pre-existing law, not from the wording of the proviso).

D.3 §56, Industrial Relations Code, 2020 — full wages pending appeal (formerly §17-B, IDA)

Section 56 — Payment of full wages to worker pending proceedings in higher courts

“Where in any case, a Tribunal by its award directs reinstatement of any workman and the employer prefers any proceedings against such award in a High Court or the Supreme Court, the employer shall be liable to pay such workman, during the period of pendency of such proceedings in the High Court or the Supreme Court, full wages last drawn by him inclusive of any maintenance allowance admissible to him under any rule, if the workman had not been employed in any establishment during such period and an affidavit by such workman had been filed to that effect in such Court:

Provided that where it is proved to the satisfaction of the High Court or the Supreme Court that such workman had been employed and had been receiving adequate remuneration during such period or a part of it, the Court may order that the award made shall not be effective or be effective only for that part of the period during which he was not so employed or receiving adequate remuneration.”

§17-B of the IDA, 1947 (inserted by Act 46 of 1982, w.e.f. 21-8-1984) is in identical terms; §56 is a verbatim successor. Reading them together shows the legislative technique of the Codes: transplant with limited reform.

F.2 Model answer — 15 marks (the dedicated Unit-5 answer)

Question

“Discuss the powers of the adjudicatory authorities in cases of discharge and dismissal of workmen under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, and examine the application of the doctrine of proportionality in the award of punishments, with reference to the prescribed case law.”

Answer

1. Introduction — the problem the law faced. Before 1971 the industrial adjudicator stood at the door of the employer’s disciplinary power but could not enter. In Indian Iron & Steel Co. Ltd. v. Their Workmen, AIR 1958 SC 130, the Supreme Court held that where a proper domestic enquiry had been held and a plausible conclusion of misconduct reached, the Tribunal had no jurisdiction to substitute its own judgment, interference being confined to four narrow grounds — want of good faith, victimisation or unfair labour practice, a basic error or violation of the principles of natural justice, and a perverse finding. Critically, the quantum of punishment lay wholly within the managerial function: once misconduct was proved, the Tribunal could neither reduce nor vary the punishment. The result was “binary justice” — either a crushing dismissal was confirmed or the workman was reinstated with full back wages, and the industrial adjudicator had no middle course. The Statement of Objects and Reasons of the Industrial Disputes (Amendment) Act, 1971 (Act 45 of 1971) records both the mischief and its cure, and expressly invokes ILO Recommendation No. 119 of 1963, which recommended that a worker aggrieved by termination be entitled to appeal to a neutral body empowered to examine the reasons for the termination, to decide upon its justification, and to award relief including reinstatement or “some other relief”.

2. The statutory text. Section 11-A (inserted by Act 45 of 1971 w.e.f. 15 December 1971; now §50 of the Industrial Relations Code, 2020) provides that where an industrial dispute relating to the discharge or dismissal of a workman has been referred for adjudication and the Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal “is satisfied that the order of discharge or dismissal was not justified”, it may by its award “set aside the order of discharge or dismissal and direct reinstatement of the workman on such terms and conditions, if any, as it thinks fit, or give such other relief to the workman including the award of any lesser punishment in lieu of discharge or dismissal as the circumstances of the case may require”, subject to the proviso that it “shall rely only on the materials on record and shall not take any fresh evidence in relation to the matter”. Three distinct powers are thus conferred: a power over the finding, a power over the quantum, and a power over the terms of relief — all exercisable on a closed record.

3. The construction of the section — Firestone. In The Workmen of M/s Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. of India P. Ltd. v. The Management, AIR 1973 SC 1227, Vaidialingam J. exhaustively reviewed the pre-1971 case law (paragraph 32 is the celebrated statement of the nine/ten principles of that era) and laid down five propositions:

  • (i) Power over the finding: “What was once largely in the realm of the satisfaction of the employer, has ceased to be so; and now it is the satisfaction of the Tribunal that finally decides the matter.” The Tribunal may now reappraise the evidence and differ from the employer’s finding “in a proper case”; the limitations of Indian Iron can no longer be invoked. But deference is not abolished: where a proper enquiry has been held and a correct finding arrived at, the Tribunal “will have to give very cogent reasons for not accepting the view of the employer”, and such an enquiry “should not be an empty formality”.
  • (ii) Power over the quantum: the Tribunal may award “any lesser punishment” even where the misconduct is proved — the very power that did not exist before 1971.
  • (iii) The proviso — “materials on record”: the expression means the composite record before the Tribunal, comprising (a) the enquiry evidence and proceedings; (b) that evidence plus further evidence led before the Tribunal; or (c) evidence placed before the Tribunal for the first time where no enquiry or a defective enquiry, together with the workman’s rebuttal. The proviso only confines the Tribunal and bars it from calling fresh evidence of its own, “as an appellate authority may normally do”.
  • (iv) The preserved right: §11-A does not abrogate the employer’s right to lead evidence for the first time where there was no enquiry or the enquiry was defective. There is no express or implied abrogation of a right “recognised over a long period of years”; and any other construction would produce “incongruous” and “startling” results, because under §33 a Tribunal may permit exactly such evidence. The Court’s homely illustration was the small employer who witnesses the misconduct himself and therefore cannot be both prosecutor and enquiry officer: he may impose punishment without an enquiry and later prove the misconduct before the Tribunal.
  • (v) No mandatory enquiry, and prospectivity: “There is no provision either in this statute or in the Act which states that an order of dismissal or discharge is illegal if it is not preceded by a proper and valid domestic enquiry.” Absence of an enquiry therefore does not make reinstatement automatic. And §11-A operates prospectively: it applies only to disputes referred on or after 15 December 1971, since it “abridges the rights of the employer” and the proviso’s phrase “in any proceeding under this section” shows that a proceeding must arise after the section came into force.

4. The doctrine of proportionality — the second statutory question. Section 11-A confers on the Tribunal, after it decides justification, a further duty to fix the relief: reinstatement on terms, or a lesser punishment, or compensation — Firestone’s tenth principle, drawn from Management of Panitole Tea Estate. The controlling doctrine is proportionality: the punishment must bear a reasonable relation to the gravity of the misconduct. Its Indian pedigree spans both service and industrial law — Ranjit Thakur v. Union of India, (1987) 4 SCC 611 (“the sentence has to suit the offence and the offender”; an “utterly disproportionate” punishment invites interference); B.C. Chaturvedi v. Union of India, (1995) 6 SCC 749 (interference where the punishment “shocks the conscience”; ordinarily remit, rarely substitute); Muriadih Colliery v. Bihar Colliery Kamgar Union, (2005) 3 SCC 331 (the Tribunal “should bear in mind the principle of proportionality between the gravity of the offence and the stringency of the punishment”); Om Kumar v. Union of India, (2001) 2 SCC 386 (primary review under Article 14 attracts proportionality; secondary review of “arbitrariness” applies the Wednesbury test, and the court must not substitute itself for the administrator).

5. The threshold — Hombe Gowda. Hombe Gowda Educational Trust v. State of Karnataka, (2006) 1 SCC 430 supplies the limit. The Respondent, a lecturer, assaulted his Principal with a chappal after a heated argument. The domestic enquiry having been held invalid, the Tribunal took evidence itself, found the assault proved, and substituted dismissal with the withholding of three increments, with 60% back wages affirmed on the High Court’s reduction. The Supreme Court set that aside, holding that the Tribunal’s jurisdiction (statutorily “akin to one under Section 11-A”) is a “limited jurisdiction”, to be exercised “only when … it is found to be grossly disproportionate” — the test being that “no reasonable person could inflict such punishment” — or where relevant facts bearing on quantum were not considered by the management. The Court added three propositions of lasting value: provocation mitigates but never excuses (“even under grave provocation a teacher is not expected to abuse the head of the institution in filthy language and assault him with a chappal”); assaulting a superior at the workplace is an act of gross indiscipline, and the maintenance of discipline in an institution is “equally important” to the workman’s protection; and the management’s failure to proceed against the other party (the Principal) does not reduce the delinquent’s punishment — “no management can ignore a serious lapse on the part of a teacher whose conduct should be an example to the pupils”, the Court deprecating “judicial adventurism” by Tribunals which ignore binding precedent under Article 141.

6. The power upheld — Scooters India. If Hombe Gowda is the ceiling, Scooters India Ltd. v. Labour Court, AIR 1989 SC 149 is the power in ordinary operation. The workman faced three charge-sheets (23.3.1981, 30.4.1981 and 21.7.1981) concerning, among other things, the distribution of an offensive pamphlet; all charges were proved in fair and lawful enquiries. Yet the Labour Court, acting under §6(2-A) of the U.P. Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (“analogous to Section 11-A”), found the termination “not justified”, ordered reinstatement with 75% back wages — blaming the workman himself for much of the “bad blood” and reducing the wage relief accordingly — and observed that “justice must be tempered with mercy”. The Supreme Court declined to interfere: “It cannot therefore be said that the Labour Court had exercised its powers under Section 6(2-A) of the Act in an arbitrary manner and not in a judicial manner”, and “merely because the Labour Court had found the enquiry to be fair and lawful … it ought not to have interfered with the order of termination”. Two lessons follow: the power over quantum does not depend upon a defect in the enquiry; and the test of legality is the quality of the reasons — a reasoned, moderate, graduated exercise of discretion will be respected.

7. The constitutional dimension — Raghubir Singh. Raghubir Singh v. General Manager, Haryana Roadways, Hissar, 2014 (6) SLR 6 (SC) shows that the doctrine binds the employer before any adjudication, as a facet of Articles 14, 16, 19 and 21. The appellant was a conductor terminated on 21.10.1994 on the footing of unauthorised absence, with the employer invoking Article 311(2)(b) although he was a “workman” under §2(s) of the IDA governed by the Model Standing Orders under the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 — not by the Haryana Civil Services (Punishment and Appeal) Rules. No enquiry was held and no reason for dispensing with it appeared in the order; the charge-sheet was never served; no second show-cause notice was given (Mohd. Ramzan Khan; Karunakar); the absence was itself the consequence of police custody in a case filed by the employer. The Supreme Court held the termination “highly disproportionate to the gravity of misconduct and therefore shock[ing] the conscience of this Court”; adopted the “doctrine of proportionality … to ensure that the action of the employer … does not impinge [the] fundamental and statutory rights” of the workman; invoked Olga Tellis on livelihood and Article 21 and the Article 12 character of the employer; held that the Labour Court’s refusal to adjudicate on merits was a “failure to exercise its statutory power coupled with duty” under §11-A; held the reference to be never barred by limitation (§10(1) “at any time”; delay only a factor in moulding relief — Avon Services, Sapan Kumar Pandit, Ajaib Singh, S.M. Nilajkar); treated the absence period as leave without wages; and ordered reinstatement with back wages from 02.03.2005 (the date the dispute was raised) with all consequential benefits, applying Deepali Gundu Surwase and Hindustan Tin Works — the employer, if it wishes to deny back wages, must plead and prove gainful employment, since denial “would amount to indirectly punishing” the workman.

8. Synthesis — the four propositions that answer any question on this unit.

  1. Justification is now the Tribunal’s judgment, not the employer’s. The adjudicator may reappraise the finding, but must respect a proper enquiry by giving cogent reasons for differing.
  2. The remedy is a spectrum, not a switch. Reinstatement on terms, a lesser punishment, or compensation — the discretion is wide but must be judicial, reasoned and recorded.
  3. Proportionality is the measuring rod, and it cuts both ways. A punishment is reduced only if grossly disproportionate (Hombe Gowda), never on compassion or an extraneous factor (Bharat Forge), while a reasoned moderate reduction will be upheld (Scooters India) and a plainly excessive termination will be struck down (Raghubir Singh).
  4. The record is disciplined. The proviso confines the Tribunal to the materials on record and forbids self-generated evidence, while the employer’s first-time evidence right survives where the enquiry was absent or defective, and the worker must be supported by wages pending appeal (§17-B / §56).

9. Conclusion. Section 11-A (now §50 IRC, 2020) marks the movement of the disciplinary power from managerial prerogative to adjudicatory scrutiny — but it is scrutiny of a disciplined kind. It converts the employer’s satisfaction into a justiciable satisfaction, gives the adjudicator the graduated remedies that Indian Iron had denied it, and, through the doctrine of proportionality, holds both employer and adjudicator to the same standard: the punishment must fit the misconduct and the misconduct alone. Where the enquiry is fair, the finding reasoned and the penalty proportionate, the adjudicator will not interfere; where the penalty is grossly disproportionate or the procedure deliberately short-circuited, it must. That is the balance struck by Firestone, fenced by Hombe Gowda, illustrated by Scooters India, and constitutionalised by Raghubir Singh.

F.3 Model answer — Dec 2024 (critical examination of the adjudicator’s power)

Question (critically examine)

“Section 11-A has replaced the satisfaction of the employer with the satisfaction of the Tribunal in matters of discharge and dismissal — yet the power is neither unfettered nor unreviewable.” Critically examine.

Answer — structure

(a) The proposition accepted, in part. The statement captures the essence of the change: before 1971 the Tribunal could not sit in judgment over a plausible finding nor touch the penalty (Indian Iron & Steel); after Firestone “the satisfaction of the Tribunal … finally decides the matter”. The subject of the newly decisive satisfaction is the composite test “was the order of discharge or dismissal justified?” — a question of the Tribunal’s own formation, not a review of the employer’s directions.

(b) Why it is “not unfettered” — five fetters. (i) The proviso: “materials on record”, no fresh evidence — the Tribunal cannot make out a case nobody pleaded. (ii) Deference to a proper enquiry: “very cogent reasons” are required to depart from a correct finding (Firestone, para 53). (iii) Proportionality threshold: interference with quantum only where “grossly disproportionate” — “no reasonable person could inflict such punishment” (Hombe Gowda). (iv) No reduction on irrelevant/compassionate grounds (Bharat Forge). (v) Employer’s surviving rights: the first-time evidence right where no enquiry/defective enquiry; and, before the adjudicator, the freedom to choose a penalty within the range of reasonable penalties (V. Ramana: review of the decision-making process, not the decision).

(c) Why it is not “unreviewable”. The award is subject to the High Court’s writ jurisdiction and to the Supreme Court under Article 136 — as is evident from the fate of the awards in Hombe Gowda (reversed) and Scooters India (upheld), and the judgments in Raghubir Singh (both the first award and the time-bar award were tested in appeal). The standard of review, however, is not appellate: an award will be interfered with only if perverse, without reasons, or based on irrelevant considerations, or if the Tribunal has exceeded its “limited jurisdiction” in modifying the punishment.

(d) The critique — where the proposition is over-simplified. (i) The Tribunal itself does not impose the punishment of its choice; it chooses between statutory options, the residual right of the employer to choose a penalty within the reasonable range surviving the transfer. (ii) The power is prospective only (Firestone) and procedural in form but substantive in effect — it “abridges the rights of the employer”. (iii) The judicial trend since 2005 (Muriadih, Bharat Forge, Hombe Gowda, A.P. SRTC v. V. Ramana) has pulled the pendulum back towards deference, so the “replacement” is partial and contextual. (iv) Under §50 IRC, 2020 the balance shifts again — in the worker’s favour procedurally (direct application under §53(6); interim relief under §50(2)), while the substantive proviso is retained.

(e) Conclusion. The proposition is best stated as a displacement of the employer’s monopoly, not of the employer’s role: the Tribunal decides justification and controls the outer bounds of punishment, while the employer’s managerial choice survives within a band of proportionate penalties, and both are disciplined by reasons on the record.

F.4 Model answer — May–June 2025 (ex parte enquiry + acquittal)

Question

A workman is dismissed after an ex parte domestic enquiry; he is subsequently acquitted in the criminal case on the same facts. Advise him on the arguments available before the Labour Court.

Answer

1. Attacking the enquiry. An ex parte enquiry is not per se invalid — the enquiry officer may proceed if the workman, duly served, wilfully absents himself; but the employer must be able to show valid service of the charge-sheet and of the enquiry notice, and that the procedure was fair. Where the charge-sheet was never served (as in Raghubir Singh, where it was sent to a village address while the workman was in custody, followed only by a newspaper notice), the enquiry is a nullity and natural justice stands violated: the workman must have notice of the specific allegations, the evidence must be led and tested in his presence, and he must have liberty to lead defence evidence (Calcutta Dock Labour Board v. Jaffar Imam; D.K. Yadav v. J.M.A. Industries). Add the non-supply of the enquiry report and the absence of a second show-cause notice on the proposed punishment (Union of India v. Mohd. Ramzan Khan; MD, ECIL v. Karunakar), noting that post-Karunakar the objection must be shown to have caused prejudice to justify interference.

2. If the enquiry is found defective, the matter is at large before the Tribunal (Firestone; Motipur Sugar Factory). The employer may then lead evidence for the first time, but the burden of proving the misconduct lies on the employer before the Tribunal, unaided by any presumption of regularity attaching to a fair enquiry; and the Tribunal decides the misconduct for itself on the whole record, including the workman’s evidence contra.

3. The acquittal. The correct proposition is that departmental and criminal proceedings are independent and rest on different standards of proof (beyond reasonable doubt versus preponderance of probabilities/conduct), so an acquittal does not automatically vitiate a domestic enquiry or its findings — provided the enquiry was held on evidence independent of the failed prosecution. But an acquittal is a highly material circumstance: (i) where the dismissal is founded exclusively on the same evidence which the criminal court has rejected, the finding and the penalty become unsustainable (Raghubir Singh, where the FIR at the employer’s instance had ended in acquittal and the termination was held bad on multiple grounds); (ii) acquittal with a clean finding of fact (e.g., that the money was deposited) destroys the substratum of the charge; and (iii) in Raghubir Singh an oral assurance of reinstatement on acquittal had been given, and the employer’s breach of that assurance was itself held to be a circumstance vitiating its conduct. Conversely, if the enquiry was fair and rested on independent evidence, the acquittal alone will not help (the well-known position on “honourable acquittal” being relevant to service cases and to relief, especially back wages, rather than to the validity of the enquiry itself).

4. Arguments on punishment and relief. Even if the misconduct survives, the workman may invoke proportionality: the punishment must fit the misconduct (Hombe Gowda also recognises interference where “relevant facts are not taken into consideration by the management” bearing on quantum); a long unblemished record, the period spent in custody, and the pendency of the criminal case are relevant; absent extraordinary circumstances, the absence period may be treated as leave without wages (Raghubir Singh). On relief, ask for reinstatement with back wages — the normal rule (Hindustan Tin Works; Surendra Kumar Verma; Deepali Gundu Surwase), the burden of proving gainful employment lying on the employer — or, in the alternative, compensation in lieu under Firestone’s tenth principle.

5. Procedure. Raise the dispute by a demand notice and conciliation, and seek a reference under §10(1)(c) or, after the IRC 2020 comes into force for the establishment, a direct application under §53(6) for relief under §50; no limitation bars the reference (§10(1) “at any time”), though delay may reduce back wages to the date of the demand (as in Raghubir Singh, where back wages ran from 02.03.2005). If the employer appeals against a reinstatement award, seek full wages last drawn under §17-B/§56 with an affidavit of unemployment.

6. Conclusion. The workman’s case rests on a three-legged stool: procedural illegality (no valid service, ex parte enquiry carried out in breach of natural justice), evidentiary bankruptcy (the acquittal and the entailed failure to prove misconduct on the record), and disproportionality of the penalty — with reinstatement and back wages as the presumptive relief.

F.5 Model answer — Nov–Dec 2025 (bank clerk; modification of punishment)

Question

A bank clerk is dismissed for a proved act of misconduct. The Labour Court reduces the punishment to stoppage of two increments. Discuss the permissibility and principles of such modification.

Answer

1. The source of the power. The Labour Court’s power is §11-A IDA / §50 IRC. It is not conditioned upon any defect in the enquiry: in Scooters India the enquiries were found “fair and lawful”, the misconduct proved, and the award of reinstatement with 75% back wages was still upheld, because the discretion had been exercised “in a judicial manner and not in an arbitrary manner”. The section expressly permits “the award of any lesser punishment in lieu of discharge or dismissal … as the circumstances of the case may require”.

2. But the jurisdiction is limited. Hombe Gowda fixes the threshold: interference with quantum is permissible “only when … it is found to be grossly disproportionate” — the test being “no reasonable person could inflict such punishment” — or where the management has failed to consider relevant facts bearing on quantum. A reduction resting on sympathy, on the workman’s family circumstances, or on the “compassionate” view of the adjudicator is impermissible (Bharat Forge: such an order “cannot be passed on an irrational or extraneous factor and certainly not on a compassionate ground”). Nor may the Tribunal sit as an appellate authority over the employer’s choice (V. Ramana; KSRTC v. A.T. Mane).

3. Banking is a context of heightened trust. The applicable standing orders/rules treat misconduct involving integrity — misappropriation, falsification of accounts, unauthorised credit, fraud on customers — as a matter of the gravest concern, because the loss of confidence, and not merely the money value, is the operative factor (KSRTC v. A.T. Mane: “it is the loss of confidence which is the primary factor”; UPSRTC v. Subhash Chandra Sharma; Rajasthan SRTC v. Sohan Lal). Where the proved misconduct is of that character, dismissal is ordinarily not grossly disproportionate and a reduction to two increments would exceed jurisdiction. Where, however, the proved misconduct is a breach of procedure without dishonesty, or an isolated lapse of long service with no loss, a reduction may be proportionate — and if it is reasoned and moderate, courts will not interfere (Scooters India; J.K. Synthetics; L.K. Verma: the power must be exercised “judiciously”).

4. The manner of the order. The order must show, on its face, the reasoning justifying the reduction: the seriousness of the charge as found, the workman’s record, the loss to the employer, the mitigating or aggravating factors, and an explanation of why the penalty chosen falls within the band of reasonable penalties. A silent or perfunctory exercise of the power invites interference (Surendra Kumar Verma: the exercise of discretion must be judicial, and reasons must appear on the record).

5. Conclusion. The modification is permissible in principle — §11-A confers exactly that power, and it is available even where the enquiry was valid — but it is sustainable only if (i) the punishment of dismissal was grossly disproportionate to the misconduct actually proved, judged by the standards of the particular employment; (ii) relevant considerations bearing on quantum were either not before the employer or not weighed by it; and (iii) the Tribunal records reasons that are cogent and legally relevant. In a banking case involving dishonesty, the answer would ordinarily be that the Tribunal could not reduce the punishment; in a case of a technical or venial breach, it could.

F.6 Short notes — the five-markers that recur

(a) Retrospectivity of §11-A

§11-A applies only to disputes referred on or after 15-12-1971 (Firestone, paras 65–67). Reasons: it abridges the employer’s rights recognised in Indian Iron, and a provision which impairs existing rights is not retrospective absent “clear, express and manifest indication”; and the proviso’s words “in any proceeding under this section” presuppose a proceeding arising after the section began. The word “has been referred” is capable of a wider reading but “cannot be isolated from the context”. The date of the order of dismissal is irrelevant; the date of the reference governs.

(b) Employer’s right to lead fresh evidence

Where no enquiry was held or the enquiry is defective, the employer may lead evidence for the first time before the Tribunal; a defective enquiry stands on the same footing as no enquiry (Motipur); the right was not abrogated by §11-A because (i) there is no express or implied abrogation, and (ii) any other view would be inconsistent with §33. The Tribunal may not refuse the opportunity if sought at the proper stage. The workman may lead evidence contra, and the merits are “at large”.

(c) “Materials on record”

A composite expression covering (i) the enquiry evidence and proceedings; (ii) that plus further evidence led before the Tribunal; (iii) evidence led before the Tribunal for the first time where there was no/defective enquiry, together with the workman’s rebuttal. The proviso to §11-A/§50 bars only the Tribunal’s own fresh evidence — the adjudicator is not an appellate court with power to remit for evidence.

(d) §11-A compared with §33

§33 governs the employer’s action during the pendency of a dispute (permission/approval; IRC §90); it is a protective provision operating before or alongside the adjudication. §11-A governs the final adjudication of the discharge/dismissal once referred. §11-A did not change §33; proceedings under §33 form part of the materials on record before the §11-A Tribunal; and because a §33 Tribunal may itself allow first-time evidence, §11-A must be construed to preserve that right (otherwise “startling results”).

(e) §17-B IDA / §56 IRC — wages pending appeal

Where an award directs reinstatement and the employer appeals to the High Court/Supreme Court, the employer must pay full wages last drawn (including maintenance allowance) during the pendency, provided the workman was not employed in any establishment and files an affidavit to that effect; if the employer proves the workman was gainfully employed at adequate remuneration, the direction may be confined to the balance period or refused. It makes the reinstatement remedy real and protects livelihood (Art 21).

(f) Reinstatement with back wages — the burden

Reinstatement with back wages is the normal rule once the termination is found illegal (Hindustan Tin Works; Surendra Kumar Verma — “as if the order has never been”); the employer who resists back wages must plead and prove that the workman was gainfully employed; denial would “indirectly punish” the workman (Deepali Gundu Surwase). But the Tribunal may mould relief — awarding no back wages, or wages from the date of the demand notice, as in Raghubir Singh.

F.8 Answer-writing technique for this unit

Do

  • Open with the statutory text and the date (Act 45 of 1971, w.e.f. 15-12-1971).
  • Quote one phrase per proposition — “not justified”, “any lesser punishment”, “materials on record”, “very cogent reasons”, “grossly disproportionate”, “no reasonable person could inflict such punishment”, “shocks the conscience”.
  • Structure by issue, not by case: justification → finding → quantum → relief → procedure.
  • Name the case and the judge/bench where it adds authority (Firestone — Vaidialingam J.; Hombe Gowda — S.B. Sinha J.; Raghubir Singh — V. Gopala Gowda J.).
  • Use the statutory successor in a footnote-style line: “now §50, IRC, 2020”.

Do not

  • Do not say §11-A applies retrospectively — the commonest error.
  • Do not say §11-A abolished the employer’s right to lead fresh evidence.
  • Do not say the Tribunal can impose whatever punishment it likes — it can only award any lesser punishment, and only for gross disproportionality.
  • Do not cite “§83 IRC” for §11-A — it is §50.
  • Do not merge Scooters India with Hombe Gowda — the contrast is the answer.
  • Do not forget the proviso — most answers lose marks by discussing the Tribunal’s power without its limit.

G.4 Glossary — the technical vocabulary of this unit

Materials on record

The composite record before the Tribunal: the enquiry proceedings and evidence; any further evidence led before the Tribunal; and evidence led before it for the first time where there was no enquiry or a defective enquiry, together with the workman’s evidence contra. Not confined to the domestic enquiry record (Firestone, para 49).

Fresh evidence vs first-time evidence

Fresh evidence — evidence the Tribunal itself calls or permits at a stage beyond the proper record; prohibited by the proviso. First-time evidence — the employer’s or workman’s evidence led before the Tribunal where no enquiry/defective enquiry; permitted, because §11-A did not abrogate that pre-existing right.

Preponderance of probabilities

The standard of proof in disciplinary proceedings (as against “beyond reasonable doubt” in criminal law) — the reason an acquittal does not automatically vitiate a departmental finding, though it remains a material circumstance.

Victimisation / unfair labour practice

Pre-1971 grounds of interference and still an independent ground today: punishment imposed not for the misconduct but for union activity or personal animosity. “Harsh punishment suggesting victimisation” was Firestone’s principle 9.

Loss of confidence

The employer-side justification for dismissal in positions of trust: not the money value but the breach of trust is decisive (A.T. Mane). Use it when opposing a reduction in banking/theft/misappropriation cases.

Moulding the relief

The Tribunal’s practice — expressly recognised in Raghubir Singh and Ajaib Singh — of awarding reinstatement without back wages, or with back wages only from the date of the demand notice, so that the employer is not punished for the workman’s delay.

Judicial adventurism

Hombe Gowda’s warning against Tribunals ignoring binding precedent (Article 141) while exercising the §11-A discretion. Useful in questions on the High Court’s supervisory role.

Compensation in lieu of reinstatement

Firestone’s tenth principle (from Panitole Tea Estate): where the relationship has broken down or reinstatement is not practicable, the Tribunal may award compensation instead. A relief distinct from the “lesser punishment” clause.

G.5 Examiner’s traps — the eight mistakes that cost marks

Traps
  1. Retrospectivity reversed. §11-A is prospective — references on/after 15-12-1971; the date of the dismissal order is irrelevant.
  2. “Employer lost the right to lead evidence.” No — the right survives where the enquiry was absent or defective; it is the Tribunal’s own evidence-gathering that the proviso bars.
  3. “No enquiry → automatic reinstatement.” No — expressly rejected in Firestone (para 53) and Motipur.
  4. Unlimited power over quantum. No — “any lesser punishment”, but only for gross disproportionality and never on compassion (Hombe; Bharat Forge).
  5. Treating Scooters India and Hombe Gowda as contradictory. They are reconcilable by degree and reasons — see Part B.4.
  6. Calling Hombe Gowda an IDA case. It arose under the Karnataka Private Educational Institutions (Discipline and Control) Act, 1975; the jurisdiction was held to be “akin to” §11-A.
  7. Mislabelling the IRC provision. It is §50 (relief) and §56 (wages pending appeal) — the “§83” figure found in some notes is wrong.
  8. Forgetting the fate of the reference in Raghubir Singh. Delay is not a bar to a reference; it only reduces back wages to the date the dispute was raised (02.03.2005).

G.8 The last-minute one-page map

If you remember nothing else, remember this chain

Misconduct proved → Punishment imposed (dismissal/discharge) → Dispute raised (no limitation) → Reference / §53(6) application → Tribunal asks: “was it justified?” (may reappraise; deference to a proper enquiry but “very cogent reasons” needed to differ; the employer may lead first-time evidence if the enquiry was absent/defective; only “materials on record”) → If unjustified: set aside + reinstate on terms, or award any LESSER punishment proportionate to the misconduct (gross disproportionality alone justifies reduction — Hombe Gowda; reasoned reductions survive — Scooters India; excessive penalties fall — Raghubir Singh) → Award → If the employer appeals: full wages last drawn (§17-B/§56) → Back wages are the norm unless the employer pleads and proves gainful employment.

A closing note for the reader

This unit is the doctrinal centre of the industrial-law course: it is where the employer’s right to punish meets the adjudicator’s power to review, and where the constitutional standard of proportionality finally enters the workshop. The whole unit can be reduced to a single question the Tribunal must ask itself, twice — once about the misconduct (“was it proved, and fairly?”) and once about the punishment (“does it fit?”). Firestone gave the Tribunal the power to ask both; Hombe Gowda reminded it that the second question is not an invitation to substitute its own taste; Scooters India showed the power working well; and Raghubir Singh showed what happens when an employer ignores the question altogether.
