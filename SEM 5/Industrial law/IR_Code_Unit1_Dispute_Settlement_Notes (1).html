PART I · 1. Why a dispute-settlement law? Object, philosophy and design

Industrial peace is a pre-condition of production, and production is a pre-condition of the welfare State promised by Part IV of the Constitution. Every industrial-relations statute therefore tries to do three things: prevent disputes, settle them amicably when they arise, and adjudicate them by an impartial forum when settlement fails — while keeping the economic weapons of strike and lock-out under discipline. The Supreme Court captured the root of industrial conflict in Hariprasad Shivshankar Shukla v. A.D. Divekar, AIR 1957 SC 121: dissatisfaction with existing economic conditions is the root cause of industrial disputes; the history of labour struggle is a continuous demand for a fair return to labour (wage increase, resistance to wage cuts, allowances and benefits).

Long title of the IR Code, 2020

"An Act to consolidate and amend the laws relating to Trade Unions, conditions of employment in industrial establishment or undertaking, investigation and settlement of industrial disputes and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto." — It merges the Trade Unions Act 1926, the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act 1946 and the Industrial Disputes Act 1947.

Preamble of the repealed IDA, 1947

"An Act to make provision for the investigation and settlement of industrial disputes, and for certain other purposes." Unit 1 is the direct successor of IDA Chapters II (Authorities), III (Reference), IV (Procedure, powers and duties) and III-A/VI-VII (arbitration, penalties, miscellaneous). The philosophy is unchanged; the machinery is redesigned.

1.1 Five design principles you should be able to state

  1. Prevention before cure — bipartite forums inside the establishment (Works Committee s.3, Grievance Redressal Committee s.4, negotiating union/council s.14) are the first tier; the Code makes the GRC compulsory at 20 workers so that individual grievances are digested in-house.
  2. Conciliation is compulsory and universal — a conciliation officer "shall" hold conciliation proceedings in every existing or apprehended dispute and whenever a strike/lock-out notice is served (s.53(1)); the IDA's distinction between public-utility and other services for compulsory conciliation IDA s.12(1) proviso is gone.
  3. Adjudication by a specialised two-member tribunal — Labour Courts and Industrial Tribunals of the IDA are merged into the Industrial Tribunal (s.44) with a Judicial Member and an Administrative Member; disputes of national importance go to the National Industrial Tribunal (s.46). Boards of Conciliation and Courts of Inquiry IDA ss.5–6 are abolished.
  4. Direct access replaces Government reference — the IDA's gatekeeping "reference" IDA ss.10, 12(5) (the source of a huge litigation stream: K.P. Krishnan, Sarathy, Telco Convoy, TISCO) is replaced by a party's own application to the Tribunal within 90 days of the conciliation failure report (s.53(6)), and by the dismissed worker's direct route after 45 days (s.4(10)). The only surviving "reference" is the Central Government's reference to the NIT (s.54).
  5. No ordinary civil suit — s.97 bars civil courts from entertaining suits in respect of matters the Tribunal/NIT is empowered to decide; industrial adjudication was deliberately kept out of the ordinary courts (the Bharat Bank debate, Part VI). Only the constitutional courts (Arts. 32, 136, 226, 227) supervise.
Quotable

"The Industrial Disputes Act is intended to be a self-contained one and it seeks to achieve social justice on the basis of collective bargaining, conciliation and arbitration. Awards are given on circumstances peculiar to each dispute and the tribunals are, to a large extent, free from the restrictions of technical considerations imposed on the courts. A free and liberal exercise of the power under Article 136 may materially affect the fundamental basis of such decisions, namely, quick solution to such disputes to achieve industrial peace." — Bengal Chemical & Pharmaceutical Works Ltd. v. Their Workmen, AIR 1959 SC 633 (explaining the self-restraint after Bharat Bank). The same sentence, with "Code" substituted for "Act", is a perfect opening for any Unit 1 answer.

Art. 32Direct SC petition only for fundamental-rights violation; rarely used in industrial matters.
Hierarchy of review of an industrial adjudication under the Code
Conciliation Officer (s.43/53) — administrative; no appeal; judicial review only for jurisdictional/illegality issues (Art. 226); not a "tribunal" for Art. 136 (Jaswant Sugar Mills)
Arbitrator (s.42) / Industrial Tribunal (s.44) / NIT (s.46) — award final & binding (ss.55, 57); no statutory appeal; s.97 bars civil suits
High Court — Art. 226 (jurisdictional error, natural justice, error apparent on the face of the record) and Art. 227 (superintendence over tribunals)
Supreme Court — Art. 136 special leave (directly from the Tribunal award too: Bharat Bank) — exercised sparingly (Bengal Chemical)
Note the statutory "no appeal" design: finality (s.45 for constitution; s.55/57 for awards) + civil bar (s.97) → only constitutional remedies remain.

3. The IR Code, 2020 and the IR (Central) Rules, 2026 at a glance

Enactment
Passed by Parliament 22–23 Sept 2020; Presidential assent 28 Sept 2020; Act No. 35 of 2020; published in Gazette 29 Sept 2020.
Commencement
Brought into force (with the other three Labour Codes) w.e.f. 21 November 2025 (as recorded in the DU case material introduction). Different provisions may be notified on different dates (s.1(3)).
Repeals
Trade Unions Act 1926; Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act 1946; Industrial Disputes Act 1947 (s.104 repeal & savings; pending proceedings saved/transferred — s.51).
Structure
14 Chapters, 104 sections, 3 Schedules — First Schedule (matters for standing orders), Second Schedule (unfair labour practices), Third Schedule (conditions of service for change of which notice is required under s.40).
Central Rules
Industrial Relations (Central) Rules, 2026, notified by the Ministry of Labour & Employment vide G.S.R. 342(E) dated 8 May 2026 (draft G.S.R. 930(E), 30 Dec 2025); in force on publication; 50 rules + 16 Forms; supersede the Industrial Disputes (Central) Rules 1957 (Parts II, V–VIII) and the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Central Rules 1946. Apply in the Central sphere only (s.2(b)(i)); State Governments frame their own rules. (Per PRS's review, the Central Trade Union Regulations, 1938 continue to operate.)
Sister Codes
Code on Wages 2019; Code on Social Security 2020 (its Central Rules also notified May 2026); Occupational Safety, Health & Working Conditions Code 2020. Definitions of "wages" and "worker" are aligned across the Codes.

Example — which Rules apply?

A dispute between Punjab National Bank (banking company → Central Government is the appropriate Government under s.2(b)(i)) and its Delhi employees is governed by the Code plus the IR (Central) Rules, 2026: the conciliation officer will be the Assistant/Regional Labour Commissioner (Central), the strike notice goes in Form XI (R.25) and the Tribunal application in Form II (R.23(9)). A dispute in a private garment unit in Okhla is in the State sphere (Government of NCT of Delhi), so the Delhi IR Rules apply and the conciliation officer is the Delhi Labour Department's officer.

6. Architecture of the dispute-settlement machinery under the Code

Tier 1 · Bi-partite (in-house)

Works Committee s.3 (100+ workers, on Government order) — amity, day-to-day friction.
Grievance Redressal Committee s.4 (20+ workers, mandatory) — individual grievances.
Negotiating union/council s.14 — collective bargaining agent.
Output: agreement / settlement outside conciliation (s.2(zi) limb 2; s.57(1)).

Tier 2 · Conciliation

Conciliation Officer s.43; duties s.53; procedure R.23.
Mandatory on every dispute and every s.62 notice.
Output: settlement (binding on all — s.57(3)) or failure report (within 45/14 days).

Tier 3A · Voluntary arbitration

Written agreement (Form V) before the dispute reaches the Tribunal s.42; arbitrator(s)/umpire; Government notification if majority parties sign (s.42(5)).
Output: arbitration award = "award" (s.2(e)); enforceable 30 days after communication (s.55).

Tier 3B · Adjudication

Industrial Tribunal s.44 — application by party within 90 days of failure report s.53(6) (Form II) or by worker after 45 days s.4(10).
National Industrial Tribunal s.46 — on Central Government reference s.54.
Output: award (s.55), binding (s.57), period (s.58), execution (s.49(10)), recovery (s.59).

Tier 4 · Constitutional review

High Court Arts. 226/227; Supreme Court Art. 136 (Bharat Bank). No statutory appeal; civil suits barred (s.97).

PART II · 7. Works Committee — s.3 read with Rule 5 IDA s.3; Central Rules 1957, rr.38–57

Statute — s.3 (paraphrased sub-section-wise)

s.3(1) In an industrial establishment in which 100 or more workers are employed, or have been employed on any day in the preceding 12 months, the appropriate Government may, by general or special order, require the employer to constitute a Works Committee in the prescribed manner, consisting of representatives of the employer and of the workers engaged in the establishment.
Proviso: (i) the number of worker-representatives shall not be less than the number of employer-representatives; (ii) worker-representatives shall be chosen in the prescribed manner from among the workers of the establishment in consultation with their registered Trade Union (registered under s.9), if any.
s.3(2) Duty: to promote measures for securing and preserving amity and good relations between employer and workers and, to that end, to comment upon matters of their common interest or concern and endeavour to compose any material difference of opinion in respect of such matters.

Rule 5, IR (Central) Rules 2026 — constitution of Works Committee (Central sphere)
  • Size: total members not exceeding 20; employer's representatives not more than the workers' representatives; adequate representation of women workers (not less than their proportion in the workforce) — new.
  • Employer's representatives: nominated by the employer, as far as possible officials in direct touch with, or associated with, the working of the establishment.
  • Workers' representatives: chosen through the negotiating union or negotiating council (s.14) — where a council exists, seats are distributed in proportion to membership; where there is no negotiating union/council, by election (electronic voting permitted) among the workers.
  • Eligibility: candidate — a worker aged 19+ with one year's continuous service; voter — worker aged 18+ with six months' service. Nominations invited with at least 3 working days' time; poll held between 3 and 15 days after scrutiny; notice of election at least 7 days before.
  • Office-bearers: Chairperson — from employer's side (nominated by employer); Vice-Chairperson — elected by worker-members; a Secretary and a Joint Secretary, one from each side, alternating every year.
  • Term: 3 years; a member who absents himself from three consecutive meetings without leave forfeits membership; casual vacancies filled in the same manner for the residue of the term.
  • Meetings: at least once in three months; members are treated as on duty (paid) while attending; minutes recorded and displayed. The Government may dissolve a committee that is not constituted or functioning in accordance with the rules.

7.1 Object, philosophy and constitutional basis

The Works Committee is the first, purely bi-partite, forum. Its object lies in the principle that "prevention is better than cure": to catch friction at the shop-floor before it hardens into an industrial dispute, and to realise the constitutional goal of workers' participation in management (Art. 43A). It is a consultative, not a bargaining, body — it "comments" and "endeavours to compose"; it neither adjudicates nor can bind the workers on conditions of service. That limitation is the ratio of the leading case below.

Case — North Brook Jute Co. Ltd. v. Their Workmen, AIR 1960 SC 879
Facts
A jute company introduced a rationalisation scheme; the Works Committee, duly constituted, approved it. Workers resisted; a lock-out followed; the Tribunal awarded wages for the lock-out period. The employer argued before the Supreme Court that the workers must be deemed to have agreed because their representatives on the Works Committee had accepted the scheme.
Issue
Can a Works Committee's approval bind the workers on a change in conditions of service (rationalisation)?
Held
No. Worker-representatives on the Works Committee represent the workers only for the purposes of the Committee's functions; the Committee was not intended to supplant or supersede the unions for the purpose of collective bargaining; it is not entitled to consider real or substantial changes in conditions of service; its task is to smooth away frictions arising in day-to-day work.
Principle
WC = consultative amity forum; decisions are not "settlements" and do not bind workers on service conditions. Followed in M.D. Ramakrishnan v. T.N. Electricity Board, (1971) 1 LLJ 433 (Mad) — the WC's functions cannot include a decision on nationalisation-driven alteration of service conditions.
Supporting authorities
  • Kemp & Co. Ltd. v. Their Workmen, (1955) 1 LLJ 48 (LAT): recommendations of a Works Committee on which workers are not fairly represented are of no value; the committee's decision "carries great weight but is not conclusive"; constitution can be challenged if unfair.
  • Union of India v. M.T.S.S.D. Workers' Union (1988, as noted in Jain): where a union commands more than 50% of the workers it should be given representation in constituting the WC; where no union has a majority, representation in proportion to strength — the logic now codified through the negotiating union/council in R.5.

Real-world illustration

Large public-sector plants (e.g., steel plants of SAIL, ordnance factories, Indian Railways workshops) have run Works Committees for decades under Central Rules 38–57, typically discussing canteen quality, drinking water, safety shoes, shift-timing adjustments during festivals and grievance follow-ups. By contrast, the Maruti Suzuki Manesar unrest (July 2012) is frequently cited in industrial-relations literature as a failure of in-house dialogue: the immediate trigger was a shop-floor disciplinary incident and grievances over contract labour and union recognition that had no trusted bipartite channel. Under the Code, such a plant (well above 100 workers) would ordinarily have a Works Committee (on Government order), a compulsory GRC (s.4) and a defined negotiating union/council (s.14), precisely to institutionalise that channel.

Critique you can write (5 marks)

Works Committees have remained "largely ornamental" (Mendiratta/Jain): (i) constitution depends on a Government order — not automatic; (ii) functions are vague and recommendations non-binding; (iii) unions see them as rivals and starve them; (iv) no sanction for non-implementation; (v) the Code has not addressed the Second National Commission on Labour's (2002) suggestion to give them a defined agenda. The only "weapon" is persuasion — which is also their virtue.

8. Grievance Redressal Committee — s.4 read with Rules 6–8 IDA s.9C (2010); s.2A

Statute — s.4 (paraphrased sub-section-wise)

(1) Every industrial establishment employing 20 or more workers shall have one or more GRCs for resolution of disputes arising out of individual grievances (mandatory — "shall"; no Government order needed).
(2) Equal number of members representing the employer and the workers, chosen in the prescribed manner.
(3) Chairperson selected from the employer's and the workers' representatives alternately on rotation every year.
(4) Total members not exceeding 10; adequate representation of women workers — not less than their proportion to total workers.
(5) An aggrieved worker may file an application before the GRC in the prescribed manner within one year from the date on which the cause of action arises.
(6) The GRC may complete its proceedings within 30 days of receipt of the application.

(9) Where an employer discharges, dismisses, retrenches or otherwise terminates an individual worker, any dispute between that worker and the employer connected with or arising out of such termination is deemed to be an industrial dispute notwithstanding that no other worker or union is a party IDA s.2A(1).

(11) Such application must be made before the expiry of two years from the date of discharge/dismissal/retrenchment/termination IDA s.2A(3) — three years.

9. Negotiating union / negotiating council — s.14 and Rule 9 (the bargaining tier that feeds Unit 1)

Although s.14 sits in Chapter III (Trade Unions), it is the engine of bi-partite settlement and the electoral base for WC/GRC worker representatives, so a short account is essential.

Statute — s.14 (essentials)
  • Where only one registered Trade Union exists in an establishment, the employer shall recognise it as the sole negotiating union.
  • Where more than one: the union with 51% or more of the workers on the muster roll as members (verified) is the sole negotiating union; otherwise the employer constitutes a negotiating council of representatives of unions having at least 20% membership, one seat per 20%.
  • Recognition operates for the prescribed period (three years, extendable) ; matters negotiable are as prescribed; facilities to the negotiating union/council as prescribed.
Rule 9 (Central) — verification of membership, negotiable matters, facilities, check-off; R.9(8): disputes about the negotiating union/council go to the Tribunal (Form II) within one year — read with s.22 (Tribunal's jurisdiction over Trade Union disputes).
Example

In a Central PSU with three unions holding 45%, 30% and 15% membership, none reaches 51%; the employer constitutes a negotiating council with the first two unions (2 seats and 1 seat respectively — one per 20%); the 15% union gets no seat. Worker representatives on the WC and GRC are then chosen through this council in proportion (Rules 5–6). A wage agreement signed by the council outside conciliation is a settlement under s.2(zi) limb 2 binding on the parties (s.57(1)); if signed in conciliation, it binds all workers, present and future (s.57(3)).

PART III · 10. Conciliation — Conciliation Officers (s.43), duties (s.53), procedure (Rule 23), powers (s.49) IDA ss.4, 11, 12

10.1 What conciliation is

"To conciliate" is to bring together, to overcome distrust or hostility by pacifying means. In industrial relations, conciliation is the intervention of a neutral third person — the Conciliation Officer — who does not decide the dispute but investigates it, brings the parties to the table, narrows differences and induces a fair and amicable settlement. It is the bridge between collective bargaining (Tier 1) and adjudication (Tier 3): under the Code, no dispute can reach the Industrial Tribunal (other than through the s.4(10) window or the NIT reference under s.54) without first passing through conciliation and ending in a failure report (s.53(6)). Conciliation may be voluntary (parties invite the officer) or compulsory (statute obliges the officer to intervene and the parties to appear; economic weapons are frozen during pendency). The Code has made it compulsory in every case.

Real example — how conciliation actually works in the banking sector

Bank employees' unions (UFBU) periodically serve strike notices on the Indian Banks' Association over wage revision or outsourcing. Because banking is in the Central sphere, the Chief Labour Commissioner (Central) immediately takes up conciliation (now compulsory under s.53(1)); several nationwide bank strikes have been averted or shortened through such conciliation sittings in New Delhi. Contrast: a settlement signed before the CLC(C) in the course of conciliation binds every worker of the establishment, member or not, and even future recruits (s.57(3)(d)).

PART IV · 12. Voluntary arbitration — Chapter VI, s.42; Rules 20–22; Forms V–VI IDA s.10A (inserted 1956); Central Rule 7, Form C

12.1 Concept and place in the scheme

Arbitration is the determination of a dispute by one or more persons chosen by the parties themselves. In industrial law it is voluntary in origin (the parties must agree) but statutory in effect (the award is an "award" under s.2(e), enforceable and binding like a Tribunal award, and the machinery of the Code — not the Arbitration & Conciliation Act, 1996 — governs it). It is India's constitutional and policy preference: the Code of Discipline in Industry (1958) adopted by the Indian Labour Conference committed employers and unions to prefer voluntary arbitration; a National Arbitration Promotion Board was set up in 1967; the Second National Commission on Labour (2002) again urged its promotion. Yet in practice fewer than a few per cent of disputes go to arbitration — the reasons are examined at the end of this Part.

Statute — s.42 Voluntary reference of disputes to arbitration (sub-section-wise)

(1) Where any industrial dispute exists or is apprehended and the employer and the workers agree to refer it to arbitration, they may, at any time before the dispute has been referred to/ is pending before a Tribunal or NIT (i.e., before an application under s.53(6)/s.4(10) or a reference under s.54), by a written agreement, refer the dispute to arbitration; the arbitration agreement must specify the arbitrator(s) — who may include a Judicial/Administrative Member (presiding officer) of a Tribunal or NIT.
(2) Where the agreement provides for an even number of arbitrators, it shall also provide for an umpire who decides if the arbitrators are equally divided; the umpire's award is deemed the arbitration award.
(3) The agreement shall be in the prescribed form (Form V), signed by the parties in the prescribed manner (R.20).
(4) A copy of the agreement shall be forwarded to the appropriate Government and to the conciliation officer; the Government shall, within the prescribed period, publish it (notification — under the 2026 Rules, on the Ministry's website/portal, R.21).
(5) Where the Government is satisfied that the persons making the reference represent the majority of each party, it may, within the prescribed time, issue a notification; thereupon employers and workers who are not parties but are concerned in the dispute are given an opportunity of presenting their case before the arbitrator(s).
(6) The arbitrator(s) shall investigate the dispute and submit to the Government the arbitration award signed by the arbitrator(s) (the award is then communicated and becomes enforceable under s.55).
(7) Where a notification under sub-s.(5) has been issued, the Government may by order prohibit the continuance of any strike or lock-out in connection with the dispute existing on the date of the reference (see also s.62(1)(f)).
(8) Nothing in the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 shall apply to arbitrations under this section. IDA s.10A(5) excluded the Arbitration Act 1940.

12.3 Case law on s.10A/s.42 arbitration (with DU material emphasis)

Case (DU material, Unit 3) — Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. Rohtas Industries Staff Union, AIR 1976 SC 425; 1976 SCR (3) 12 (Krishna Iyer, Chandrachud & Gupta JJ.)
Facts
Two connected managements at Dalmianagar had a long-running dispute marked by inter-union rivalry (Mazdoor Sangh v. Seva Sangh). A strike ended by a memorandum of agreement dated 2 October 1957, reached during conciliation; clause 7 referred two questions to joint arbitrators (two retired Calcutta High Court Judges, J.N. Majumdar and R.C. Mitter) under s.10A: (a) workers' claim to wages for the strike period and (b) the company's claim to compensation for losses caused by the strike. After hearings spread over 18 months the arbitrators (1959) held the strike illegal, denied strike-period wages, and directed the striking workers to pay compensation for loss of profits (₹6,90,000 in one case, ₹80,000 in the other) plus one-eighth of the costs.
Procedural history
The Mazdoor Sangh filed writ petitions under Art. 226; the Patna High Court quashed the compensation part. Managements appealed by special leave (Art. 136). Both sides accepted the denial of wages.
Issues
(1) Is an award under s.10A a "private arbitration" immune from Art. 226? (2) Even if amenable, should the High Court have exercised restraint? (3) Was there an error of law apparent on the face of the award? (4) Is a management's claim for compensation an "industrial dispute" arbitrable under the Act?
Arguments
Managements: s.10A award savours of private arbitration; not a "tribunal" for Art. 136 (Engineering Mazdoor Sabha), so no certiorari; even otherwise, discretionary restraint; error not apparent; the compensation was recoverable as damages for the tort of conspiracy by an illegal strike. Union: the arbitrator under s.10A is a statutory tribunal; the award is enforceable through State machinery; error of law is patent; an illegal strike does not per se give rise to liability in damages; the compensation claim is not an industrial dispute.
Held
(i) Art. 226 is "in a sense wider than Art. 136" — it runs against "any person or authority"; the s.10A arbitrator, though perhaps not a "tribunal" under Art. 136 (as held in Engineering Mazdoor Sabha), is a quasi-statutory body whose award derives force from the statute (after the 1964 amendments to ss.10A, 17, 17A, 18, 23 the award is published, binding and enforceable like a Tribunal award, strikes are barred during arbitration); it therefore falls "within the rainbow of statutory tribunals amenable to Art. 226". (ii) An award may be quashed for an error of law apparent on the face of the award (the Champsey Bhara test — the error must appear in the award or a document incorporated in it). (iii) The arbitrators' proposition that an illegal strike is per se the tort of conspiracy entitling the employer to loss of profits was "an unhappy error of law — loudly obtrusive on the face of the award"; the tort of conspiracy requires predominant intent to injure, not merely an illegal strike pursued for the workers' own interests. (iv) The compensation claim was a consequence of the wages dispute and hence connected with the industrial dispute — arbitrable — but the award on it was bad in law. Appeals dismissed.
Principle
Awards under s.10A (now s.42) are subject to judicial review under Art. 226 for jurisdictional error and error of law apparent on the face; an illegal strike does not by itself make strikers liable in damages for loss of profits. Krishna Iyer J.'s prefatory lament about "dawdling dysfunction" — a 1948 dispute decided in 1976 — is the best-known critique of the system's delays.
Engineering Mazdoor Sabha v. Hind Cycles Ltd., AIR 1963 SC 874 (Const. Bench)

An arbitrator appointed under s.10A is not a "tribunal" within Art. 136: his authority flows from the parties' agreement rather than from the State's investiture of judicial power, though he is a "statutory arbitrator" with quasi-judicial functions; the Court held (per Gajendragadkar J.) that his award may nonetheless be examined under Art. 227 (and Art. 226) by the High Court. This is the case that made the Art. 136 door doubtful for s.10A awards; Rohtas (1976) noted the doubt for Art. 136 while affirming Art. 226; Gujarat Steel Tubes (1980) effectively reopened Art. 136 after the 1964 amendments.

Gujarat Steel Tubes Ltd. v. Gujarat Steel Tubes Mazdoor Sabha, AIR 1980 SC 1896 (3-Judge Bench, Krishna Iyer J.)

Mass dismissal of about 853 workers after a strike; dispute referred to an arbitrator under s.10A who upheld the dismissals; the High Court declined to interfere. The Supreme Court held: (i) after the 1964 amendment an arbitrator under s.10A is a statutory arbitrator whose award has all the trappings of an award, and the Court may entertain an appeal under Art. 136 from it (the majority distinguished Engineering Mazdoor Sabha); (ii) the arbitrator has the same powers as a Tribunal under s.11A (now s.50) to examine the punishment and grant reinstatement — since s.11A's "arbitrator" was expressly included; (iii) a strike, even if illegal, does not automatically justify dismissal; punishment must be proportionate; reinstatement ordered with partial back wages. It remains the leading authority for the proposition that the s.42 arbitrator is a limb of the statutory adjudicatory system — and that the Bharat Bank test (State-constituted body with judicial functions and trappings) is now satisfied.

Karnal Leather Karamchari Sanghatan v. Liberty Footwear Co., AIR 1990 SC 247
Facts
A committee of arbitrators under s.10A directed reinstatement of certain workmen; the management did not comply and challenged the award on the ground that the arbitration agreement had not been published in the Gazette as required by s.10A(3).
Held
Publication of the arbitration agreement under s.10A(3) is mandatory, not directory, and non-publication invalidates the award. Reasons: the Act seeks social justice through collective bargaining; voluntary arbitration is part of the infrastructure of dispensation of justice; workers must know what is referred and who the arbitrator is, so as to be able to participate (especially given the s.10A(3A) opportunity to non-signatories); the absence of a penalty for non-compliance does not make the provision directory. Also held: ss.10 and 10A are alternative remedies — once the parties choose arbitration, the Government cannot refer the same dispute for adjudication.
Under the Code
s.42(4) + R.21 — publication is now on the Ministry's website within the prescribed period; the mandatory character should carry over (the purpose — notice to all workers — is identical).
Other points on the arbitrator's powers
  • Rajinder Kumar Kindra v. Delhi Administration, (1984) 2 LLJ 517 (SC): an arbitrator under s.10A may re-appraise the evidence led in a domestic inquiry and interfere with perverse findings.
  • FACT Employees' Assn. v. Management, FACT Ltd., (1983) 1 LLJ 240: an arbitrator cannot alter a service condition fixed by statute; only the legislature can.
  • Rule 28 of the 1957 Central Rules (now the corresponding 2026 rule) allows an arbitrator/Tribunal to correct clerical or accidental slips in an award; an arbitrator has no power of review beyond that.
  • An arbitrator's award must be reasoned — the "speaking award" norm of "processual justice" (Rohtas).
Real examples of industrial arbitration in India
  • Ahmedabad textile industry: since the 1918 mill strike led by Gandhiji, the Textile Labour Association and the Ahmedabad Millowners' Association resolved wage and bonus disputes through a standing arbitration arrangement — the model that inspired s.10A (1956) and the Code of Discipline (1958).
  • Rohtas Industries (1957–59) above — two retired High Court Judges as joint arbitrators under a conciliation settlement.
  • Public-sector practice: many PSU/bank wage disputes name a serving or retired Judge or a senior Labour Commissioner as arbitrator; under s.42(1) even a sitting Judicial Member of the Industrial Tribunal may be named.
Why voluntary arbitration remains under-used (write as a 5-mark critique)
  1. Both sides must agree — employers fear "legislative" awards; unions fear pro-management arbitrators.
  2. No panel of trained, credible arbitrators; costs borne by parties (Tribunal is free).
  3. Absence of appeal cuts both ways.
  4. Rivalry among unions (as in Rohtas) makes majority representation for a s.42(5) notification hard to establish.
  5. The compulsory conciliation-to-Tribunal pipeline (s.53(6)) is now quick to access, reducing the incentive to opt out.

PART V · 13. Adjudication — the Industrial Tribunal (ss.44, 45, 47, 48, 51, 52) IDA ss.7, 7A, 7C, 8, 9

Adjudication is the compulsory, binding, third-party determination of an industrial dispute by a statutory tribunal. Unlike the civil court, the industrial tribunal is not confined to enforcing existing contractual rights: it can create new rights and obligations (revise wages, rewrite conditions of service, order reinstatement) "in accordance with social justice" — which is why Bharat Bank had to decide whether such a body is a "tribunal" exercising the State's judicial power at all. The Code re-engineers the IDA's three-tier structure (Labour Court / Industrial Tribunal / National Tribunal, with jurisdiction divided by the Second and Third Schedules) into a single Industrial Tribunal with a Judicial and an Administrative Member, plus a National Industrial Tribunal for disputes of national importance.

Statute — s.44 Industrial Tribunal

(1) The appropriate Government may, by notification, constitute one or more Industrial Tribunals for the adjudication of industrial disputes and for performing such other functions as may be assigned to them under the Code (e.g., s.22 Trade Union disputes; s.59(2) computation; s.91 complaints; s.95 interpretation; standing-order appeals in some sets of rules).
(2) Every Tribunal consists of two members appointed by the appropriate Government — one Judicial Member and one Administrative Member.
(3)–(4) Qualifications: Judicial Member — is or has been a Judge of a High Court, or has been a District Judge / Additional District Judge for the prescribed period (three years under the Code). Administrative Member — a senior administrator with labour experience: an officer who has held a post not below Joint Secretary to the Government of India (or equivalent State rank), or a senior labour official (e.g., Deputy Chief Labour Commissioner (Central)/Joint Labour Commissioner of a State) with a law degree and substantial experience in labour matters, as prescribed. The qualifications, appointment, term of office, salaries and allowances, resignation, removal and other conditions of service of the members are governed by s.184 of the Finance Act, 2017 (i.e., by the Tribunal (Conditions of Service) Rules made thereunder, as amended by the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021).
(5) The appropriate Government specifies the area/class of disputes over which each Tribunal has jurisdiction; the Judicial Member presides.
(6)–(7) Benches: the Government may notify that certain matters be heard by both members and others by a single member. The Code itself requires the two-member bench for: (a) application and interpretation of standing orders; (b) discharge or dismissal of workers including reinstatement/relief; (c) legality of a strike or lock-out; (d) retrenchment and closure; (e) Trade Union disputes (s.22) — i.e., the old Second-Schedule "rights" disputes and the gravest Third-Schedule items. Other matters may be heard by the Judicial Member or the Administrative Member sitting singly, as notified.
(8) Where the members differ, see s.47.
(9) The Industrial Tribunal constituted by the Central Government also exercises the powers of the Employees' Provident Funds Appellate Tribunal under s.7D of the EPF & MP Act, 1952 (that Tribunal stands merged).

Statute — s.45 Finality of constitution; s.47 Decision; s.48 Disqualifications; s.51 Transfer; s.52 Adjustment

s.45 IDA s.9 — No order of the Government appointing a member of a Tribunal/NIT shall be called in question in any manner, and no act or proceeding before a Tribunal/NIT shall be called in question merely on the ground of the existence of any vacancy in, or defect in the constitution of the Tribunal/NIT. (This is an ordinary finality clause: it does not cure a tribunal that is acting without jurisdiction or in breach of a mandatory procedural requirement — cf. Bharat Bank ground 4 where the award signed by two of three members was held bad by Mahajan J.; and State of Haryana v. Haryana Co-operative Transport Ltd., AIR 1977 SC 237: the High Court may still examine the validity of the presiding officer's appointment under Arts. 226/227.)
s.47 — Where a matter is heard by both members, they decide together; if they differ on any point, they state the point(s) of difference and refer them to the appropriate Government (Central Government for NIT), which refers the point(s) to the Judicial Member of another Tribunal/NIT; the point is then decided according to the majority of the members who have heard it, including those who first heard it. (Contrast Bharat Bank's three-member tribunal that "adjusted" its difference internally.)
s.48 IDA s.7C — No person shall be appointed to, or continue in, the office of member of a Tribunal/NIT if (a) he is not an independent person (not connected with the industry or the dispute), or (b) he has attained the age of 65 years. (Independence + judicial background = "assurance of independence" that is unique — Statesman (P) Ltd. v. H.R. Deb, AIR 1968 SC 1495, which also held that a magistrate "holds a judicial office".)
s.51 — All cases pending before Labour Courts, Industrial Tribunals and National Tribunals under the IDA immediately before the commencement of the Code stand transferred to the corresponding Tribunal/NIT under the Code, which may proceed de novo or from the stage reached, as it deems fit.
s.52 — Presiding officers of IDA Labour Courts/Tribunals are adjusted (continue) as Judicial Members of the Code's Tribunals on the prescribed terms, ensuring continuity of adjudication.

Related law — s.184 Finance Act 2017; Tribunals Reforms Act 2021; Madras Bar Association line of cases

Section 184 of the Finance Act, 2017 empowered the Central Government to prescribe by rules the qualifications, appointment, tenure, salaries and removal of members of 19 (now more) central tribunals, including the industrial tribunals. The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 fixed a four-year tenure, a minimum age of 50, age limits of 67 (members)/70 (chairpersons), and a Search-cum-Selection Committee headed by the CJI or his nominee. In Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (2020–2021, incl. (2021) 7 SCC 369 and the July 2021 judgment) the Supreme Court struck down or read down provisions (e.g., the 4-year tenure/50-year minimum age) as violating judicial independence; Parliament re-enacted several of them. For the exam: (i) the independence and security of tenure of Tribunal members is a constitutional requirement flowing from Bharat Bank's own logic — they exercise the State's judicial power; (ii) the Administrative Member innovation has been criticised as diluting the "judicial" character that Bharat Bank stressed, though the Judicial Member presides and the two-member bench is reserved for rights disputes.

14. National Industrial Tribunal — ss.46 and 54 IDA ss.7B, 10(1A), 10(6)

Statute — s.46 Constitution; s.54 Reference and functions

s.46(1) The Central Government may, by notification, constitute one or more National Industrial Tribunals for the adjudication of industrial disputes which, in its opinion, involve questions of national importance or are of such a nature that industrial establishments situated in more than one State are likely to be interested in or affected by them. (2) Two members appointed by the Central Government — a Judicial Member who is or has been a Judge of a High Court, and an Administrative Member of the rank of Secretary to the Government of India (or equivalent) with experience in labour matters; conditions of service under s.184 Finance Act 2017; s.48 disqualifications and s.47 procedure on difference apply.
s.54(1) The Central Government may, by notification, refer to a NIT any industrial dispute of the above description, whether or not it is the appropriate Government in relation to that dispute (the only surviving Government "reference"). (2) On such reference, no Tribunal shall have jurisdiction over the matter; proceedings pending before a Tribunal on that matter are deemed quashed, and it is not lawful for the appropriate Government to entertain/permit any application on that matter while the NIT is seised. (3) The NIT holds its proceedings expeditiously and submits its award to the Central Government (then s.55 applies — enforceable 30 days after communication; the Central Government may exercise the s.55(3)–(5) power to declare it unenforceable on public grounds and reject/modify within 90 days, laying it before Parliament).

Leading case on the NIT reference power — FDC Ltd. v. Union of India, 136 (2007) DLT 266 (Del)

A reference of 58 individual medical-representative terminations spread across States to a National Tribunal at Calcutta was quashed: individual fact-bound dismissals are not a "question of national importance"; "establishments in more than one State" means different establishments, not one employer's branches; the NIT is not immune from Arts. 226/227. Real example — the bank-award tradition

The wage structure of Indian bank employees was built by all-India adjudication: the Sastry Award (1953) of the All-India Industrial Tribunal (Bank Disputes) — the very Tribunal whose award was challenged in Bharat Bank (1950) — and the Desai Award (1962) of the National Industrial Tribunal (Bank Disputes) presided over by Justice K.T. Desai, constituted under s.7B after the 1956 amendment because the dispute affected banks in every State. Since 1966 the parties have preferred bipartite settlements (now the 12th, 2024), illustrating the policy hierarchy: settlement first, adjudication last.

How s.50 (ex-s.11A) operates — Workmen of Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Management, AIR 1973 SC 1227 (principles, as summarised in the DU/Cornell material)
  1. Before s.11A, the Tribunal could interfere with a dismissal after a fair domestic inquiry only for want of good faith, victimisation/unfair labour practice, violation of natural justice, or a perverse finding (Indian Iron & Steel Co. v. Workmen, AIR 1958 SC 130). s.11A/s.50 confers a wider jurisdiction: the Tribunal may re-appraise the evidence, disagree with the employer's findings on guilt, and substitute the punishment.
  2. Where the inquiry is defective or none was held, the employer may lead evidence before the Tribunal to justify the dismissal (the proviso to "materials on record" includes such evidence adduced before the Tribunal).
  3. The Tribunal may impose a lesser punishment (e.g., stoppage of increments, withholding back wages) — the doctrine of proportionality in industrial law.
  4. Reinstatement is not automatic: loss of confidence (Binny), closure, superannuation, or long lapse of time may justify compensation instead.

16. Awards — form, communication, enforceability, binding effect, period, recovery (ss.55–59) IDA ss.15–19, 33C

Statute — s.55 Form of award, its communication and commencement

(1) Every arbitration award and every award of a Tribunal/NIT shall be in writing and signed by the arbitrator(s) / all members of the Tribunal or NIT (cf. Bharat Bank ground 4 under IDA s.16).
(2) Every such award shall be communicated to the parties concerned and to the appropriate Government (Central Government for NIT/central arbitration) in the prescribed manner (portal/e-mail — R.46–47). IDA s.17 required publication in the Official Gazette within 30 days — held directory in Remington Rand of India v. Workmen, AIR 1968 SC 224.
(3) An award becomes enforceable on the expiry of 30 days from the date of its communication. Proviso: where (a) the appropriate Government is a party to a dispute decided by a Tribunal, or (b) the award is one of a NIT, and the Government is of opinion that it would be inexpedient on public grounds affecting national economy or social justice to give effect to the whole or part of the award, it may by notification declare that the award shall not become enforceable on the expiry of 30 days.
(4) After such declaration the Government may, within 90 days of communication, make an order rejecting or modifying the award and shall lay the award with the order before the State Legislature/Parliament at the first available opportunity.
(5) An award so rejected/modified becomes enforceable 15 days after it is laid; where no order is made within 90 days, the award becomes enforceable on expiry of the 90 days.
(6) Subject to the above, the award comes into operation from the date specified in it, or, if none, from the date it becomes enforceable.

Statute — s.57 Persons on whom settlements and awards are binding IDA s.18

(1) A settlement arrived at otherwise than in conciliation binds the parties to the agreement.
(2) An arbitration award that has become enforceable binds the parties to the arbitration agreement.
(3) A settlement in conciliation, an arbitration award where a s.42(5) notification was issued, and an award of a Tribunal/NIT that has become enforceable bind: (a) all parties to the dispute; (b) all other parties summoned to appear (unless the adjudicator records that they were summoned without proper cause); (c) where a party is an employer — his heirs, successors or assigns in respect of the establishment; (d) where a party is composed of workers — all persons employed in the establishment (or the part concerned) on the date of the dispute and all who are subsequently employed there.

Statute — s.58 Period of operation IDA s.19

(1)–(2) Settlement: operates from the agreed date or from signature; binding for the agreed period or 6 months; continues thereafter until 60 days after a written notice of intention to terminate is given by one party to the other. (3) Award: remains in operation for 1 year from the date it becomes enforceable; Government may reduce the period, or extend it by up to 1 year at a time, subject to a maximum of 3 years. (4) If since the award there has been a material change in circumstances, the Government (suo motu or on application) may refer the award to the Tribunal/NIT to decide whether the period should be shortened; that decision is final. (5) Sub-s.(3) does not apply to an award that imposes no continuing obligation once implemented. (6) Even after expiry, the award continues to bind until 60 days after notice of termination by a party. (7) A notice of termination is effective only if given by a party representing the majority of persons bound. Case law carried forward: after termination the award's terms survive as implied terms of the contract until replaced by a fresh settlement/award (South Indian Bank v. A.R. Chacko, AIR 1964 SC 1522; Life Insurance Corporation v. D.J. Bahadur, AIR 1980 SC 2181).

Statute — s.59 Recovery of money due from employer IDA s.33C

(1) Money due under a settlement, award or Chapters IX–X → application (by the worker, his authorised person, or heirs) to the appropriate Government within 1 year (condonable) → certificate to the Collector → recovery as arrears of land revenue (Forms VII–VIII, R.24). (2) Where a question arises as to the amount of money or the money-value of a benefit → decided by the Tribunal specified by the Government within 3 months (extendable for reasons) (Forms IX–X). (3) Tribunal may appoint a Commissioner to compute (R.48–50). (4) Decision forwarded to Government for recovery under (1). (5) A single application may be made for several workers. Principle: the s.33C(2)/s.59(2) forum is executory — it can compute an existing right but cannot adjudicate a disputed entitlement that requires a fresh award (Central Bank of India v. P.S. Rajagopalan, AIR 1964 SC 743; Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Ganesh Razak, (1995) 1 SCC 235).

PART VI · 18. Judicial review of industrial adjudication — Articles 136, 226 and 227

The Code, like the IDA, provides no appeal from an award. Finality is deliberate (speed, industrial peace, specialised justice). But finality cannot immunise a tribunal that exceeds its jurisdiction or denies a fair hearing. Hence the perennial question — first answered in Bharat Bank (1950), the very first special-leave petition against an Industrial Tribunal award under the new Constitution — of which constitutional remedy lies and how far it goes.

Article 136 — text

"(1) Notwithstanding anything in this Chapter, the Supreme Court may, in its discretion, grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India. (2) Nothing in clause (1) shall apply to any judgment, determination, sentence or order passed or made by any court or tribunal constituted by or under any law relating to the Armed Forces."

18.1 The test of a "tribunal" for Art. 136 (as developed from Bharat Bank onwards)

Is body X a "tribunal" whose "determination" can be appealed under Art. 136?
1. Is it constituted by the State (statute/Government), deriving authority from the sovereign — not merely from the parties' agreement?
2. Is it invested with any part of the judicial functions of the State — deciding a lis between parties by ascertaining facts on evidence and applying law/norms, with a binding result?
3. Does it have the "trappings of a court" — procedure with pleadings, evidence on oath, cross-examination, civil-court powers, penalties for false evidence, binding award enforceable by State machinery?
It is a "tribunal": Industrial Tribunal/NIT (Bharat Bank; Bengal Chemical), Election Tribunal (Durga Shankar Mehta, 1954), Central Government under the Companies Act (Harinagar Sugar Mills, 1961), Speaker under the Tenth Schedule (Kihoto Hollohan, 1992), statutory arbitrator under s.10A/s.42 after 1964 (Gujarat Steel Tubes, 1980; contra Engineering Mazdoor Sabha, 1963)
Even where the answer is "yes", leave is discretionary: the Court is "not a court of error"; it interferes for excess of jurisdiction, violation of natural justice, arbitrary or despotic procedure, error of law of general importance, or grave injustice — and ordinarily expects the High Court route (Art. 226/227) to be used first.

No; it strengthens. 19. Case 1 (DU material) — Bharat Bank Ltd., Delhi v. Employees of the Bharat Bank Ltd., 1950 SCR 459 : AIR 1950 SC 188

Court, bench and opinions

Supreme Court of India, Constitution Bench of five — Kania C.J., Fazl Ali, Patanjali Sastri, Mahajan and B.K. Mukherjea JJ.; decided 26 May 1950. Civil Appeal No. XXXIV of 1950, by special leave, from the award of the All-India Industrial Tribunal (Bank Disputes). Counsel: Dr Bakshi Tek Chand (with Veda Vyas and S.K. Kapur) for the Bank; B. Sen for the employees; Alladi Krishnaswami Aiyar (with Jindra Lal) for the Union of India. The DU case material reproduces Mahajan J.'s opinion (paras 22–67). On jurisdiction: Kania C.J., Fazl Ali and Mahajan JJ. (majority) held that Art. 136 extends to an Industrial Tribunal's award; Mukherjea and Patanjali Sastri JJ. dissented. On the merits: Mahajan J. would have quashed the award; Kania C.J., Fazl Ali, Patanjali Sastri and Mukherjea JJ. found no ground to admit/allow the appeal — so the appeal was dismissed although the jurisdictional point was decided in the Bank's favour. (Students who read only the DU extract often miss this; state it and you will stand out.)

Facts
Bharat Bank Ltd., Delhi, a company under the Indian Companies Act, received demands from its employees; on an unfavourable response the employees struck work on 9 March 1949 (there had been an earlier strike in December 1948 whose dispute was already pending before a Tribunal). The Bank served notices to resume work and, on their failure, discharged a number of employees between 19 and 24 March 1949. The Central Government, under Ordinance VI of 1949 read with s.7 of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, constituted a three-member Tribunal for adjudication of industrial disputes in banking companies, and under s.10 referred the disputes listed in Schedule II of the notification. Item 18 read: "Retrenchment and victimization (specific cases to be cited by employees)." The Tribunal heard the Bharat Bank dispute at Delhi. At the first sitting (Rule 17 of the 1947 Rules) the parties merely stated their cases — Mr Parwana for the employees (not on oath, no affidavit, no cross-examination, only an abstract of correspondence), Mr Ved Vyas for the Bank — the Bank filed a lengthy affidavit disputing the facts; no evidence was recorded; arguments followed. Meanwhile, in November 1949 one member (Mr Chandrasekhara Aiyar) was appointed to the Bengal Boundary Commission; the other two telegraphed the Labour Ministry to fill the vacancy or reconstitute the Tribunal; the Ministry advised them to proceed; the Tribunal was never reconstituted. The award dated 19 January 1950, directing reinstatement of the discharged employees, was signed by only two of the three members; it was published in the Gazette of India on 4 February 1950 and declared binding for one year. The Bank obtained special leave under Art. 136 — the first such case.
Issues
(1) Jurisdiction: Is an Industrial Tribunal a "tribunal" within Art. 136, and is its award a "determination" — i.e., is the word "tribunal" used in the same sense as "court" or in a wider sense? (2) If yes, what are the limits of the Court's power under Art. 136 over such awards? (3) Merits: was the award vitiated by (a) misconstruction of "victimization", (b) reinstatement of participants in an illegal strike (s.23(b)), (c) absence of any evidence / breach of natural justice, (d) signature by two of three members (ss.8, 16)?
Arguments — respondents & Union of India (Alladi Krishnaswami Aiyar)
(i) "Tribunal" in Art. 136 must be read ejusdem generis with "court": the words "judgment, decree, sentence, order" connote courts exercising the judicial power of the State; an Industrial Tribunal decides on considerations of social justice and expediency, may create new contracts, and is not bound by law — it is administrative/quasi-judicial, like the Australian arbitration courts discussed in Shell Co. of Australia v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation, (1931) AC 275; (ii) the award is a "lifeless" document until the Government declares it binding under s.15(2) and may be sent to the Legislature (s.15(3)) — it is not an enforceable "determination"; (iii) the appropriate remedy for excess of jurisdiction is certiorari under Art. 226, not appeal; (iv) allowing appeals would destroy the speed and finality on which industrial peace depends.
Arguments — appellant Bank (Dr Bakshi Tek Chand)
(i) Art. 136 uses "tribunal" in addition to "court" deliberately — otherwise it is redundant — so it covers bodies that are not courts but determine matters judicially; (ii) the Industrial Tribunal has judicial members, follows a judicial procedure under the Act and Rules, decides a lis between parties, and the Government cannot alter its award (s.15(2) is mandatory except where Government is a party); (iii) "determination" is the apt word for such a body's decision; (iv) on merits, the four grounds above — especially that the award rested on no evidence and was signed by only two members.
Decision on jurisdiction (Mahajan J., with Kania C.J. and Fazl Ali J.)
  • Art. 136 is of the widest amplitude and overriding ("notwithstanding anything in this Chapter"): unlike Arts. 132–134 it is not confined to final decisions of High Courts; it reaches any court or tribunal and even interlocutory orders. It confers a discretionary power, not a right of appeal.
  • "Tribunal" is wider than "court". Drawing on Halsbury, R. v. London County Council, (1931) 2 KB 215 (Scrutton L.J. — printed as "Saville L.J." in the DU extract: it is enough if the body has to decide rightly after hearing evidence and opposition, without being strictly a court), Shell Co. (Lord Sankey's list of what does not make a body a court), Huddart, Parker & Co. v. Moorehead, 8 CLR 330 (Griffith C.J.'s definition of judicial power), and Cooper v. Wilson, (1937) 2 KB 309 (the four requisites of a true judicial decision), the Court held that a "tribunal" for Art. 136 is a body constituted by the State and invested with some of the judicial functions of the State — one "adorned with similar trappings as a court" though not strictly a court. Bodies that discharge purely administrative or executive duties, or that derive authority only from the parties' agreement (private arbitrators), are excluded.
  • The Industrial Tribunal satisfies the test: s.7 requires adjudication of disputes in accordance with the Act; presiding officers are judicial persons; s.11(3) gives civil-court powers (attendance, oath, documents, commissions); proceedings are judicial proceedings for ss.193 and 228 IPC; the 1947 Rules (rr. 3, 13, 14, 17, 19, 21, 30) require pleadings, evidence, examination and cross-examination, notice to parties by name and a reasoned award; s.29 penalises breach of awards. It decides a lis between two or more parties. It has the first three Cooper v. Wilson requisites fully and a considerable element of the fourth.
  • Power to make new contracts does not make it non-judicial: following Western India Automobile Assn. v. Industrial Tribunal, Bombay, (1949) FCR 321, an industrial tribunal may impose new obligations, but it must do so within the statute and on judicial lines — the legislature has entrusted this jurisdiction to it.
  • The "lifeless award" argument rejected: the s.15(2) declaration is a mandatory, ministerial act (Government "shall" declare the award binding); relying on Rex v. Electricity Commissioners, (1924) 1 KB 171 (a body's decision does not cease to be judicial because confirmation by another authority is needed) and Rex v. Minister of Health (1929), and on the analogy of the Army Act (courts-martial decisions require confirmation yet Art. 136(2) had to expressly exclude military tribunals — implying that such tribunals would otherwise fall within Art. 136). Where Government is a party its overriding powers presuppose a valid determination.
  • Art. 226 does not oust Art. 136: the availability of certiorari from the High Court does not circumscribe the Supreme Court's power; indeed the High Court's decision could itself come up in appeal, and in industrial matters "expedition is the crux", so abuse of power should be corrected quickly.
  • It is not an arbitration tribunal: the parties do not choose it; the State constitutes it and its award binds by force of statute.
Scope of Art. 136 (the self-limiting principle)
"This Court under Article 136 would not constitute itself into a mere court of error. Extraordinary powers have to be exercised in rare and exceptional cases and on well-known principles." The Court will not substitute its own decision; it interferes only where the Tribunal has exceeded its jurisdiction, approached the questions in a manner likely to result in injustice, or adopted a procedure contrary to natural justice — denial of hearing, refusal to record evidence, arbitrary or despotic conduct — and then it quashes the award and directs the Tribunal to proceed according to law. Kania C.J.: the Court "will be very reluctant to entertain such an application".
Decision on merits (Mahajan J.)
  1. "Victimization" ground — rejected. Whether "victimization" in item 18 bears its ordinary meaning (unjustly dealt with) or a technical trade-union meaning is not a matter for Art. 136; "this Court is not a mere court of error", and the award was not materially affected.
  2. Illegal strike ground (s.23(b)) — rejected. Even if the March 1949 strike during the pendency of the earlier reference was illegal, the Act prescribes penalties for illegal strikes but nowhere says such persons cannot be reinstated; the Government referred victimisation arising out of that very strike; the Tribunal was bound to decide item 18.
  3. No evidence / natural justice — upheld. The onus of proving victimisation lay on the employees; Mr Parwana's statement was not on oath, was not tested by cross-examination and was unsupported by affidavit; the only evidence on record was the Bank's affidavit, which the Tribunal ignored. "The Act as well as the Rules … contemplate a proper hearing, discovery and inspection of documents and production of evidence." An award on no evidence is against all principles of natural justice. "It happens that when the safeguard of an appeal is not provided by law the tendency sometimes is to act in an arbitrary manner like a benevolent despot. Benevolent despotism, however, is foreign to a democratic Constitution."
  4. Two of three members — upheld. s.16 (award "shall be signed by all the members", with liberty to record a dissent) is mandatory; all three heard the case; s.8 required the Government to reconstitute the Tribunal when a member's services ceased to be available, which was never done; "the idea of three persons hearing a case and two of them deciding it is repugnant to all notions of fairness" — the third member's view might have altered the result (the two who signed had in fact differed on a question of law). The award of two was therefore not the award of the Tribunal constituted by the Government.
Mahajan J. would quash the award and direct the still-functioning Tribunal to re-adjudicate item 18. But the majority (Kania C.J., Fazl Ali, Patanjali Sastri, Mukherjea JJ.) held this was not a fit case for interference and dismissed the appeal; Kania C.J. noted the aggrieved party could seek "other appropriate proceedings" (i.e., Art. 226).
The dissent (Mukherjea J., Patanjali Sastri J. concurring)
An Industrial Tribunal is not a judicial tribunal: it does not administer the law of the land but decides on grounds of social and economic policy and may create new rights — a function more legislative/administrative than judicial; the "materials and considerations" on which it decides are such that an appellate court cannot effectively review them; therefore its determinations are outside Art. 136. Patanjali Sastri J. added that the award lacks binding force until the Government's declaration. Both agreed that even if jurisdiction existed this was not a fit case. (Mukherjea J. nevertheless accepted that the Tribunal "functions judicially" in procedure — the germ of the later quasi-judicial characterisation; the Capsule summary notes his emphasis on its wider powers such as reinstatement.)
Principles evolved
  1. "Tribunal" in Art. 136 ≠ "court": it means any State-constituted body invested with part of the State's judicial power, having the trappings of a court — a functional test, later refined in Associated Cement Cos. v. P.N. Sharma, AIR 1965 SC 1595 (the "inherent judicial power of the State" test) and applied to election tribunals, the Speaker (Tenth Schedule), the Central Government under the Companies Act, etc.
  2. An Industrial Tribunal's award is a "determination" appealable by special leave; the Government's post-award powers do not change its character.
  3. Art. 136 is discretionary and exceptional — not a court of error; grounds: jurisdiction, natural justice, arbitrariness, grave injustice (developed in Bengal Chemical, 1959; Wenger, 1964; Kamani Metals, 1967).
  4. Industrial adjudication must rest on evidence and a fair hearing; an award on no evidence is a nullity — the origin of the "no evidence" ground in Indian administrative law.
  5. A multi-member tribunal must decide as constituted: all members who heard must sign (now s.55(1) "signed by all the members"); a vacancy must be filled/reconstituted (now s.47 mechanism on difference; s.45 finality does not cure want of jurisdiction) — see also United Commercial Bank Ltd. v. Their Workmen, AIR 1951 SC 230 (award by a tribunal whose vacancy was filled without proper reconstitution held invalid, prompting amendment of s.8).
  6. Availability of Art. 226 does not bar Art. 136; in industrial matters expedition is vital.
Later treatment
Bengal Chemical (1959) and Hindustan Antibiotics v. Workmen, AIR 1967 SC 948: the Court is not a regular court of appeal from tribunals; Wenger & Co. v. Workmen, AIR 1964 SC 864: it decides general questions of law to guide industrial adjudication, not facts; Kamani Metals & Alloys v. Workmen, AIR 1967 SC 1175: interference only for excess of jurisdiction, substantial error in applying settled principles, or gross and palpable error causing substantial injustice; Jaswant Sugar Mills (1963): conciliation officer is not a tribunal; Engineering Mazdoor Sabha (1963): s.10A arbitrator not a tribunal (Art. 227 lies); Rohtas (1976) and Gujarat Steel Tubes (1980): statutory arbitrator amenable to Art. 226 and (after 1964) Art. 136. The Court's practice today: SLPs directly from Tribunal awards are discouraged; the party must ordinarily go to the High Court first.
How to write Bharat Bank in the exam (structure for a 15–20 mark answer)
  1. Frame: first SLP against an Industrial Tribunal award; Art. 136 text; two issues.
  2. Facts in 5 lines (strike 9 Mar 1949 → discharges → 3-member Tribunal, item 18 → award 19 Jan 1950 signed by 2 → SLP).
  3. Preliminary objection and both sides' arguments (Alladi v. Bakshi Tek Chand).
  4. Majority reasoning: wide amplitude; tribunal ≠ court; State-constituted + judicial functions + trappings (list them); s.15(2) ministerial; Army Act/Art. 136(2) analogy; Art. 226 no bar.
  5. Limits: not a court of error; grounds of interference.
  6. Merits: four grounds; "benevolent despotism"; "three hearing, two deciding" — and the actual outcome (appeal dismissed 4:1 on merits).
  7. Dissent (Mukherjea/Patanjali Sastri) in 4 lines.
  8. Principle + later cases + application to the Code (ss.44–49, 55, 97) — conclude that the Code's Tribunal is a fortiori a tribunal.

20. Other cases you must be able to cite (compact briefs, grouped by theme)

20.1 Scope of Art. 136 after Bharat Bank

Bengal Chemical & Pharmaceutical Works Ltd. v. Their Workmen, AIR 1959 SC 633

Facts: Employer sought special leave against a Tribunal's award on wage scales/dearness allowance. Held: Art. 136 confers no right of appeal; it is a discretionary power that cannot be construed to give a party a right he does not otherwise have; the IDA is self-contained and aims at social justice through collective bargaining, conciliation and arbitration; awards turn on circumstances peculiar to each dispute. The Court will interfere only where there is a violation of natural justice causing substantial and grave injustice, an important principle of industrial law requiring elucidation, or circumstances that otherwise merit its consideration. Principle: the "sparing exercise" doctrine — the necessary counterweight to Bharat Bank.

Wenger & Co. v. Workmen, AIR 1964 SC 864; Hindustan Antibiotics Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1967 SC 948; Kamani Metals & Alloys Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1967 SC 1175

The Supreme Court in an Art. 136 appeal from an industrial award does not act as a regular court of appeal on facts; it decides general questions of law to guide industrial adjudication; an award cannot be assailed merely because another adjudicator might have decided differently or elaborate reasons were not given; interference requires excess of jurisdiction, a substantial error in applying settled principles, or a gross and palpable error causing substantial injustice.

Associated Cement Companies Ltd. v. P.N. Sharma, AIR 1965 SC 1595

Refined the Bharat Bank test: the basic test of a "tribunal" is whether the body is invested with the inherent judicial power of the State to deal with disputes between parties and determine them on the merits fairly and objectively; the trappings of a court are helpful indicators but not conclusive. Applied to hold the State Government acting under the Punjab Welfare Officers Recruitment Rules a tribunal.

Jaswant Sugar Mills Ltd. v. Lakshmi Chand, AIR 1963 SC 677

Facts: Conciliation officer refused permission under s.33 to dismiss workmen; employer sought special leave. Held: though the officer must act judicially in deciding the s.33 application, he is not invested with the State's judicial power to adjudicate rights with finality; he is an administrative authority; no appeal lies under Art. 136 (relief lies under Art. 226/227). Principle: Conciliation Officer ≠ tribunal.

20.2 Government reference and its judicial review (IDA era — now confined to s.54 NIT references, but still examined)

State of Bombay v. K.P. Krishnan, AIR 1960 SC 1223 (Const. Bench, Gajendragadkar J.)
Facts
Workmen of Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. (respondents) claimed bonus; conciliation failed; the Bombay Government refused to refer the dispute under s.12(5), giving as its reason that the workmen had resorted to go-slow during 1952–53. The workmen sought mandamus.
Held
(i) Under s.12(5) the Government must consider the failure report but is not confined to it; its power under s.10(1) is administrative and discretionary, yet it must be exercised on relevant considerations germane to the dispute; (ii) if the reasons given are extraneous, irrelevant or not germane, or amount to adjudicating the merits, the High Court can issue mandamus directing the Government to reconsider/refer; (iii) punishing workmen for go-slow by refusing a reference on an unrelated bonus claim was an extraneous consideration; direction to reconsider upheld.
Principle
Judicial review of the refusal to refer for extraneous reasons; the Government cannot decide the merits. Later: Ram Avtar Sharma v. State of Haryana, AIR 1985 SC 915 (refusal on merits quashed); Telco Convoy Drivers (1989); Sultan Singh v. State of Haryana, (1996) 2 SCC 66 (no hearing required before refusal; fresh reference possible on fresh material — also Western India Match Co. v. Workers' Union, AIR 1970 SC 1205, "at any time"); Secretary, Indian Tea Assn. v. Ajit Kumar Barat, (2000) 3 SCC 93 (prima facie view permissible, but no adjudication).
State of Madras v. C.P. Sarathy, AIR 1953 SC 53

The order of reference under s.10 is an administrative act; the Government's opinion that a dispute exists or is apprehended is subjective and need not be supported by reasons or a quasi-judicial inquiry; the Tribunal cannot go behind the reference to question the Government's satisfaction, but it can decide whether the dispute referred is in fact an industrial dispute. A general reference in respect of an industry (cinema theatres in Madras) was upheld.

Telco Convoy Drivers Mazdoor Sangh v. State of Bihar, AIR 1989 SC 1565 (DU material, Unit 2)

Facts: Union of about 750 convoy drivers engaged through contractors claimed they were Telco's workmen; the State Government refused to refer, holding that there was no employer–employee relationship. Held: in deciding whether to refer, the Government cannot adjudicate the dispute itself; whether the drivers were workmen of Telco was the very dispute, to be decided by the Tribunal; the refusal was quashed and the Government directed to refer. Principle: the Government's function under ss.10/12(5) is administrative; it may not usurp the Tribunal's role. Under the Code the point recurs in a different form: the Tribunal decides the threshold question on the party's own application; the Central Government under s.54 must equally refrain from prejudging.

Tata Iron & Steel Co. Ltd. v. State of Jharkhand, (2014) 1 SCC 536 (DU material, Unit 2)

A reference framed in language that presupposed the answer (assuming the workmen's entitlement) was quashed as prejudging the dispute; the Government was directed to make a fresh, neutrally worded reference. Principle: the referring authority (and, by analogy, a party drafting Form II) must frame the dispute without prejudging; the Tribunal decides.

Sharad Kumar v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi, AIR 2002 SC 1724 (DU material, Unit 2)

The Government cannot refuse a reference by itself deciding that the employee was not a "workman" (a managerial post) — a disputed, mixed question of fact and law for the Tribunal; refusal quashed. Under the Code the same question is decided by the Tribunal on the s.53(6)/s.4(10) application.

FDC Ltd. v. Union of India, 136 (2007) DLT 266 : (2007) 2 LLJ 413 (Del) (Shiv Narayan Dhingra J.) — DU material notes: "Section 54, IR Code pari materia with s.7B IDA"
Facts
By notification dated 22 May 2001 the Ministry of Labour, under s.7B IDA, constituted a National Tribunal at Calcutta and referred to it the question whether FDC Ltd.'s termination of 58 medical representatives (dismissed between 1997 and 1999 in different States for different alleged misconducts) was legal and justified. FDC challenged the notification under Art. 226.
Arguments — FDC
Each MR's case rested on individual facts, witnesses and records spread across the country; many had already raised disputes before State Labour Courts/Tribunals (some decided); of the 58, 24 had raised no dispute, 4 had resigned, 2 were still employed, 15 were probationers/trainees — hence non-application of mind; a dispute is not of "national importance" merely because one establishment's employees work in several States; s.7B requires a rational, demonstrable satisfaction; the reference was mala fide and prejudicial.
Arguments — Union of India
Terminations without charge-sheets/retrenchment compensation would send wrong signals in the vital pharmaceutical industry; a single forum would harmonise potentially conflicting State awards; the establishment operated in more than one State, satisfying s.7B; the National Tribunal (a High Court Judge) was beyond Arts. 226/227; venue could be shifted for convenience.
Held
(i) The pre-requisites of s.7B are: a dispute involving a question of national importance, or one in which industrial establishments in more than one State are likely to be interested/affected, and the Central Government's opinion to that effect. (ii) A cluster of individual termination disputes requiring separate fact-finding for each worker is not a single dispute of national importance; national importance connotes an important question of law affecting workmen or employers in general across India (e.g., whether hospitals or research institutes are "industries") or the fate of large classes of workers. (iii) "Industrial establishments situated in more than one State" means different establishments (of the same or diverse nature) — not the branches of one establishment in several States. (iv) Dragging a Chandigarh MR to Calcutta makes justice more distant and expensive; there was no application of mind. (v) The plea that a National Tribunal has High Court status and is immune from Arts. 226/227 is wrong: National and State Tribunals have the same status and both are amenable to writ jurisdiction. Notification quashed.
Principle (for s.54 of the Code)
The Central Government's power to refer to the NIT is conditioned on a genuine, reasoned opinion about the nature of the dispute; it is judicially reviewable for non-application of mind and misreading of the statutory conditions; multi-State presence of a single employer is not enough; the NIT is not above the High Courts.

20.3 Tribunal's jurisdiction, procedure and awards

Delhi Cloth & General Mills Co. Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1967 SC 469

The Tribunal must confine itself to the points referred and matters incidental thereto (s.10(4)); where the reference was whether the "lock-out" was justified, the Tribunal could consider the employer's plea that the establishment had in fact been closed (closure v. lock-out) as incidental. Basis of the May–June 2025 Q.4 problem.

Sirsilk Ltd. v. Government of Andhra Pradesh, AIR 1964 SC 160

Facts: After the Tribunal sent its award to the Government but before publication under s.17, the parties reached a settlement and jointly asked the Government not to publish; the Government said publication was mandatory. Held: s.17(1) is mandatory and the Government cannot ordinarily withhold publication; but where a binding settlement under s.18(1) is reached before publication, publishing the award would create a conflict between two binding instruments and defeat the Act's object of industrial peace; in such a case the Government should withhold publication and the settlement prevails. Under the Code: there is no Gazette publication; the award is communicated (s.55(2)) and becomes enforceable after 30 days. See PYQ Nov–Dec 2025 Q.8(c) for the Code-era reasoning.

Remington Rand of India Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1968 SC 224

The 30-day period in s.17(1) for publication of the award is directory; publication after 30 days (44 days in a Cornell practice problem) does not invalidate the award, because the provision fixes a duty on the Government whose breach should not prejudice the parties. Under the Code the corresponding duty is prompt communication under s.55(2) and Rules 46–47.

State of Bihar v. Kripa Shankar Jaiswal, AIR 1961 SC 304

Conciliation officer's entry without notice does not affect jurisdiction; the 14-day period for the report is directory; a settlement/reference is not vitiated by delay in the report.

Premier Automobiles Ltd. v. Kamlekar Shantaram Wadke, AIR 1975 SC 2238

Four propositions on civil-court jurisdiction: (1) a dispute that is not an industrial dispute and involves only common-law/contractual rights → civil court; (2) an industrial dispute involving general-law rights → civil court or the IDA forum; (3) a dispute about rights/obligations created by the IDA → only the IDA machinery; (4) a right created by the Act and enforceable under it cannot be pursued in a civil court. Now largely codified by s.97 of the Code.

Statesman (P) Ltd. v. H.R. Deb, AIR 1968 SC 1495; State of Haryana v. Haryana Co-operative Transport Ltd., AIR 1977 SC 237; United Commercial Bank Ltd. v. Their Workmen, AIR 1951 SC 230

Appointment and constitution of tribunals: judicial background gives an assurance of independence; "holding a judicial office" includes a magistrate; despite the finality clause (s.9 → s.45) the High Court may examine the validity of a presiding officer's appointment; an award by an improperly reconstituted tribunal is without jurisdiction (the sequel to Bharat Bank's ground 4).

Citation discipline

In a Code-based answer cite the Code section first, then the IDA section in brackets, then the case: e.g., "Under s.53(6) [IDA s.12(5)] the failure report now entitles the party to move the Tribunal directly; the K.P. Krishnan–Telco Convoy controversy over Government refusal is therefore confined to s.54 references." Examiners reward this translation more than a recital of old sections.

Dec 2024 · Q.1 · 15 marks (problem)
Sheetal, employed for eight years as a "senior sales executive" in a pharmaceutical company, was dismissed after a domestic inquiry for alleged misconduct. Her union raised a dispute; conciliation failed. The appropriate Government declined to refer the dispute, recording that (i) Sheetal, drawing ₹45,000 p.m. and supervising two junior salesmen, was not a "workman", and (ii) the inquiry appeared fair, so "no useful purpose would be served". Advise Sheetal on her remedies. Would your answer differ under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020?

Issues

  1. Can the appropriate Government, while deciding whether to refer under IDA ss.10(1)/12(5), decide that Sheetal is not a "workman" or that the inquiry was fair?
  2. What is the remedy against such a refusal?
  3. How is the same fact-situation processed under the Code?

Law and application — IDA position (the frame of the question and of the DU cases)

Under s.12(5) the Government, on receiving the conciliation failure report, "may" refer the dispute if satisfied that there is a case for reference, and if it does not refer it must record and communicate reasons. The power is administrative (State of Madras v. C.P. Sarathy, AIR 1953 SC 53): the Government forms a prima facie opinion whether an industrial dispute exists or is apprehended; it may decline a reference that is frivolous, stale or patently outside the Act (Bombay Union of Journalists v. State of Bombay, AIR 1964 SC 1617). But it cannot adjudicate the merits or decide disputed questions of fact and law that are the very subject of the dispute. Whether a person is a "workman" (here, whether a sales executive supervising two juniors is employed in a supervisory/managerial capacity — cf. Sharad Kumar v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi, AIR 2002 SC 1724) and whether a domestic inquiry was fair (a matter for the Tribunal under s.11A — Firestone) are exactly such questions. Telco Convoy Drivers Mazdoor Sangh v. State of Bihar, AIR 1989 SC 1565, quashed a refusal based on the Government's own finding that no employer–employee relationship existed; Ram Avtar Sharma v. State of Haryana, AIR 1985 SC 915, quashed a refusal that decided the merits of a termination; State of Bombay v. K.P. Krishnan, AIR 1960 SC 1223, held that a refusal on extraneous or non-germane grounds is amenable to mandamus under Art. 226 directing reconsideration. Ground (ii) ("inquiry appeared fair, no useful purpose") is a merits determination; ground (i) is a disputed mixed question reserved for the Tribunal — note also that Sheetal is a sales promotion employee, expressly included in the definition of worker/workman by the Sales Promotion Employees (Conditions of Service) Act, 1976, and that mere supervision of two juniors does not make her managerial (the nature of the principal duties test — Ananda Bazar Patrika v. Workmen, AIR 1970 SC 1760).

Remedy (IDA): a writ petition under Art. 226 for certiorari quashing the refusal and mandamus directing the Government to reconsider/refer (K.P. Krishnan; Telco Convoy). The Court will not itself adjudicate whether she is a workman; it will send the matter for reference. Alternatively, because her dispute concerns dismissal, she could (after the 2010 amendment) apply directly to the Labour Court under s.2A(2) after 45 days of her conciliation application, within three years (s.2A(3)) — bypassing the Government altogether. No SLP lies directly against the Government's refusal (it is not a "tribunal" — Bharat Bank test; Jaswant Sugar Mills), though an SLP lies against the High Court's judgment.

Position under the IR Code, 2020 — the answer changes materially

  1. No Government reference exists for Tribunal adjudication. Sheetal's dispute is a deemed industrial dispute under s.2(q) read with s.4(9) (dismissal of an individual worker). Her pathway: GRC (s.4(5), within one year) → conciliation through her union (s.4(8), 60 days; s.53) → on failure, she or the union applies to the Industrial Tribunal in Form II within 90 days of the failure report (s.53(6); R.23(9)), or, without waiting for the report, directly after 45 days from her conciliation application (s.4(10)), subject to the two-year outer limit from dismissal (s.4(11)).
  2. The threshold question — is she a "worker" under s.2(zr) (sales promotion employees are expressly included; the exclusion is for managerial/administrative employees and supervisors drawing above ₹18,000) — is decided by the Tribunal itself (two-member bench, since it is a dismissal case — s.44). Her s.50 remedies (reinstatement, back wages, lesser punishment) follow if the dismissal is unjustified; the Tribunal relies on the record and may examine the fairness of the inquiry.
  3. The Government's opinion on "usefulness" is legally irrelevant; the only surviving reference power is the Central Government's power to refer disputes of national importance to the NIT (s.54), which is not attracted and which, in any event, is reviewable for non-application of mind (FDC Ltd. v. Union of India, 2007).

Conclusion

Under the IDA, advise a writ under Art. 226 (mandamus to reconsider the reference) or the s.2A(2) direct route; under the Code, advise a direct Form II application to the Industrial Tribunal (s.4(10)/s.53(6)) within the 2-year/90-day limits — the K.P. Krishnan–Telco problem no longer arises because the gatekeeper has been removed. Add that pending the challenge she should seek interim relief under s.50 and, if reinstated and the employer appeals, full wages under s.56.

Dec 2024 · Q.4(a) · 10 marks
Explain the conciliation machinery for the settlement of industrial disputes. What is the legal effect of a settlement arrived at in conciliation and of a failure of conciliation? (Answer with reference to the IR Code, 2020 and the 2026 Rules.)

Introduction. Conciliation is the statutory mediation of an industrial dispute by a Government-appointed Conciliation Officer (CO) who investigates, persuades and records — but does not decide. It is the compulsory middle tier of the Code's machinery between bi-partite forums (ss.3–4, 14) and adjudication (ss.44–46). Under the IDA it was compulsory only in public utility services on a strike notice (s.12(1)); under s.53(1) it is compulsory in every case.

Machinery. (1) Appointment — s.43 IDA s.4: appropriate Government appoints COs for areas/industries, permanently or temporarily (CLC(C) organisation in the Central sphere). (2) Trigger — s.53(1): an existing or apprehended dispute, or a strike/lock-out notice under s.62; also the GRC route for individual grievances (s.4(8), R.8); proviso — no conciliation after two years from the dispute's arising. (3) Duties — s.53(2): investigate without delay all matters affecting the merits and right settlement; do all things to induce a fair and amicable settlement; s.53(3): on settlement, send a report with the signed memorandum (Form I, R.4); s.53(4): on failure, send a full report of steps, facts and reasons; s.53(5): within 45 days (14 days where a s.62 notice) unless extended by written agreement. (4) Powers — s.49(1), (2), (6), (7): own procedure; entry after notice; compel attendance and documents; public servant; s.61 confidentiality; no lawyers (s.94(3)). (5) Procedure — R.23: portal registration, notices, written statements, joint/separate sittings (video-conferencing permitted), affidavits, failure report uploaded within 7 days. (6) Deemed dates — s.60: commencement on receipt of notice/taking up; conclusion on signing of settlement or receipt of the failure report. During pendency: strikes/lock-outs barred (+7 days) (s.62(1)(d)); conditions of service frozen (s.90).

Legal effect of a settlement in conciliation. It is a "settlement" under s.2(zi) limb 1; binds all parties to the dispute, summoned parties, the employer's successors and all present and future workers of the establishment (s.57(3)) — unlike a private settlement which binds only signatories (s.57(1)); operates for the agreed period or 6 months and thereafter until 60 days after notice by a majority party (s.58(1)–(2), (7)); a strike on covered matters is illegal (s.62(1)(g)); breach is an offence (s.86). Courts treat it as standing "on a higher plane" and presume it fair unless mala fide or unconscionable (Royal Calcutta Golf Club Mazdoor Union, 1957; Herbertsons, 1977; K.C.P. Ltd., 1996). A settlement reached after an award is made but before it takes effect prevails over the award (Sirsilk, 1964).

Legal effect of failure. The CO has no power to pass any order (Sasamusa Sugar Works; Security Paper Mill v. R.S. Sharma); his report is not a "determination" (Jaswant Sugar Mills). Under the IDA the report went to the Government which decided whether to refer (s.12(5); K.P. Krishnan). Under the Code the failure report is the jurisdictional key: within 90 days of it any party may apply to the Industrial Tribunal (s.53(6), Form II); the prohibition on strike/lock-out continues for 7 days and then revives if a Tribunal application is filed (60 days after conclusion of adjudication); delay in the report does not vitiate proceedings (Kripa Shankar Jaiswal).

Evaluation. Advantages — informal, quick, cheap, relationship-preserving; limitations — no power to impose, overloaded officers, ritualised "failure". The Code strengthens conciliation by making it universal, time-bound and electronically tracked, and by giving the failure report direct legal consequences.

Dec 2024 · Q.4(b) · 10 marks
"An award of a voluntary arbitrator under the industrial law is not beyond the reach of judicial review." Discuss the grounds and the constitutional provisions under which such an award may be challenged.

Nature of the arbitrator. Under s.42 IDA s.10A the parties choose the arbitrator, but the statute supplies the agreement's form (Form V), requires publication (R.21), allows a s.42(5) notification binding non-signatories, bars strikes during arbitration (s.62(1)(f)), makes the award an "award" (s.2(e)) enforceable after 30 days (s.55), binding (s.57(2)–(3)), executable as a decree (s.49(10)) and penal on breach (s.86), and excludes the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (s.42(8)). The arbitrator is therefore a statutory (quasi-statutory) tribunal, not a private arbitrator.

Constitutional routes. (1) Art. 226: Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. Rohtas Industries Staff Union, AIR 1976 SC 425 (DU material) — certiorari runs against "any person or authority"; a s.10A award, being the decision of a quasi-statutory body enforceable by State machinery, is "within the rainbow of statutory tribunals amenable to Art. 226"; the compensation award was quashed for an error of law apparent on its face. (2) Art. 227: Engineering Mazdoor Sabha v. Hind Cycles, AIR 1963 SC 874 — even though the s.10A arbitrator was held not to be a "tribunal" for Art. 136, the High Court's supervisory jurisdiction extends to him. (3) Art. 136: after the 1964 amendments, Gujarat Steel Tubes v. Mazdoor Sabha, AIR 1980 SC 1896, treated the s.10A arbitrator as a statutory tribunal whose award can be appealed by special leave and who possesses s.11A (now s.50) powers; Bharat Bank's test (State-constituted body exercising judicial functions with trappings) is satisfied once the statute clothes the award with binding force. No statutory appeal, review or s.34-type petition exists.

Grounds. (a) Error of law apparent on the face of the award (the Champsey Bhara test applied in Rohtas — e.g., holding that an illegal strike is per se the tort of conspiracy); (b) want/excess of jurisdiction — dispute not covered by the agreement, non-existent "industrial dispute", agreement not validly made/published (Karnal Leather Karamchari Sanghatan v. Liberty Footwear, AIR 1990 SC 247: non-publication of the agreement is fatal), arbitrator altering a statutorily fixed condition (FACT Employees' Assn.); (c) violation of natural justice — no hearing, no evidence, bias (Bharat Bank principle that an award on no evidence is a nullity applies); (d) perversity/no evidence; (e) misconduct or mala fides; (f) failure to give reasons (a "speaking award" is a norm of processual justice — Rohtas). The court does not re-appreciate evidence or sit in appeal on proportionality unless shocking.

Conclusion. The statement is correct: finality under the Code (no appeal; s.97 civil bar) coexists with constitutional review; the award of a s.42 arbitrator is reviewable under Arts. 226/227 (High Court) and, per Gujarat Steel Tubes, Art. 136 — though the Supreme Court will insist on the High Court route first and will interfere only on the recognised grounds (Bengal Chemical).

May–June 2025 · Q.1 · 15 marks
"The Industrial Tribunal is not a court, yet its award is a 'determination' by a 'tribunal' within Article 136 of the Constitution." Critically examine with reference to Bharat Bank Ltd. v. Employees. Has the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 altered the position?

Framework. Art. 136(1) empowers the Supreme Court, in its discretion, to grant special leave from any "judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order" of "any court or tribunal"; Art. 136(2) excludes Armed Forces tribunals. Two words carry the debate — "tribunal" and "determination".

Bharat Bank (1950 SCR 459). [Facts in five lines — strike of 9 March 1949, discharges, three-member Tribunal under s.7, item 18 "retrenchment and victimization", award of 19 January 1950 signed by two members, SLP.] Preliminary objection (Alladi Krishnaswami Aiyar for the Union): tribunal = court; the Tribunal decides on social justice and can make new contracts, so it does not exercise the judicial power of the State; the award is "lifeless" until the Government's s.15(2) declaration; remedy is Art. 226. Majority (Kania C.J., Fazl Ali, Mahajan JJ.): (1) Art. 136 is of the widest amplitude and overriding; (2) "tribunal" would be redundant if it meant "court"; it denotes a body constituted by the State and invested with some of the State's judicial functions, having the trappings of a court (R. v. LCC; Shell Co.; Huddart Parker; Cooper v. Wilson); (3) the Industrial Tribunal satisfies the test — it adjudicates a lis per the Act (s.7), has judicial members, civil-court powers (s.11(3)), penalties for false evidence (ss.193/228 IPC), rules requiring pleadings, evidence and cross-examination, and a binding award (s.29); (4) the power to create new obligations (Western India Automobile Assn.) does not make it non-judicial; (5) the s.15(2) declaration is ministerial (Rex v. Electricity Commissioners; Army Act/Art. 136(2) analogy); (6) Art. 226 does not oust Art. 136. Limits: not a court of error; interference only for excess of jurisdiction, breach of natural justice or arbitrary procedure — quash and remit. Dissent (Mukherjea, Patanjali Sastri JJ.): the Tribunal decides by policy, not law; its determinations are unsuited to appellate review; not within Art. 136. Outcome: jurisdiction upheld 3:2; on merits, only Mahajan J. would have quashed (no evidence — "benevolent despotism is foreign to a democratic Constitution"; two of three members signing); the appeal was dismissed.

Critical examination. (i) The functional test proved durable — applied to election tribunals (Durga Shankar Mehta, 1954), the Central Government under the Companies Act (Harinagar Sugar Mills, 1961), the Speaker (Kihoto Hollohan, 1992) and refined as the "inherent judicial power of the State" test (Associated Cement v. P.N. Sharma, 1965). (ii) The dissent's fear of appellate flooding was answered not by denying jurisdiction but by self-restraint: Bengal Chemical (1959), Wenger (1964), Kamani Metals (1967) — the Court decides general questions of law, not facts, and ordinarily insists on the Art. 226/227 route first. (iii) The test excludes purely administrative authorities — the Conciliation Officer (Jaswant Sugar Mills, 1963) and, historically, the Government deciding whether to refer — and originally excluded consensual arbitrators (Engineering Mazdoor Sabha, 1963) until the statute clothed their awards with binding force (Gujarat Steel Tubes, 1980). (iv) Bharat Bank's merits discussion supplied enduring procedural norms: decision on evidence, notice by name, all members to sign, no "three hearing and two deciding".

Under the IR Code, 2020 the position is reinforced, not altered. The Industrial Tribunal (s.44) and NIT (s.46) are State-constituted with a Judicial Member (HC/District Judge) presiding; s.48 secures independence; s.49(3) confers CPC powers and deems proceedings judicial for IPC ss.193/228 (BNS 229/267); s.49(9) contempt powers; s.49(10) execution as a civil decree; s.55 makes the award enforceable by efflux of 30 days without any Government declaration (the Government's public-grounds power exists only where it is a party or for NIT awards) — answering Patanjali Sastri J.'s "lifeless award" point; s.45 finality and s.97 civil-court bar make constitutional review the only corrective; s.2(e) itself calls the award a "determination". Even the s.42 arbitrator's award is an "award" enforceable and executable under the Code, so Gujarat Steel Tubes applies a fortiori. The one systemic change — the Administrative Member — does not detract, since the bench is presided over by the Judicial Member and the two-member bench is reserved for rights disputes; the Bharat Bank test looks to the State's investiture of judicial functions, not the composition.

Conclusion. The statement accurately captures the ratio: not a court, but a tribunal; award = determination; Art. 136 available but exceptional. The Code confirms every premise and adds new trappings; the answer is therefore the same — with the practical caveat that the Supreme Court will expect the High Court to be approached first.

May–June 2025 · Q.4 · 15 marks (problem)
Workers of XYZ Ltd. allege that the employer imposed an illegal lock-out from 1 April and claim wages for the period. The dispute before the Industrial Tribunal is framed as: "Whether the lock-out declared by the management from 1 April is legal and justified; if not, to what relief are the workers entitled?" The employer pleads that the establishment was in fact permanently closed on 1 April owing to losses and that the Tribunal cannot go into "closure" as it is outside the dispute. Decide. Would your answer differ if the Central Government had referred the matter to the NIT?

Issues

  1. Is the Tribunal confined to the dispute as framed, and can it examine the plea of closure?
  2. Lock-out v. closure — the legal distinction and consequences.
  3. The NIT variant.

Law

Jurisdiction and the terms of the dispute. Under IDA s.10(4) the Tribunal had to confine its adjudication to the points referred and matters incidental thereto (Delhi Cloth & General Mills v. Workmen, AIR 1967 SC 469; Pottery Mazdoor Panchayat v. Perfect Pottery, AIR 1979 SC 1356). Under the Code the Tribunal's jurisdiction arises on the party's application in Form II (s.53(6)) or a s.54 reference; it adjudicates the dispute as framed and questions incidental to it, and it alone decides objections to its jurisdiction (Dharangadhra). Whether the cessation on 1 April was a "lock-out" or a "closure" is a jurisdictional fact incidental to — indeed inseparable from — the question whether the lock-out was legal and justified; the Tribunal not only may but must decide it (DCGM). A neutrally framed question is essential (TISCO v. State of Jharkhand, 2014) — the framing here is neutral.

Lock-out v. closure. Lock-out (s.2(u)) is the temporary closing of a place of employment or suspension of work or refusal to continue to employ — a weapon in the employer's hands during a dispute; the employer–employee relationship subsists. Closure (s.2(h)) is the permanent closing down of a place of employment or part thereof — the end of the business and of the relationship. Tests: intention (bona fide and permanent v. tactical), conduct (disposal of assets, notices, retrenchment compensation), and subsequent events (Express Newspapers v. Workmen, AIR 1963 SC 569; Kairbetta Estate v. Rajamanickam, AIR 1960 SC 893 on lock-out; Management of Kairbetta distinguishes lay-off). A closure that is a "sham or camouflage" to defeat workers is treated as a lock-out and wages may be awarded; a genuine closure attracts the closure provisions (60 days' notice under s.74, compensation under s.75; and, for establishments with 300+ workers, prior permission under s.80) but not lock-out wages. The legality of a lock-out is tested under s.62(2) (notice/cooling-off; bars during conciliation/adjudication) and s.63; wages for an illegal lock-out period are ordinarily awarded, while for a legal but unjustified lock-out the Tribunal weighs justification (the Statesman/India Marine Service line).

Application

The Tribunal (two-member bench, because legality of a lock-out and closure are both s.44 two-member matters) should (i) record evidence on whether the 1 April cessation was intended to be permanent — sale of machinery, cancellation of licences, s.74 notice to Government, payment of s.75 compensation, prior permission under s.80 if 300+ workers; (ii) if closure is genuine, hold that there was no lock-out and decline lock-out wages, leaving the workers to closure compensation (enforceable under s.59); (iii) if the "closure" is a camouflage (e.g., production resumed, no notice, no compensation), hold it an illegal lock-out (no notice under s.62(2); if conciliation was pending, barred by s.62(2)(d)) and award full wages for the period, with penalties under s.86(14) for the employer's illegal lock-out. The employer's objection that closure is "outside the dispute" fails.

NIT variant

If the Central Government had referred the dispute to the NIT under s.54 (national importance/multi-State), the analysis of jurisdiction is the same — the NIT decides incidental questions — but two additional points arise: (a) the reference itself is reviewable if the dispute is a single-establishment, fact-bound dispute dressed up as "national" (FDC Ltd. v. Union of India, 2007 — establishments in several States means different establishments, not one employer's branches); and (b) on such reference any Tribunal proceedings on the same matter stand quashed (s.54(2)), and the Central Government may, on public grounds, defer/modify the NIT award under s.55(3)–(5). The answer on the merits would not differ.

Conclusion

The Tribunal has jurisdiction to decide whether the alleged lock-out was in truth a closure, that being incidental to and inseparable from the dispute framed; the outcome depends on the bona fides and permanence of the cessation; the employer's technical objection is rejected.

Voluntary arbitration under s.10A of the IDA [now s.42 of the IR Code, 2020].

Meaning. Voluntary reference by the employer and workers, by written agreement, of an existing or apprehended industrial dispute to arbitrator(s) of their choice, at any time before it is before a Tribunal/NIT (s.42(1)). Essentials: prescribed form (Form V) signed per R.20/R.22, naming arbitrator(s) — who may include a Tribunal member; umpire where even number (s.42(2)); copy to Government and CO; publication (s.42(4), R.21) — mandatory (Karnal Leather, 1990); Government's notification where signatories represent the majority, giving non-signatories a hearing (s.42(5)) and enabling prohibition of strike/lock-out (s.42(7); s.62(1)(f)); arbitrator investigates and submits a signed award (s.42(6)); A&C Act 1996 excluded (s.42(8)). Nov–Dec 2025 · Q.1 · 15 marks (problem)
The management of ABC Mills and the recognised union agreed in writing to refer their wage-revision dispute to Mr. J, a retired District Judge, as arbitrator under s.10A [s.42]. The agreement was forwarded to the Government and published; a majority notification was issued. Mr. J heard the parties and, without recording any evidence on the company's financial capacity and without giving reasons, rejected the union's demand. The union wants to challenge the award directly in the Supreme Court under Art. 136. Advise. Would your answer be the same if the award had been made by the Industrial Tribunal? Would it differ if the arbitration agreement had not been published?

Issues

  1. Is an award of a s.10A/s.42 arbitrator appealable under Art. 136? Which forum should the union approach?
  2. Grounds available on these facts.
  3. Comparison with a Tribunal award.
  4. Effect of non-publication of the agreement.

Law and application

(1) Forum. The Bharat Bank test requires a State-constituted body invested with judicial functions and trappings. Engineering Mazdoor Sabha v. Hind Cycles (1963) held the s.10A arbitrator (as the section then stood) not a "tribunal" for Art. 136 because his authority derived from the parties' agreement — leaving Arts. 226/227. Rohtas Industries (1976, DU material) held the award amenable to Art. 226 as the decision of a quasi-statutory body, noting the 1964 amendments (publication, binding effect under s.18, enforceability under s.17A, bar on strikes under s.23) had assimilated the arbitrator to the statutory system. Gujarat Steel Tubes (1980) accepted an Art. 136 appeal against a s.10A award and held the arbitrator possesses s.11A powers. Under the Code the assimilation is complete — the arbitration award is an "award" (s.2(e)), enforceable (s.55), binding on all where notified (s.57(3)), executable as a decree (s.49(10)); the majority notification was issued here. Advice: an SLP under Art. 136 is maintainable (Gujarat Steel Tubes), but the Supreme Court will ordinarily decline to entertain a direct petition and expect a writ petition under Arts. 226/227 before the High Court first (Bengal Chemical; practice since Bharat Bank). File in the High Court; an appeal by special leave lies thereafter.

(2) Grounds. (a) No evidence / natural justice: wage revision turns on the industry's financial capacity and the region-cum-industry principle (Express Newspapers; Hindustan Times v. Workmen, AIR 1963 SC 1332); deciding without any evidence on capacity mirrors the vice condemned in Bharat Bank — an award on no evidence is arbitrary, "benevolent despotism". (b) Unreasoned award: a s.10A/s.42 award must be a speaking award; absence of reasons is itself an error apparent and prevents review (Rohtas). (c) Any misapplication of settled wage-fixation principles is an error of law apparent on the face. Relief: quash and remit to the arbitrator (or a fresh arbitrator by agreement); pending decision the union may seek interim relief. Note that the arbitrator's decision on a demand dispute (interest dispute) is otherwise entitled to deference — courts do not re-fix wages.

(3) If the award had been made by the Industrial Tribunal. The maintainability question becomes straightforward: the Tribunal is a "tribunal" under Art. 136 (Bharat Bank), and a fortiori under the Code (s.44 Judicial Member; s.49 powers; s.55 enforceability). The grounds and the Court's restraint are identical; additionally, a Tribunal award on wages is preceded by a two-member/single-member bench as notified, is communicated under s.55(2), and if the Government is a party it may invoke s.55(3). In both cases the High Court route is the practical first step, but the doctrinal doubt raised by Engineering Mazdoor Sabha does not exist for the Tribunal. So the answer is "the same in result, but stronger in maintainability".

(4) If the agreement had not been published. Under Karnal Leather Karamchari Sanghatan v. Liberty Footwear (1990) publication of the arbitration agreement under s.10A(3) (now s.42(4), R.21 — on the Ministry's website) is mandatory; non-publication renders the award invalid and unenforceable, because workers must know what is referred and who decides, and non-signatories' s.42(5) rights depend on it. The union could therefore attack the award as a nullity in the High Court (and the management could not enforce it); the merits grounds would become unnecessary. Ironically, a union that participated might face an argument of waiver/estoppel, but Karnal Leather rejected the "participation cures non-publication" argument.

Conclusion

Advise a writ petition under Arts. 226/227 (with Art. 136 available thereafter, and in principle directly per Gujarat Steel Tubes), on the grounds of no evidence, no reasons and error of law; the answer is the same in substance for a Tribunal award, with clearer Art. 136 maintainability; if the agreement was never published the award is void ab initio under Karnal Leather.

Nov–Dec 2025 · Q.8(c) · 5 marks (short note)
Withholding publication of an award — Sirsilk Ltd. v. Government of Andhra Pradesh — and the position under the IR Code, 2020.

IDA position. s.17(1) required every award to be published within 30 days of receipt; s.17(2) made the published award final; s.17A made it enforceable 30 days after publication. In Sirsilk Ltd. v. Govt. of A.P., AIR 1964 SC 160, the parties settled after the award reached the Government but before publication and jointly asked that it not be published; the Government refused, treating s.17(1) as mandatory. The Supreme Court held: publication is ordinarily mandatory and the Government has no discretion to withhold it (a safeguard against executive interference); but where a valid settlement under s.18(1) has been reached before publication, publishing the award would create two conflicting binding instruments and defeat the Act's object of industrial peace; in that exceptional case the Government should withhold publication and the settlement prevails. Related: the 30-day period is directory (Remington Rand, 1968); the Government cannot modify an award except under s.17A where it is a party.

Under the Code. Gazette publication is abolished: the award is communicated to the parties and the Government (s.55(2)) and becomes enforceable on the expiry of 30 days (s.55(3)); only where the Government is a party (or for NIT awards) may it defer/reject/modify on public grounds (s.55(3)–(5)). So the Sirsilk device of "withholding publication" has no literal counterpart. The underlying principle survives through other doors: (i) a settlement reached before the award is made should be placed before the Tribunal, which may record it and make an award in terms of it (or permit withdrawal of the application); (ii) a settlement reached after communication but before the 30-day enforceability date is a settlement under s.2(zi)/s.57(1) (or s.57(3) if in conciliation) which the parties can jointly ask the Tribunal/Government to take on record; courts applying Sirsilk's reasoning would give effect to the later, consensual instrument, since both instruments cannot operate on the same matter (s.62(1)(g); s.58); (iii) in any event the parties bound by an award can terminate it after its period by 60 days' notice (s.58(6)) and replace it by settlement. Thus the note should conclude that the Code converts Sirsilk from a rule about publication into a rule about which binding instrument prevails — the parties' subsequent settlement.

C.L.C. 2004/2006; L.C.-I 2002; C.L.C. 2000/2001; L.C.-I 2004/2006; L.C.-II 2002/2004/2005; L.C.-I 2005 (three variants combined)
(a) "The Supreme Court has no jurisdiction to grant special leave to appeal from the award of an Industrial Tribunal as the latter is neither a court nor does it exercise the judicial power of the State." Comment.
(b) "The Industrial Disputes Act is intended to be a self-contained Act and seeks to achieve social justice on the basis of conciliation, arbitration or adjudication. Notwithstanding the finality attached to the orders/awards of these authorities, it is still desirable that there be some forum to correct any misuse of power or procedural irregularities by such authorities." Do you feel Art. 136 has provided such a forum? Give reasons.
(c) Discuss and define the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court vis-à-vis the determinations of industrial tribunals.

Thesis. The proposition in (a) was the respondents' preliminary objection in Bharat Bank and was rejected by the majority; (b) and (c) ask for the reasons and the limits. Structure: (1) the text of Art. 136 and the "self-contained code" premise; (2) the meaning of "tribunal" — Bharat Bank's functional test; (3) why the Industrial Tribunal qualifies; (4) the answer to the "judicial power of the State" argument; (5) the discipline of the jurisdiction; (6) the position under the IR Code.

1. Text and premise. Art. 136 is discretionary and overriding, reaching "any court or tribunal" and any "determination". The industrial statute is indeed self-contained: no appeal (Code: s.45 finality; s.55 enforceability; s.97 civil-court bar), because industrial peace needs speed and finality. But finality was never meant to license excess of jurisdiction or denial of hearing; the Constitution's supervisory jurisdictions (Arts. 226, 227, 136) are the "forum to correct misuse of power".

2. "Tribunal" ≠ "court". Mahajan J.: the framers used both words; "tribunal" must add something — bodies that are not courts but are constituted by the State and invested with some of the State's judicial functions, "adorned with similar trappings as a court". Sources: Halsbury; R. v. LCC (1931) — deciding rightly after hearing evidence and opposition; Lord Sankey in Shell Co. (1931) — the negative catalogue (finality, oath, appeal, etc., do not by themselves make a court); Griffith C.J. in Huddart Parker — judicial power is the power to decide controversies between subjects with binding authority; Cooper v. Wilson (1937) — four requisites of a judicial decision. Later crystallised in Associated Cement v. P.N. Sharma (1965): investiture with the inherent judicial power of the State.

3. Why the Industrial Tribunal qualifies. Adjudication of a lis per the statute (IDA s.7 → Code s.44, s.53(6)); judicial members (s.7A → s.44(3), s.48); civil-court powers and judicial-proceeding status (s.11(3) → s.49(3)); rules requiring pleadings, evidence, cross-examination, notice by name, reasoned award (1947 Rules → 2026 Rules); binding award enforceable by State machinery and penal on breach (ss.17–19, 29 → ss.55–58, 86, 49(10)). Kania C.J.: functions "very near those of a Court, although it is not a Court in the technical sense".

4. The "judicial power of the State" objection. Three answers: (i) the power to make new contracts (interest disputes) is conferred by the State through the statute and exercised judicially (Western India Automobile Assn., FC 1949) — legislatures may entrust such power to tribunals (now Art. 323B(2)(h)); (ii) the Government's declaration under s.15(2) was ministerial and the Court reasoned from Rex v. Electricity Commissioners and the Army Act analogy that dependence on confirmation does not de-judicialise a decision — under the Code even that dependence has gone (s.55(3): enforceable by efflux of time); (iii) the dissent's concern (Mukherjea J.: decisions on policy are unsuited to appeal) is met by the discretionary character of Art. 136 rather than by denying jurisdiction.

5. Discipline of the jurisdiction (answer to (c)). The Court is "not a court of error" (Bharat Bank); it interferes for excess of jurisdiction, breach of natural justice, arbitrary/despotic procedure, award on no evidence; it quashes and remits rather than deciding the dispute; it does not review facts (Wenger, 1964); it decides general questions of industrial law (Bengal Chemical, 1959; Hindustan Antibiotics, 1967); it requires substantial and grave injustice or a settled principle misapplied (Kamani Metals, 1967); it expects the High Court route first. It does not extend to the Conciliation Officer (Jaswant Sugar Mills) or, formerly, to a Government's reference decision — those are administrative. It does extend (per Gujarat Steel Tubes) to statutory arbitrators. Outcome in Bharat Bank itself illustrates the discipline: jurisdiction affirmed, yet the appeal dismissed 4:1 on merits.

6. Under the IR Code. Every trapping is retained or strengthened (s.44 Judicial Member; s.49(3), (9), (10); s.55; s.97). Hence Art. 136 remains the ultimate corrective forum — used sparingly — while Arts. 226/227 are the working forum. Conclusion: statement (a) is wrong in law; Art. 136 does provide the desired forum, disciplined by self-restraint; the jurisdiction is supervisory, exceptional and corrective, not appellate in the ordinary sense.

L.C.-I 2002; L.C.-II 2009 (variant)
Are the decisions/orders of the following authorities appealable under Art. 136 before the Supreme Court? Give reasons: (i) Conciliation Officer; (ii) Arbitrator; (iii) Industrial Tribunal.

Test: a State-constituted body exercising part of the State's judicial power with the trappings of a court (Bharat Bank; Associated Cement v. P.N. Sharma).

(i) Conciliation Officer — No. His functions are administrative: he mediates, investigates, records settlement or failure (ss.43, 53); he decides no rights and passes no binding order (Sasamusa; Security Paper Mill). Even where he must act judicially — permission/approval under s.90 (IDA s.33) — he is not a tribunal because he cannot finally adjudicate rights (Jaswant Sugar Mills v. Lakshmi Chand, AIR 1963 SC 677). Remedy: Art. 226/227. A memorandum of settlement is not an "order" against which certiorari lies (Jain), though the settlement's validity can be tested before the Tribunal.

(ii) Arbitrator under s.42 (IDA s.10A) — Yes, with a history. Initially "No": Engineering Mazdoor Sabha v. After the 1964 amendments (publication, majority notification binding non-signatories, enforceability under s.17A, binding effect under s.18, strike bar under s.23) the arbitrator became a statutory tribunal: Rohtas Industries (1976) — Art. 226 lies; Gujarat Steel Tubes (1980) — Art. 136 entertained; arbitrator has s.11A powers. A purely private/commercial arbitrator remains outside Art. 136 (Bharat Bank itself so noted).

(iii) Industrial Tribunal / NIT — Yes. Bharat Bank (3:2); consistently followed (Bengal Chemical; Wenger; Kamani Metals), with the discretion exercised sparingly; a fortiori under ss.44–49, 55 and 97 of the Code. Note also (iv) the appropriate Government deciding whether to refer (IDA) — administrative, not appealable (only Art. 226 mandamus: K.P. C.L.C. 2002; L.C.-I 2005
A company retrenched one workman by paying retrenchment compensation. The workman alleged victimisation and raised a dispute; conciliation failed; the Government refused to refer, stating that "the case did not disclose any legal flaw and was not a fit case for reference". Decide. Can a court in any situation direct the appropriate Government to make a reference?

IDA. The refusal decides the merits ("no legal flaw") — impermissible: the Government's power under ss.10(1)/12(5) is administrative and confined to a prima facie view of whether a dispute exists; whether retrenchment complied with ss.25F/25G and whether it was colourable victimisation are adjudicatory questions. Following K.P. Krishnan (extraneous/non-germane reasons → mandamus to reconsider), Ram Avtar Sharma (refusal on merits quashed), Telco Convoy (Government cannot adjudicate), the High Court would quash the refusal and direct the Government to reconsider and, in appropriate cases, to refer. Can the court "direct a reference"? Ordinarily it directs reconsideration; but where the only reasons are patently extraneous and no other conclusion is possible, courts have directed the reference itself (Telco Convoy; Sharad Kumar). The Government may also refer later on fresh material (Western India Match; Sultan Singh). Since this is an individual retrenchment, s.2A also gave the workman the direct Labour Court route (post-2010).

Code. The problem dissolves: retrenchment of an individual worker is a deemed industrial dispute (s.2(q), s.4(9)); after the conciliation failure the workman (or union) applies to the Industrial Tribunal within 90 days (s.53(6)) — or 45 days after his conciliation application without a report (s.4(10)) — within two years of retrenchment (s.4(11)). The Tribunal decides compliance with ss.70–71 [IDA ss.25F–25G] and victimisation (an unfair labour practice under the Second Schedule, s.84), granting s.50 relief. The Government has no say; the only reviewable reference decision is the Central Government's s.54 NIT reference (FDC).

L.C.-II 2001/2002/2005 (recast)
Describe the powers of the appropriate Government to refer industrial disputes for adjudication [IDA s.10(1)]. Has the IR Code, 2020 abolished the reference system? Evaluate the change.

IDA s.10(1). Where the Government was "of opinion that any industrial dispute exists or is apprehended", it could at any time by order in writing refer (a) to a Board, (b) any connected matter to a Court of Inquiry, (c) a Second-Schedule matter to a Labour Court, (d) any matter to a Tribunal; provisos: Third-Schedule matters affecting ≤100 workmen could go to a Labour Court; in PUS with a s.22 notice the reference was mandatory unless frivolous/vexatious; s.10(1A) Central reference to the National Tribunal; s.10(2) mandatory reference on joint application; s.10(3) prohibition of strikes; s.10(4) confinement to points referred; s.10(5) common reference; s.10(6) NT ouster. The power was administrative (Sarathy), reviewable for extraneous reasons (K.P. Krishnan), not a power to adjudicate (Telco Convoy, Sharad Kumar), exercisable "at any time" even after refusal (Western India Match), and constitutionally valid (DCGM v. Shambhu Nath).

The Code. Yes — for the Industrial Tribunal the reference system is abolished: access is by the party's application after the failure report (s.53(6)) or after 45 days for termination disputes (s.4(10)); TU disputes and negotiating-union disputes go directly (s.22, R.9(8)); the Tribunal frames its own issues. The reference survives only for the NIT (s.54) and, in a residual sense, for interpretation questions (s.95) and period-shortening references (s.58(4)). Evaluation: gains — removes executive gatekeeping and the huge Art. 226 docket on refusals; certainty of timelines (90 days/45 days/2 years); parity with the constitutional idea of access to justice. Costs — the Government's screening of frivolous/stale disputes is lost (partly replaced by limitation periods and the Tribunal's own power to reject); risk of overloading Tribunals; loss of the s.10(2) joint-application and s.10(5) common-reference tools (though s.92 transfer and the NIT can consolidate); political neutrality of "national importance" references still contestable (FDC). On balance the change implements the Second National Commission on Labour's recommendation and the judicial critique in Rohtas of "dawdling dysfunction".

Jain, "Further Questions" (undated DU paper)
An award submitted by an arbitrator under s.10A [s.42] and published by the Government had gone against the union. What arguments would you advance on behalf of the union to challenge it, and in which forum?

Forum: High Court under Arts. 226/227 (Rohtas; Engineering Mazdoor Sabha), with Art. 136 available thereafter (and in principle directly — Gujarat Steel Tubes). Arguments (in descending order of strength, adapted to facts): (1) Nullity for non-compliance with s.42/R.20–21 — agreement not in Form V, not signed by authorised representatives (R.20/R.22), arbitrator's consent missing, agreement not published (Karnal Leather), or entered into after the dispute was already before the Tribunal (s.42(1)); (2) Excess of jurisdiction — matters outside the agreement decided; conditions fixed by statute altered (FACT); non-signatory workers bound without a s.42(5) notification; (3) Natural justice — no hearing/evidence/cross-examination; bias or interest of the arbitrator; (4) Error of law apparent on the face — e.g., misapplication of wage-fixation principles, treating an illegal strike as per se disentitling reinstatement or as a tort (Rohtas), ignoring s.50 proportionality (Gujarat Steel Tubes); (5) Unreasoned/non-speaking award; (6) Perversity — finding on no evidence (Bharat Bank principle); (7) if a subsequent settlement exists, Sirsilk reasoning that the consensual instrument should prevail. 24. Practice problems under the Code (with skeleton solutions)

Problem 1 — GRC timelines

Meena is dismissed on 10 Jan 2026 from a 40-worker unit. She approaches the GRC on 5 Nov 2026; the GRC gives no decision. She applies to the CO on 20 Feb 2027 and to the Tribunal on 1 May 2027. Is she in time? Skeleton: GRC application within 1 year (s.4(5)) — yes; GRC period 30 days → expired 5 Dec 2026; CO application due within 60 days of that (by 3 Feb 2027) (s.4(8)) — late; but s.4(9)–(10) create an independent route: dismissal is a deemed dispute; a direct Tribunal application lies 45 days after the CO application (i.e., after 6 April 2027) within 2 years of dismissal (by 9 Jan 2028) (s.4(11)) — the 1 May 2027 application is maintainable; the 60-day lapse may be raised by the employer but the Tribunal's jurisdiction under s.4(10) is "notwithstanding anything in this section or s.53". Also the s.53(1) proviso two-year bar on conciliation is not crossed.

Problem 2 — Works Committee "consent"

The Works Committee of a jute mill "approves" a new shift system reducing overtime; workers strike after notice; the employer says they are bound. Skeleton: WC is consultative (s.3(2)); its members represent workers only for WC functions; it cannot alter conditions of service (North Brook Jute; M.D. Ramakrishnan); a shift change is a Third-Schedule matter requiring 21 days' notice of change (s.40); workers are not bound; the strike's legality turns on s.62 compliance and pendency bars, not on the WC's approval.

Problem 3 — Strike during conciliation

Union serves a 60-day notice on 1 March; CO starts conciliation on 3 March; workers strike on 20 March while conciliation is pending. Skeleton: The 14-day cooling-off (s.62(1)(b)) expired on 15 March, but the strike is barred during conciliation and 7 days after (s.62(1)(d)); the CO's 14-day report period (s.53(5)) ran to 17 March unless extended — if the failure report was submitted on 17 March, the bar ended 24 March; a strike on 20 March is illegal (s.63) → penalties s.86(13), (15); non-strikers protected (s.93); employer may lock out in consequence (s.63(3)); wages for the strike period ordinarily not payable (Rohtas — no compensation claim though).

Problem 4 — Late Tribunal application

Failure report received on 1 June; the union files Form II on 5 September (day 96). Skeleton: s.53(6)/R.23(9) prescribe 90 days; the Code contains no express condonation for s.53(6) (contrast the express condonation proviso to s.59(1)). Arguments: (i) Limitation Act s.5 applies only to "courts"; Tribunals are not courts unless the special law so provides (Nityananda M. Joshi v. LIC, AIR 1970 SC 209 held s.33C(2) proceedings not governed by the Limitation Act) — so no condonation; (ii) contra: the period is directory and the Tribunal has incidental power to entertain for sufficient cause; (iii) for termination disputes the s.4(10)–(11) route (2 years) remains open; (iv) parties may re-initiate conciliation if within the 2-year proviso to s.53(1). Advise filing with an application explaining delay and, alternatively, a fresh conciliation request.

Problem 5 — Arbitration without notification

Two of three unions (60% of workers) sign an arbitration agreement; the Government publishes it but issues no s.42(5) notification; the third union strikes during the arbitration. Skeleton: Without a s.42(5) notification the award binds only signatories (s.57(2)); the strike bar in s.62(1)(f) applies only where a notification has been issued — so the third union's strike is not illegal on that ground (check s.62(1)(a)–(d), (g)); the Government should have considered notification since signatories represented the majority; the third union could have been heard under s.42(5); advise the employer to seek the notification and, meanwhile, conciliation.

Problem 6 — Government deferring its own award

A State transport corporation (State Government a party) loses before the Tribunal; the award is communicated on 1 July; on 20 July the Government notifies that it shall not become enforceable; no further order is passed. Skeleton: s.55(3) proviso permits the declaration because the Government is a party; s.55(4) gives 90 days from communication (till 29 September) to reject/modify and lay before the Legislature; absent an order, the award becomes enforceable on expiry of the 90 days (s.55(5)); workers may then execute under s.49(10) and, if reinstatement is directed and the Government appeals, claim s.56 wages. A challenge to the declaration itself lies under Art. 226 if the "public grounds" are a sham.

Problem 7 — Protected worker dismissed during pendency

During a Tribunal proceeding on bonus, the employer dismisses the union general secretary (a recognised protected worker) for assaulting a supervisor, paying one month's wages and applying for approval. Skeleton: For a protected worker s.90(3) requires express prior permission for any action (connected or not); the "pay and apply for approval" route in s.90(2) is unavailable; the dismissal is void; the worker may complain under s.91 (Form XVI) and the Tribunal adjudicates as an industrial dispute; Jaipur Zila Sahakari Bhoomi Vikas Bank (2002) — worker deemed in service; penalty under s.86(20). Check R.39 compliance (names by 30 April; recognition within 15 days; 1%/min 5/max 100).

Problem 8 — NIT reference of individual disputes

The Central Government refers to a NIT the terminations of 40 employees of a private bank spread over eight States. Skeleton: s.54 requires a question of national importance or a dispute in which establishments in more than one State are interested; per FDC Ltd. (2007) fact-bound individual terminations of one employer's branches do not qualify; non-application of mind; the reference is quashable under Art. 226 (the NIT is not immune); the proper route is each worker's own Tribunal application under s.4(10)/s.53(6) before the Central-sphere Tribunal (banking → Central Government appropriate). Note the ouster consequence of a valid reference (s.54(2)).

Problem 9 — Wrong bench; incomplete signatures

(a) A dismissal dispute is decided by the Administrative Member sitting alone; (b) in another matter both members hear the case, one retires, and the remaining member signs the award. Skeleton: (a) s.44 requires the two-member bench for discharge/dismissal — coram non judice; s.45 finality covers "vacancy or defect in constitution", not want of jurisdiction (Bharat Bank ground 4 reasoning; Haryana Co-op. Transport); award liable to be quashed under Art. 226. (b) s.55(1) requires signature by all members; "three hearing and two deciding is repugnant to fairness" (Mahajan J.); the Government must fill the vacancy and the matter be reheard or continued from the stage reached as the reconstituted Tribunal decides (cf. United Commercial Bank, 1951; s.51 analogy); s.47 does not apply because there is no "difference" but an absence.

Problem 10 — Lawyer in conciliation

The employer brings an advocate to the conciliation sitting; the union objects; before the Tribunal the union objects again. Skeleton: s.94(3) — no legal practitioner in conciliation (absolute); s.94(4) — before the Tribunal only with the consent of the other parties and leave (Paradip Port Trust; Thyssen Krupp, 2023); an advocate who is a genuine office-bearer of an employers' association may appear in that capacity (s.94(2)) but not as a device; the Tribunal may grant leave to both sides for parity.

What did Cooper v. Wilson supply?The four requisites of a true judicial decision; the Industrial Tribunal has the first three and much of the fourth.
What is the s.56 benefit?Full last-drawn wages to a reinstated worker while the employer's High Court/Supreme Court challenge is pending.
Can a civil court grant an injunction against a strike under the Code?No — s.97 bars suits/injunctions on matters within the Tribunal's competence; the remedy is under the Code.

26.3 Glossary

Award
Interim or final determination of an industrial dispute by a Tribunal/NIT, including an arbitration award under s.42 (s.2(e)).
Bi-partite forum
Body composed of employer and worker representatives only (WC, GRC, negotiating council) — no third party.
Conciliation
Statutory mediation by a Conciliation Officer aimed at a settlement; compulsory under s.53.
Determination
The word in Art. 136 (and s.2(e)) describing a tribunal's binding decision; held to include Industrial Tribunal awards.
Failure report
CO's report under s.53(4) that no settlement could be reached; triggers the 90-day Tribunal window.
Industrial adjudication
Compulsory determination of an industrial dispute by a statutory tribunal that may create new rights (interest disputes) as well as enforce existing ones (rights disputes).
Interest v. rights dispute
Interest: what the terms should be (wages, bonus); rights: what existing terms/law entitle (dismissal, standing orders). Two-member bench mandatory for the main rights disputes (s.44).
Negotiating union/council
Recognised bargaining agent under s.14 (51% union; else council of 20%+ unions).
Protected worker
Union office-bearer recognised under s.90(3)/R.39, immune from action during pendency without permission.
Settlement
Agreement in conciliation, or a written signed agreement outside it delivered to the authorities (s.2(zi)).
Trappings of a court
Features (evidence on oath, cross-examination, civil-court powers, penalties for false evidence, binding decision) indicating a body exercises judicial functions — Bharat Bank test.
Tribunal (Art. 136)
A body constituted by the State and invested with part of the State's judicial power, though not a court.
Umpire
Third arbitrator who decides when an even number of arbitrators are equally divided (s.2(c), s.42(2)).
