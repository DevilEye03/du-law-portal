0Orientation: How Unit 3 Fits Together

Unit 3 is the “output” unit of the whole dispute-settlement course. Units 1–2 deal with how a dispute travels through the machinery (Works Committee → Grievance Redressal Committee → Conciliation → Voluntary Arbitration → Adjudication → Reference). Unit 3 asks: what comes out at the other end? The answer is one of two statutory instruments —

① A Settlement — the consensual output

An agreement between employer and workers (in or outside conciliation), given statutory force by the Code. It is peace by agreement. IDA §2(p) IRC §2(zi)

② An Award — the adjudicatory output

A determination imposed on the parties by an arbitrator, Industrial Tribunal or National Industrial Tribunal. It is peace by adjudication. IDA §2(b) IRC §2(e)

The organising idea of Unit 3

Settlements and awards are two species of the same genus — instruments that replace the parties' freedom of action with legally binding obligations. Because they bind, the law must answer five questions about each of them:

  1. What is it? (nature / definition / ingredients)
  2. Who does it bind? (persons bound — private vs. public binding effect)
  3. For how long? (duration / period of operation)
  4. How does it end? (termination — notice, majority requirement, expiry)
  5. How is it controlled? (enforcement, criminal sanction, recovery, and judicial review)

Every case in this Unit is an answer to one of these five questions. Keep this grid in your head and no question in the paper can surprise you.

ASettlement — Nature, Duration & Termination

A.1 What is a “Settlement”? — The Statutory Definition

Definition — IDA, 1947, §2(p)

“Settlement” means a settlement arrived at in the course of conciliation proceeding and includes a written agreement between the employer and workmen arrived at otherwise than in the course of conciliation proceeding where such agreement has been signed by the parties thereto in such manner as may be prescribed and a copy thereof has been sent to an officer authorised in this behalf by the appropriate Government and the Conciliation Officer.

Definition — Industrial Relations Code, 2020, §2(zi)

“Settlement” means a settlement arrived at in the course of conciliation proceeding and includes a written agreement between the employer and worker arrived at otherwise than in the course of conciliation proceeding where such agreement has been signed by the parties thereto in such manner as may be prescribed and a copy thereof has been sent to an officer authorised in this behalf by the appropriate Government and to the conciliation officer.

Ingredients of a statutory settlement — break the definition into five elements
  1. Parties: an employer and workmen/workers (or their unions/representatives). Note that an agreement requires the “right” counterparties — the Rules require signatures of the employer and, on the workers’ side, the prescribed office-bearers of the trade union, or five duly authorised worker representatives (IR Rules, 2026).
  2. Subject-matter: must relate to an industrial dispute or a matter in dispute — a settlement is a mode of resolving a dispute, not a general commercial contract.
  3. Two permissible routes:
    • Route 1 — during conciliation (¶1 of the definition): settlement arrived at in the course of conciliation proceedings (IDA §12(3) / IRC §53(3)). This limb is self-executing: the definition does not require any further formality.
    • Route 2 — otherwise than in conciliation (¶2 of the definition): a written agreement signed outside conciliation — but only if (a) signed in the prescribed manner, and (b) a copy is sent to the officer authorised by the appropriate Government and to the Conciliation Officer.
  4. Writing and prescribed manner: a purely oral settlement, however genuine, is not a statutory “settlement” for the second limb. Under the IR Rules, 2026 every settlement — whether in conciliation or otherwise — must be recorded in Form I and signed by authorised representatives; copies go to the Deputy Chief Labour Commissioner (Central) and the Conciliation Officer, and Conciliation Officers maintain a register of settlements (serial number, name of industry, parties, date, and whether arrived at in conciliation or by mutual negotiation).
  5. Legal result intended: to produce a binding instrument with the consequences in §18 / §57 (persons bound) and §19 / §58 (period of operation).

A.2 The Two Kinds of Settlement — and why the difference decides every question

A.3 Nature of a Settlement — Five Characteristics

  1. Consensual, not imposed. Its source of authority is the parties’ own agreement; the statute merely adds legal force. Hence the courts’ repeated refrain that a settlement reflects “free will” and produces “more lasting peace than an award” (Sirsilk).
  2. Statutory, not merely contractual, once the formalities are met. A §18(3)/§57(3) settlement is a statutory instrument: it can be enforced criminally (§29 / §86(17)-(18)) and money due under it can be recovered as arrears of land revenue (§33-C / §59).
  3. Collective in operation. It may be born of an agreement with a union or a group of workers, but once it falls within §18(3)/§57(3) it operates as a species of collective agreement, binding even those who never signed it — including workers recruited years later. Herbertsons: a settlement which is “just and fair” and signed with the recognised union is binding on all workmen, even on a small minority who are not its members; the majority union cannot be defeated by a splinter union’s dissent.
  4. Not appealable, but reviewable at the threshold. A Tribunal does not sit in appeal over the terms of a settlement; but a settlement can be challenged on grounds of fraud, misrepresentation, coercion, or because it was not a settlement at all (e.g., an agreement outside the factory gate involving non-workmen). Where such a challenge succeeds, the aggrieved party may raise a fresh industrial dispute; the settlement is then liable to be set aside only on those limited grounds.
  5. Preferred over adjudication by the statutory scheme. The Act’s architecture (conciliation first) and §40/§9-A (no notice of change needed to give effect to a settlement or award — IRC §40 proviso (a) & (d)) show that the settlement is the primary output; adjudication is a fallback.
Related authorities you can cite (beyond the two prescribed cases)
  • Herbertsons Ltd. v. Workmen of Herbertsons Ltd., (1977) 2 SCC 515 — A settlement signed with the recognised union, if just and fair, binds all workmen; the Tribunal cannot substitute its own view of the terms; mere disagreement with parts of a settlement is not a ground to disturb it.
  • P. Virudhachalam v. Lotus Mills, (1998) 2 SCC 625 — Reaffirms that a §12(3)/§18(3) conciliation settlement is a collective agreement binding on all workmen, including dissenting minority unions, and even on workers who did not participate.
  • State of Bihar v. D.N. Ganguly, AIR 1958 SC 1018 — The Government cannot cancel or supersede a §10(1) reference pending adjudication; and where parties settle pending an adjudication, the settlement is to be respected (the reasoning later adopted in Sirsilk).
  • Bata Shoe Co. (in D.N. Ganguly) — a settlement reached during conciliation brings the dispute to an end; the reference machinery cannot be used to reopen what has been settled by agreement.

A.4 Persons Bound — IDA §18 and IRC §57 Compared

IDA §18 — Persons on whom settlements and awards are binding

(1) A settlement arrived at by agreement between the employer and workman otherwise than in the course of conciliation proceeding shall be binding on the parties to the agreement.
(2) Subject to sub-section (3), an arbitration award which has become enforceable shall be binding on the parties to the agreement who referred the dispute to arbitration.
(3) A settlement arrived at in the course of conciliation proceedings under this Act or an arbitration award or an award of a Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal which has become enforceable shall be binding on — (a) all parties to the industrial dispute; (b) all other parties summoned to appear in the proceedings as parties to the dispute, unless the adjudicator records the opinion that they were summoned without proper cause; (c) where a party is an employer, his heirs, successors or assigns in respect of the establishment to which the dispute relates; (d) where a party is composed of workmen, all persons who were employed in the establishment/part on the date of the dispute and all persons who subsequently become employed in that establishment or part.

IRC §57 — Persons on whom settlements and awards are binding

Sub-sections (1), (2) and (3) reproduce the IDA scheme verbatim in substance, except the language is modernised (“worker” instead of “workman”) and clause (b) of §57(3) is retained identically. The four-fold binding effect of §57(3)(a)–(d) is unchanged.

Answer-template for “who is bound?”

Always answer in the §18(1)/§18(3) [IRC §57(1)/§57(3)] binary and then add the two riders: (i) a summoned party who was summoned “without proper cause” is not bound; (ii) the extension in clause (d) to “all persons who subsequently become employed” is what makes a settlement/award operate as a running obligation on the industry, not merely on the individuals who were present on the day.

A.5 Duration & Operation — IDA §19(1)–(2), (7) and IRC §58(1)–(2), (7)

IDA §19(1), (2) and (7) — Settlement

(1) A settlement shall come into operation on such date as is agreed upon by the parties to the dispute, and if no date is agreed upon, on the date on which the memorandum of settlement is signed by the parties.
(2) Such settlement shall be binding for such period as is agreed upon, and if no such period is agreed upon, for a period of six months from the date on which the memorandum is signed, and shall continue to be binding after expiry of that period until the expiry of two months from the date on which a written notice of intention to terminate the settlement is given by one party to the other.
(7) No notice given under sub-section (2) [or (6)] shall have effect, unless it is given by a party representing the majority of persons bound by the settlement or award, as the case may be.

IRC §58(1), (2) and (7) — Settlement

Identical in structure. Two changes to note: (i) the post-expiry continuation is until the expiry of sixty days’ notice (not “two months”); (ii) §58(7) reproduces the majority-notice rule of IDA §19(7).

Precision points that fetch marks
  • Six months is only the default period for a settlement. There is no outer limit for a settlement (unlike an award, capped at 3 years). Parties may agree to a 10-year settlement if they wish.
  • Award: 1 year, extendable to a maximum of 3 years. Settlement: agreed period, no statutory ceiling.
  • Notice period: IDA = 2 months; IRC = 60 days. Do not mix them.
  • Majority requirement (sub-s. (7)): applies to both settlements and awards. It is the answer to the classic problem “can a minority union terminate a settlement to press a new demand?” — No.

A.6 Termination — Mechanics and Consequences

  1. By expiry + notice: the ordinary mode. After expiry of the agreed/default period, either side (representing the majority) may serve written notice; the settlement remains binding for a further 2 months/60 days and then ceases.
  2. By a fresh settlement or award: the parties may replace a settlement by a new one; a competent award on the same matter may also supersede a settlement that has ceased to operate — but a settlement that is in operation cannot be quietly displaced (this is exactly the vice Sirsilk prevents).
  3. By adjudication of invalidity: if the settlement was procured by fraud, misrepresentation, undue influence or is otherwise not “just and fair”, the aggrieved workman/union may raise a fresh industrial dispute. If the settlement is declared invalid, the parties revert to the pre-settlement position (and, in the Sirsilk-type case, the withheld award can then be published).
  4. Not by unilateral “withdrawal” or “resignation” from the settlement — acceptance once given cannot be recalled to defeat benefits already taken: workers who have taken the benefit of a settlement are bound by it (the “approbate and reprobate” principle applied in a series of cases; see also Cipla Ltd. v. Anant Ganpat Patil (Bom. HC) — a settlement binds irrespective of whether individual workers sign an undertaking).
Practical illustration — how §19 plays out on the ground

Scenario: A Delhi auto-component plant signs a 3-year wage settlement with its recognised union in conciliation before the Conciliation Officer on 1 April 2024. In 2026 a rival union claims 51% membership and serves notice terminating the settlement, demanding fresh negotiation.

Analysis: (i) The 2024 settlement is a §12(3) settlement → binding on all workmen under §18(3), union membership irrelevant. (ii) The 3-year period has not expired, so the termination notice is premature. (iii) Even after expiry, the notice must be given by a party representing the majority of persons bound — §19(7). (iv) Until that requirement is met, the settlement continues for a further 2 months after the notice. (v) A fresh demand while the settlement operates is itself actionable as an unfair labour practice and, if a strike is called over it, the strike would be illegal (strike prohibited during the operation of a settlement — IDA §23(c); IRC §62(2)(g)).

A.7 Breach and Enforcement of a Settlement

Criminal sanction

IDA §29 Breach of any term of a settlement or award binding on a person is punishable with imprisonment up to six months, or fine, or both; in a continuing breach the court may award compensation out of the fine to the affected person.

IRC §86(17)–(18) Punishable with fine not less than ₹20,000 and up to ₹2,00,000, or imprisonment up to 3 months, or both; a continuing breach attracts an additional fine up to ₹1,000 per day, and the court may direct compensation to the affected person.

Recovery of money

IDA §33-C Money due under a settlement or award may be computed and recovered as an arrear of land revenue. IRC §59 reproduces this. Note the asymmetry Rohtas highlighted: the Act provides for recovery by a worker from an employer — there is no provision for the converse (employer claiming money from workmen).

Writ remedy

Failure to implement a binding settlement can be corrected by mandamus under Article 226 against the defaulting employer where it is a State instrumentality, or against statutory authorities; private employers are ordinarily dealt with through §29/§33-C and industrial adjudication.

Effect on change of service conditions

No notice of change is needed where the change is “in pursuance of any settlement or award” — IDA §9-A proviso; IRC §40 proviso (a) & (d). This is the practical pay-off of having a settlement: it authorises the change.

A.8 Settlements and the Sirsilk Collision (the bridge to Part B)

Everything in Part A comes together in one problem: the award is ready, the settlement is already in force. Under §18(1) [§57(1)] the settlement binds the signatories from the moment it operates; under §18(3) [§57(3)] the award, once published and enforceable, would bind “all persons” in the establishment. If the terms differ, the establishment is subject to two inconsistent binding instruments. Sirsilk solved this by directing the Government not to publish the award — an exceptional and judge-made resolution, retained (and not codified) under the IR Code. The full treatment is in Part D-3.

Exam-ready formulation for the nature of settlements

“A settlement is a bilateral, consensual instrument which the statute converts into a binding collective obligation. Its nature is threefold: (i) its source is agreement, not adjudication; (ii) its effect, under §18(3)/§57(3), extends beyond the signatories to successors, assigns, present and future workmen; and (iii) its life, under §19/§58, is autonomous — it cannot be terminated except by the expiry of the agreed period/default period and a written notice given by a party representing the majority of those bound.”

BAward — Nature & Duration

B.1 Definition and Ingredients — IDA §2(b) & IRC §2(e)

IDA §2(b)

“Award” means an interim or a final determination of any industrial dispute or of any question relating thereto by any Labour Court, Industrial Tribunal or National Industrial Tribunal and includes an arbitration award made under section 10-A.

Industrial Relations Code, 2020, §2(e)

“Award” means an interim or a final determination of any industrial dispute or of any question relating thereto by any Industrial Tribunal referred to in section 44 or National Industrial Tribunal referred to in section 46 and includes an arbitration award made under section 42.

Five ingredients — memorise as the “D-T-F-I-Q” checklist
  1. D — Determination: a concluded decision on the merits. A mere opinion, observation, interim direction that is not a “determination”, or an administrative order, is not an award.
  2. T — Two types: interim or final. An interim award decides part of the dispute or an urgent issue (e.g., interim relief/wages, an issue tried as preliminary) without ending the reference; a final award disposes of the reference.
  3. F — Forum: IDA — Labour Court (§7-A), Industrial Tribunal (§7-B), National Tribunal (§7-C) or a §10-A arbitrator. IRC — Industrial Tribunal (§44), National Industrial Tribunal (§46), or an arbitrator under §42. The merger of the Labour Court and the Industrial Tribunal into a single Industrial Tribunal (sitting as a single Judicial Member, or as Judicial + Administrative Member) is the structural change you must mention.
  4. I — Industrial dispute: there must be an industrial dispute (§2(k) / §2(q)) or a question relating thereto. No industrial dispute, no jurisdiction, no award — Rohtas Industries (the compensation claim, being a tortious claim by the employer, was not an industrial dispute; consent cannot create jurisdiction).
  5. Q — Questions relating thereto: ancillary, incidental and preliminary questions covered — the award may decide “any question relating thereto”, e.g., whether the parties stand in the relationship of employer–workman.
Which “awards” exist in practice?
  • Final award — the normal disposition of a reference.
  • Interim award — usually on (i) interim relief/wages, (ii) a preliminary issue (jurisdiction, whether the claimant is a workman), or (iii) part of the dispute capable of separate disposal. Under the IR Code, §50(2) now expressly empowers the Tribunal/NIT to grant interim relief in discharge/dismissal disputes, but “shall rely only on the materials on record and shall not take any fresh evidence”.
  • Arbitration award under §10-A / §42 — deemed an award, hence published/enforced like a tribunal award; and because it is an award of a statutory arbitration, the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 does not apply (IRC §42(8) says so expressly).
  • Award in terms of settlement — where parties settle during adjudication and ask the Tribunal to record the settlement, the resulting award is an award in terms of the settlement (this is the correct practice commended in Sirsilk itself).

B.3 From Award to Enforceability — the Complete Timeline

B.4 Publication, Finality and Enforceability — the Four Key Provisions

IDA §17 — Publication of reports and awards

(1) Every report of a Board or Court together with any minute of dissent recorded therewith, every arbitration award and every award of a Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal shall, within a period of thirty days from the date of its receipt by the appropriate Government, be published in such manner as the appropriate Government thinks fit.
(2) The award published under sub-section (1) shall be final and shall not be called in question by any Court in any manner whatsoever [subject to §17-A].

IDA §17-A — Commencement of the award

(1) An award shall become enforceable on the expiry of thirty days from the date of its publication under §17 — proviso: where an award is made by a Tribunal in a dispute to which the appropriate Government is a party, or by a National Tribunal, and the Government is of the opinion that it would be inexpedient on public grounds affecting national economy or social justice to give effect to it, the Government may, by notification, declare that the award shall not become enforceable on the expiry of the said period.
(2) Where such a declaration is made, the appropriate Government may, within 90 days from the date of publication, make an order rejecting or modifying the award, and shall lay the award together with a copy of the order before the State Legislature/Parliament.
(3) Where the award so rejected/modified is laid before the Legislature, it becomes enforceable on the expiry of 15 days from the date it is laid; and where no order is made under sub-s. (2), the award becomes enforceable on the expiry of the 90-day period.
(4) Subject to the above, the award comes into operation from the date specified in it, or, if none, from the date it becomes enforceable.

IRC §55 — Form of award, its communication and commencement

(1) The award of a Tribunal (single Judicial Member / single Administrative Member / two-member bench) or of a National Industrial Tribunal shall be in writing and signed electronically or otherwise by both members or by the member by whom the award is delivered.
(2) Every award “shall be communicated to the parties concerned and the appropriate Government”. Change The older requirement of publication in the Official Gazette by the Government is replaced by communication.
(3) An award becomes enforceable on the expiry of 30 days from the date of communication — with the same proviso as §17-A(1): where the award is given by a Tribunal in a dispute to which the appropriate Government is a party, or by an NIT, and it is inexpedient on public grounds affecting national economy or social justice to give effect to it, the Government may by notification declare that the award shall not become enforceable on the expiry of the 30-day period.
(4) On such a declaration, the Government may, within 90 days from communication, reject or modify the award and shall lay the award and the order before the Legislature/Parliament “on the first available opportunity”.
(5) An award so rejected/modified becomes enforceable on the expiry of 15 days from laying; where no order is made, it becomes enforceable on expiry of the 90-day period.
(6) The award comes into operation from the date specified in it, or from the date it becomes enforceable.

Two comparisons examiners love (worth full marks)
  1. Publication → Communication. Under §17(1) the Government published the award and the public knew of it; under §55(2)/(3) the award is communicated to the parties and the Government, and the 30-day enforceability clock runs from communication. The consequence: the accused employer can no longer plead ignorance of a gazetted award, and the trigger for enforceability is receipt, not gazetting. Note also that the discretionary “manner of publication” is gone.
  2. Loss of the express finality clause. §17(2) IDA declared the published award “final and not callable in question by any Court in any manner whatsoever”. §55 does not reproduce that clause. The bar on civil courts is now found in §97 IRC (jurisdiction of civil courts barred) — while constitutional review under Articles 226/227 and 136 is untouched. Arguably this is a drafting simplification, not a substantive change: even under the IDA, §17(2) never ousted Article 136 or Article 226 — Bharat Bank had already said so in 1950.

B.5 Full Wages Pending Higher-Court Proceedings — IDA §17-B / IRC §56

The rule in one paragraph

Where a Tribunal/NIT by its award directs reinstatement and the employer carries the matter to the High Court or the Supreme Court, the employer must pay the workman, during the pendency of those proceedings, full wages last drawn (inclusive of maintenance allowance admissible under any rule), provided (i) the workman was not employed in any establishment during that period and (ii) an affidavit to that effect is filed in Court. If it is proved to the Court’s satisfaction that the workman was employed and received adequate remuneration during that period (or part of it), the Court shall order that no wages be payable under the section for that period or part.

This is the anti-delay device: the employer cannot crush a reinstatement award by appealing and leaving the workman unpaid for years. Its operation depends on the workman filing the required affidavit — a point frequently tested in problem questions.

B.6 Duration of an Award — §19(3)–(6) / §58(3)–(7)

  1. Default duration: one year from the date the award becomes enforceable (§19(3) / §58(3)).
  2. Power to reduce: the appropriate Government may reduce the period and fix such period as it thinks fit (first proviso).
  3. Power to extend: the Government may, before expiry, extend the operation by any period not exceeding one year at a time, so that the total period does not exceed three years from the date the award came into operation (second proviso).
  4. Material change: where the Government, on its own motion or on the application of a party, considers that there has been a material change in the circumstances on which the award was based, it may refer the award (or part) to the Tribunal for a decision whether the period of operation should be shortened; the decision is final (§19(4) / §58(4)).
  5. Non-continuing awards: the one-year rule does not apply to an award which “by its nature, terms or other circumstances does not impose, after it has been given effect to, any continuing obligation” — e.g., an award merely directing payment of a sum, or ordering reinstatement (a one-time act) (§19(5) / §58(5)).
  6. Post-expiry continuance: after the period of operation ends, the award remains binding until 2 months (IDA) / 60 days (IRC) have elapsed from written notice of termination given by any party bound (§19(6) / §58(6)).
  7. Majority notice: the notice has no effect unless given by a party representing the majority of persons bound (§19(7) / §58(7)).

B.7 The Nature of Industrial Adjudication — why an award is not an ordinary decree

The classic statement comes from the Federal Court in Western India Automobile Association v. Industrial Tribunal, Bombay, (1949) FCR 321 — adopted and endorsed in Bharat Bank (1950), and again in Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. Brijnandan Pandey, (1956) SCR 800:

“Adjudication does not … mean adjudication according to the strict law of master and servant. The award of the tribunal may contain provisions for settlement of a dispute which no court could order if it was bound by ordinary law, but the tribunal is not fettered in any way by these limitations … Industrial arbitration may involve the extension of an existing agreement or the making of a new one, or in general the creation of new obligations or modification of old ones, while commercial arbitration generally concerns itself with interpretation of existing obligations.” — Mahajan J. (as he then was), Western India Automobile Association (FCR), cited with approval in Bharat Bank and in the Leading Case Materials
“A court of law proceeds on the footing that no power exists in the courts to make contracts for people; and the parties must make their own contracts. The courts reach their limit of power when they enforce contracts which the parties have made. An Industrial Tribunal is not so fettered and may create new obligations or modify contracts in the interests of industrial peace, to protect legitimate trade union activities and to prevent unfair practice or victimisation.” — Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. Brijnandan Pandey (1956) SCR 800, approved in the course materials

From this follows the five-fold nature of an award:

  1. Binding like a decree — enforceable through the machinery of the Act; under the IR Code, §49(9) expressly provides that an award, order or settlement of a Tribunal/NIT “shall be executed in accordance with the procedure laid down for execution of orders and decree of a civil court under Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908”, and the Tribunal is deemed a civil court for that purpose.
  2. Prospective and regulatory, not merely compensatory — it governs future conduct (wages, service conditions, work norms) and not merely the past wrong.
  3. Constitutive, not purely declaratory — it creates new rights and obligations which the parties never agreed to; it can “add to or alter the terms or conditions of the contract of service” (Bharat Bank; Western India Automobile Association).
  4. Quasi-judicial in character — hence susceptible to certiorari and Article 136, unlike the decision of a purely domestic or administrative body (Bharat Bank).
  5. Limited in review, not in reach — no court may reopen its merits, but the High Court and Supreme Court may correct jurisdictional error, patent illegality and breach of natural justice (Part C).
Real-world illustrations
  • Wage-board awards in the banking/PSU sector: bipartite settlements between the Indian Banks’ Association and the unions are signed in conciliation before the Chief Labour Commissioner (Central) — precisely to obtain the §18(3)/§57(3) erga omnes binding effect on all bank employees, including those not members of the signatory unions. This is the settlement device used on the largest scale in India.
  • Industrial Tribunal awards as “new contracts”: a Tribunal that re-fixes wage scales, dearness allowance slabs, or leave rules is doing what no civil court can do — it is creating a new service regime for the establishment. That is why the award must be published/communicated and why the Government has a limited, time-bound filter.
  • Interim relief under §50(2) IRC: in a dismissal dispute, a worker dismissed in 2024 can now obtain interim relief from the Industrial Tribunal while the reference is pending — but only on the material already on record, so the employer cannot be ambushed with fresh evidence at the interim stage.
Common mistakes on Awards
  • Saying an award becomes enforceable “from the date it is made/passed” — wrong. It is 30 days from publication (IDA) or communication (IRC).
  • Confusing §15 (duty to submit the award to Government expeditiously) with §17 (publication) and §17-A (enforceability). Three different stages, three different provisions.
  • Assuming the Government’s power under §17-A(1)/§55(3) proviso is available in every case — it is confined to (i) disputes in which the appropriate Government is itself a party and (ii) awards of the National Industrial Tribunal.
  • Assuming a settlement also needs “publication”. It does not; it needs the §2(p)/§2(zi) formalities and filing (Form I under the IR Rules, 2026).
  • Stating that the Government can extend an award’s life indefinitely — the ceiling is three years, and for a settlement there is no Government power of extension at all.

CJudicial Review of Industrial Awards

The core tension of Sub-unit (c)

An award is meant to be final — a dispute that has travelled through conciliation, a failure report, a reference and full adjudication must end somewhere, or industrial peace is defeated by litigation. Yet an award is the act of a statutory tribunal which may err on law, exceed its jurisdiction, or violate natural justice. Sub-unit (c) is the study of how Indian law resolves this tension: finality subject to a narrow, principled power of judicial review. Two propositions must be memorised:

  1. The finality clause (§17(2) IDA) excludes civil courts and ordinary appeals — it does not and cannot exclude Articles 226/227 and 136.
  2. The Supreme Court exercising Article 136 is not a court of error: it does not re-decide the dispute, but it will quash an award vitiated by jurisdictional excess, patent illegality or breach of natural justice (Bharat Bank).

C.2 The Constitutional Avenues

Article 136 — Supreme Court (the Bharat Bank jurisdiction)

Art. 136(1) gives the Supreme Court a discretionary power to grant special leave to appeal “from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India” (excluding courts/tribunals constituted under laws relating to the Armed Forces).

Bharat Bank decided three things about Art. 136: the word “tribunal” is wider than “court” and includes a body with the “trappings of a court”; the word “determination” deliberately widens the reach; and the power is overriding and must be exercised to prevent grave miscarriage of justice — but the Court will not act as a mere court of error.

Articles 226 & 227 — High Courts

Certiorari to quash an award for want or excess of jurisdiction, error of law apparent on the face of the record, or violation of natural justice; prohibition to stop proceedings that are beyond jurisdiction; mandamus to compel implementation, or to compel a Government that has failed to publish/notify, or to compel payment under a §33-C/§59 determination (where the respondent is amenable to writ jurisdiction).

Writ is discretionary: delay, laches, suppressed material facts, an adequate alternative remedy, or the petitioner’s own conduct may defeat it. But the existence of an alternative remedy is not an absolute bar — it is a self-imposed rule of prudence (Workmen of Cochin Port Trust v. Board of Trustees, (1978) 2 SCC 119).

Articles 32 / 14 / 19 / 21

An award which violates a fundamental right can be challenged under Art. 32 (or Art. 226). Awards affecting State instrumentalities must satisfy Art. 14 (non-arbitrariness) and can violate Art. 19(1)(g) if they impose unreasonable restrictions on the employer’s business; Art. 21 issues arise in disciplinary awards (life/livelihood — the cases in Units 4–6).

Article 43-A & the Directive Principles

Part IV informs the interpretation of pre-Constitution labour statutes: Rohtas — “The new light shed by the benign clauses of Part IV must illumine even pre-Independence statutes in the interpretative process.” Workers’ participation in management (Art. 43-A) supports the settlement preference in Sirsilk.

C.3 The Grounds of Judicial Review — the complete catalogue

C.3.1 Ground 1 — Want or excess of jurisdiction

  • An award deciding a matter outside the terms of reference is a nullity: the Tribunal must “confine its adjudication to the points specified and matters incidental thereto” — Delhi Cloth & General Mills v. The Workmen, AIR 1967 SC 469.
  • A reference that itself prejudges the dispute is defective and liable to be quashed with a direction for a fresh reference — TISCO v. State of Jharkhand, (2014) 1 SCC 536.
  • If there is no industrial dispute, the whole adjudication is without jurisdiction — Rohtas Industries (a claim by the employer for damages is not an industrial dispute; §10-A jurisdiction rests on the existence of an industrial dispute and cannot be conferred by consent).
  • Jurisdictional facts (e.g., whether the claimant is a “worker”, whether the establishment is an “industry”) are for the Tribunal to decide; the Government cannot decide them at the reference stage — Telco Convoy Drivers (1989); Sharad Kumar (2002).

C.3.2 Ground 2 — Error of law on the face of the award (the Rohtas doctrine)

This is the ground the prescribed case Rohtas Industries elaborates in the greatest detail. Its content:

  • Jurisdiction exists at common law, independently of statute, to set aside an award for an error of law appearing on the face of the award — though the jurisdiction “is not lightly to be exercised” (Halsbury’s Laws of England, 4th edn., Vol. 2, para 623, p. 334, quoted and adopted).
  • The test: you must be able to find in the award, or in a document actually incorporated with it, “some legal proposition which is the basis of the award and which is erroneous” (Champsey Bhara & Co. v. Jivraj Balloo, per Lord Dunedin); “where the question of law necessarily arises on the face of the award” (Hodkinson v. Verne, per Williams J.); if the finding admits of only one legal proposition as its foundation and that law is erroneous, the court has the power — and therefore the duty — to set it right (James Clark).
  • Sub silentio errors count. The arbitrator may say nothing about the law: “such cute silence confers no greater or subtler immunity … The law sets no premium on juggling with drafting the award or hiding the legal error by blanking out. The inscrutable face of the sphinx has no better title to invulnerability than a speaking face which is a candid index of the mind.”
  • But courts do not speculate: the judge cannot “explore by changing subterranean routes or ferret out by delving deep what lies buried in the unspoken cerebration of the arbitrator”; the error must be transparent, or “lingering between the lines or merely wearing a verbal veil”.
  • If an arbitrator chooses to give reasons, those reasons are examinable; if he chooses to give none, the award is not for that reason bad (Bungo Steel Furniture v. Union of India — Bhargava J.).

C.3.3 Ground 3 — Breach of natural justice

Bharat Bank is the fountainhead. The Tribunal had decided the reference on the strength of the parties’ “statements of case” — not on oath, without cross-examination, and without any evidence. The Court quashed the award, observing that the procedure adopted was “against all principles of natural justice” and that:

“It happens that when the safeguard of an appeal is not provided by law the tendency sometimes is to act in an arbitrary manner like a benevolent despot. Benevolent despotism, however, is foreign to a democratic Constitution … That kind of procedure to my mind is unwarranted by the statute and is foreign to a democratic Constitution.” — M.C. Mahajan J., Bharat Bank Ltd. v. Employees, AIR 1950 SC 188

Related sub-grounds: denial of an opportunity to cross-examine or to lead evidence; refusal to adjourn for a genuine reason; bias (including the enquiry-officer-as-witness problem in the disciplinary context); and non-compliance with a mandatory procedural provision — e.g., Bharat Bank’s second ratio: the award was signed by two of three members without the Government reconstituting the Tribunal under §8, contrary to the then-mandatory §16.

C.3.4 Ground 4 — Perversity, irrationality and non-application of mind

An award may be interfered with where it is “patently illegal”, perverse, or shows a failure to apply the mind to critical constituents of the dispute (course materials on grounds of writ admissibility). But note the standard is high, and the remedy is remand, not substitution:

  • Workmen of Cochin Port Trust v. Board of Trustees, (1978) 2 SCC 119 — The employer’s special leave petition had been dismissed without reasons; it then succeeded before the Kerala High Court in a writ petition. The Supreme Court held that a non-speaking dismissal of an SLP does not preclude the employer from resorting to Article 226; but on the merits, the award disclosed no patent illegality and no breach of natural justice, and the High Court ought not to have interfered. The award was reinstated.
  • Cochin Shipyard Ltd. v. Industrial Tribunal (as discussed in the course materials) — Even where the High Court has power to modify a punishment awarded under §11-A (now §50 IRC), it must refrain from substituting its own judgment unless the award exhibits perverse error. Limited review, high threshold is the formula.

C.4 The Limits of Review — what the courts will not do

  • Re-appreciate evidence. The Tribunal is the final judge of facts; the writ court/Article 136 Court does not re-weigh evidence unless the finding is perverse or based on no evidence.
  • Substitute its own award or punishment. “This Court is not to substitute its decision for the determination of the Tribunal” — Bharat Bank. The remedy is to quash and remand, not to re-adjudicate.
  • Act as a court of error of first resort. Article 136 is discretionary; “extraordinary powers have to be exercised in rare and exceptional cases and on well-known principles” (Bharat Bank).
  • Entertain a challenge on grounds of mere hardship or inconvenience, or on the ground that the court would have reached a different conclusion on a question of construction (Rohtas, citing Halsbury).
  • Bypass the statutory machinery where the Act is self-contained. Where the right and the remedy are given by the same statute “uno flatu”, the statutory remedy ordinarily prevails — Rohtas, citing Barraclough v. Brown and Premier Automobiles Ltd. v. Kamlekar Shantaram; but this does not oust constitutional remedies.
  • Grant relief if the petitioner is guilty of delay/laches or of suppressing material facts — an equitable jurisdiction.

C.6 Judicial Review of Arbitration Awards under §10-A / §42 — the Rohtas Scenario

  1. An arbitration award is an “award” for all purposes (§2(b) IDA / §2(e) IRC) — published (§17(1)) / communicated (§55(2)) and enforceable under §17-A / §55(3) in the same way. Hence it is reviewed on the same principles as a Tribunal award.
  2. The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 does not apply — IRC §42(8) expressly so provides (and this was the position in substance under the IDA too, as the Rohtas discussion shows: “we are not concerned with a private arbitration but a statutory one … deriving its validity, enforceability and protective mantle … from §10-A”).
  3. Consent cannot create jurisdiction. The reference must be of an existing or apprehended industrial dispute. “No industrial dispute, no valid arbitral reference” — Rohtas (obiter). Consequently, an arbitrator cannot be clothed with power by the parties’ agreement to decide a claim which is outside §2(k) — e.g., a management’s claim for damages against strikers.
  4. The remedy for an illegal strike lies inside the Act — prosecution under §26 (now the penalty provisions of the Code for illegal strikes, §86(13)–(16)). The Act, being a self-contained code, gives “the right and the remedy … uno flatu”; you cannot sue in tort for conspiracy or bring an action outside the statute.
  5. But an award that travels outside the Act is reviewable for error of law on its face — which is precisely how the Supreme Court disposed of the compensation award in Rohtas without needing to decide the jurisdiction question finally.

C.7 Review of the Reference to a National Industrial Tribunal — FDC Ltd. v. Union of India

FDC Ltd. v. Union of India, 136 (2007) DLT 266

Context (the course cross-reference): the case material notes that the power to refer to a National Industrial Tribunal under §7-B of the IDA is “pari materia” with §54 read with §46 of the IR Code (Central Government’s power to refer, to an NIT, a dispute involving questions of national importance or establishments in more than one State).

The problem: the Central Government referred to a National Industrial Tribunal the individual termination disputes of 58 medical representatives of one company — workers spread over several States, each with his own facts, several of whom had already raised separate disputes before State Labour Courts/Tribunals, and 24 of whom had raised no dispute at all.

The challenge: (i) the notification was vitiated by non-application of mind — no material demonstrated that the dispute was of “national importance” or that establishments in more than one State were interested in or affected by it; (ii) the mere fact that the employees of one establishment worked in more than one State does not convert an individual termination dispute into one affecting establishments situated in more than one State; (iii) the power under §7-B must be exercised in a guided and rational manner, and the notification must show the basis of the satisfaction.

Principle to extract: the power to constitute/refer to a National Industrial Tribunal is a special jurisdiction, exercisable only on the statutory precondition — national importance, or the interest/effect on establishments in more than one State — and the exercise of it is amenable to judicial review for absence of material and non-application of mind. Under the Code the same discipline applies to §46 (constitution of NITs) read with §54 (reference by the Central Government).

C.8 A Practical Check-list for a Review Problem

  1. Is the instrument an award, a settlement, or neither? Fix the status first (§2(b)/§2(e) or §2(p)/§2(zi)); an instrument that is not a statutory settlement cannot enjoy §18(3)/§57(3) effect.
  2. Has it become enforceable? Only an enforceable award/settlement binds under §18(3)/§57(3). Watch the 30-day rule and any §17-A/§55(3) notification.
  3. Is the defect one of the four grounds? (jurisdiction / error of law on the face / natural justice / perversity). Quote the leading authority for each.
  4. Which court, which remedy? High Court under Arts. 226/227 (certiorari/prohibition/mandamus); Supreme Court under Art. 136. Consider alternative remedy, delay, and the self-contained-code doctrine.
  5. What relief is appropriate? Quashing + remand; reinstatement of the award; a direction to publish/notify; or (in the Government-filter case) striking down/reading down the notification.
  6. Add the modern layer: note the IRC equivalent of each step (§55(2) communication; §55(3)–(6) enforceability/filter; §95 interpretation; §97 civil-court bar) and, where relevant, the constitutional doubt over §55(3)–(6).

① Bharat Bank Ltd. v. Employees CASE No. 8 · Unit 3

AIR 1950 SC 188 · 1950 SCR 459 · Appeal by special leave under Article 136 · Bench: M.C. Mahajan J. (delivering the leading opinion, with separate opinions of the other Judges) · Decided 1950
Context — why this case is the first page of Unit 3

Bharat Bank is the first case in which the Supreme Court granted special leave to appeal against the determination of an Industrial Tribunal under Article 136. The whole question was whether it could do so at all. The answer fixed the constitutional status of industrial adjudication in India: an Industrial Tribunal is a “tribunal” within Article 136, its award is a “determination”, and its awards are therefore subject to the Supreme Court’s overriding power of judicial review — but only on the limited grounds on which such power is exercised. Everything in Sub-unit (c) of Unit 3 begins here.

Facts
  1. Bharat Bank Ltd., Delhi, a company registered under the Indian Companies Act, had an industrial dispute with its employees. The employees made certain demands; receiving an unfavourable response, they struck work on 9 March 1949.
  2. The Bank served notices on the striking employees to resume work. When they did not, the Bank discharged a number of them between 19 March and 24 March 1949.
  3. The Central Government constituted a Tribunal of three persons for the adjudication of industrial disputes in banking companies under §7 of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, and referred the disputes mentioned in Schedule II of the notification to it under §10. Item 18 of the Schedule read: “Retrenchment and victimization (specific cases to be cited by employees).”
  4. The Tribunal heard the dispute at Delhi and made its award on 19 January 1950. It was published in the Gazette of India dated 4 February 1950 and declared binding for one year.
  5. The award was signed by only two of the three members — the third (Mr Chandrasekhara Aiyar) had been appointed a member of the Boundary Commission in Bengal in November 1949. The other two members had asked the Labour Ministry to fill the vacancy or reconstitute the Tribunal; the Ministry advised them to proceed as they were. The Tribunal was never reconstituted by notification.
  6. On the merits, the Tribunal had proceeded without taking any evidence. At the first sitting, as Rule 17 required, it called upon the parties to “state their cases”. Mr Parwana stated the employees’ case (victimisation in individual cases) and Mr Ved Vyas stated the Bank’s case; the parties then argued, and the Tribunal straightaway made its award. Parwana’s statement was not on oath; there was no examination or cross-examination; no affidavit was filed by him or any employee; the original correspondence was not produced. The Bank had disputed the facts by a lengthy affidavit, which the Tribunal did not even refer to, and the Bank’s request to lead evidence (about a scurrilous letter circulated by the employees) was refused.
  7. The Bank applied for special leave under Article 136. The Central Government and the respondents raised a preliminary objection to jurisdiction: the Supreme Court could not grant special leave against the determination of an Industrial Tribunal, since the Tribunal did not exercise the judicial powers of the State and its determination was not a “judgment, decree or order of a court”.
Issues
  1. Preliminary/jurisdictional: Does the word “tribunal” in Article 136 include an Industrial Tribunal constituted under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947?
  2. Does the word “determination” in Article 136 include the award of an Industrial Tribunal — particularly when, under the statutory scheme as it then stood, the award acquired binding force only upon a declaration by the appropriate Government under §15(2) of the Act?
  3. If Article 136 applies, should the Court interfere in the present case — on the grounds of (i) misconstruction of “victimization”, (ii) reinstatement of persons guilty of an illegal strike, (iii) an award based on no evidence, and (iv) an award signed by only two of three members?
Arguments
For the Central Government & respondents (objecting) — Mr Alladi Krishnaswami Aiyar
  1. The expression “tribunal” means the seat of a Judge, a court of justice; its necessary attribute is the power to give a final judgment between two parties carrying legal sanction of its own force. Placed in juxtaposition with “court”, it can only mean a body exercising the judicial functions of the State — not one with merely quasi-judicial or administrative powers.
  2. The orders against which special leave lies must be of the same nature as those passed by a court: there must be a judicial determination of a controversy between two parties.
  3. What gives binding force to an Industrial Tribunal’s award is the Government’s declaration; “the spark of life to it is given by that declaration and without that, the award is lifeless and has no enforceability” — hence it cannot be an appealable determination.
  4. In disputes between the Government and its employees the award can even be rejected by the Government; a body whose determination can be so rejected cannot, by its own act, impose liabilities or affect rights.
  5. If the Tribunal exceeded its statutory jurisdiction, the remedy lies elsewhere — under Article 226 by certiorari — not under Article 136.
For the Bank (appellant) — Dr Bakshi Tek Chand
  1. Whenever a tribunal — judicial or quasi-judicial — determines a matter in a judicial manner, that determination falls within Article 136.
  2. An Industrial Tribunal has no administrative or executive functions; its duty is to adjudicate an industrial dispute, i.e., to act as a Judge. Its functions are judicial in nature, though its powers are wider than an ordinary court’s because it may grant reliefs no court could grant — but that flows from the statute.
  3. The plain words of Article 136 should not be given a narrow meaning when the Constitution intended to confer the widest power on the Supreme Court; the words “determination” and “tribunal” were deliberately inserted.
  4. As between private employers and employees — and even in certain cases between the Government and its employees — the decision of the Tribunal is binding on the Government, which has no power to affirm, modify or reject it; it may only announce it and give it enforceability. That does not affect appealability.
  5. If the intention was that “tribunal” should bear the same meaning as “court”, the word would have been unnecessary — because all tribunals exercising judicial functions already fall within the definition of “court”.
  6. Article 136 is meant to be exercised wherever there is a miscarriage of justice by the determination of any tribunal.
Decision (Holding)
  • The preliminary objection was overruled. An Industrial Tribunal is a “tribunal” within the meaning of Article 136; the application for special leave is competent.
  • On the merits, the award was quashed on two independent grounds: (i) the procedure adopted by the Tribunal was contrary to all principles of natural justice — the award was based on no evidence; and (ii) the award was signed by only two of the three members of the Tribunal, the Tribunal never having been reconstituted as §8 required, so that “the award given by two of them is not the award of the Tribunal constituted by the Government”.
  • The Supreme Court directed that the Tribunal — which was still functioning — re-adjudicate Item 18 of the reference and submit its award on that point to the Government. The employees were not to be penalised for the Tribunal’s procedure.
  • The Court declined to interfere on the other two grounds pressed by the Bank (the interpretation of “victimization”, and the reinstatement of persons said to be guilty of an illegal strike), holding that these were not of such a vital character as to justify special leave — because this Court is not a mere court of error.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. Article 136 covers an Industrial Tribunal. The word “tribunal” is wider than “court”: it takes in bodies “adorned with similar trappings as a court but strictly not coming within that definition”. The condition precedent is that the body (a) is constituted by the State, and (b) is invested with part of the judicial functions of the State, i.e., it decides litigated questions according to law; bodies exercising purely administrative or executive functions are outside.
  2. The word “determination” was placed in Article 136 to widen its reach; it includes the determination of an Industrial Tribunal.
  3. A confirmatory or declaratory step by the executive does not affect appealability. Where the Government’s function is automatic — §15(2) left it no discretion to affirm, modify or reject — the rights of the parties are affected by the Tribunal’s adjudication, not by the Government’s declaration (relying on Rex v. Electricity Commissioners: “I know of no authority which compels me to hold that a proceeding cannot be a judicial proceeding subject to confirmation or approval”; and Rex v. Minister of Health: confirmation by a Minister does not affect the power of judicial review).
  4. The Supreme Court under Article 136 is not a court of error. It interferes because the Tribunal has exceeded its jurisdiction, or has approached the questions in a manner likely to result in injustice, or has adopted a procedure contrary to the well-established rules of natural justice — “if it has denied a hearing to a party or has refused to record his evidence or has acted in any other manner in an arbitrary or despotic fashion”. The Court then quashes the award and directs the Tribunal to decide afresh; it does not substitute its own decision.
  5. Non-compliance with a mandatory procedural provision vitiates the award. On the section as it then stood, §16 required the award to be signed by all the members; “the idea of three persons hearing a case and two of them deciding it is repugnant to all notions of fairness”. Where a member becomes unavailable and the Government does not fill the vacancy, the Government must still reconstitute the Tribunal under §8; otherwise the body that signs is not the body that was constituted.
Principles & Observations (a goldmine for essays)
  • Industrial adjudication is judicial, not administrative. The Tribunal “has the first three requisites and characteristics of a court and a considerable element of the fourth”; it applies “the law of the land”, though a law different from that administered by ordinary courts; it is composed of persons qualified to be Judges; it is deemed a civil court for certain purposes; and disobedience of its award is punishable under §29. It therefore exercises judicial power.
  • The constitutive character of industrial adjudication (approved from Western India Automobile Association v. Industrial Tribunal, Bombay, (1949) FCR 321): a tribunal of this nature “can do what no court can, namely, add to or alter the terms or conditions of the contract of service”. This is why industrial adjudication is not ordinary arbitration — the arbitrator under the IDA creates obligations in the interest of industrial peace.
  • Natural justice in industrial adjudication: the Act and the Rules contemplate a proper hearing, discovery and inspection of documents and production of evidence; a Tribunal cannot decide on the parties’ “statements of case” alone. The famous dictum: “when the safeguard of an appeal is not provided by law the tendency sometimes is to act in an arbitrary manner like a benevolent despot. Benevolent despotism, however, is foreign to a democratic Constitution.”
  • Remedy under Article 226 does not bar Article 136. “Whenever judicial review is permissible in one form or another, this Court as the highest court in the land can exercise its special powers … What it has to ultimately decide it can decide earlier.” In an industrial dispute, where expedition is the crux, abuse of power by tribunals must be corrected with expedition.
  • Construction of Article 136: “notwithstanding anything in this Chapter” gives the power an overriding character; it can be exercised even against interlocutory orders and against orders of courts subordinate to High Courts.
  • On the merits (dicta): (i) “victimization” is an ordinary English word meaning that a person has been unjustly dealt with; it is not defined in the statute; and the Tribunal’s interpretation did not materially affect its determination, so no interference. (ii) The Act nowhere says that persons guilty of an illegal strike cannot be reinstated; the consequences of an illegal strike are those laid down in the Act, and the Tribunal had jurisdiction to deal with victimisation arising out of the strike. (iii) The Bank’s contentions on these points did not warrant interference — a lesson in self-restraint.
How to use Bharat Bank in the exam
  • Art. 136 / status of the Tribunal (Unit 1 & 3, PYQ May–June 2025 Q.1, Nov–Dec 2025 Q.1): cite it for the proposition that an Industrial Tribunal is a tribunal under Art. 136; give the twin test (State-constituted + judicial functions); mention Shell Co. of Australia, R. v. London County Council, Cooper v. Wilson, Huddart Parker (adopted); and the “confirmatory declaration” reasoning.
  • Natural justice (Unit 3 & 4): the “benevolent despot” passage is the single most quotable line; pair it with the “no evidence” facts.
  • Mandatory procedure (§16): cite it for the proposition that a defective signature/award is a nullity — and note the 1956 change (only the presiding officer need now sign; IRC §55(1) requires electronic or other signature by the member(s) delivering the award).
  • Nature of the award: use the case with Western India Automobile Association for the “constitutive” character of the award — it creates obligations which no court could create.
Precision guards
  • Bharat Bank was decided when §16 required the signature of all members and §15(2) required a Government declaration. Do not state the case as if it were decided under today’s §16 or under §17/§17-A, which were introduced/amended in 1956. The principle survives; the statutory setting does not.
  • The Court did not hold that every error of law justifies interference. It expressly refused to interfere on two of the four grounds — the case is as much about restraint as about intervention.

② Rohtas Industries Ltd. & Anr. v. Rohtas Industries Staff Union & Ors. CASE No. 9 · Unit 3

1976 AIR 425 · 1976 SCR (3) 12 · Appeal by special leave under Article 136 from the High Court (writ petitions under Article 226) · Bench: V.R. Krishna Iyer, Y.V. Chandrachud and A.C. Gupta JJ. · Judgment delivered by Krishna Iyer J.
Context — why this case is prescribed

Everything a student needs on the judicial review of an award is in this judgment: (i) the writ power under Article 226 over a §10-A arbitration award; (ii) the ground of “error of law on the face of the award”, with its full English ancestry and the bold extension to sub silentio errors; (iii) the limits of transplanting English tort law (conspiracy) into Indian industrial law; and (iv) the doctrine that the Industrial Disputes Act is a self-contained code in which right and remedy are given uno flatu. It also contains the course’s only judicial discussion of whether an award can give the employer damages against strikers — a question no Indian tribunal had ever before answered in the affirmative.

Facts
  1. Two connected managements of industries in the same locality (the appellants) had a running industrial dispute with their workers, with a long history “moving in a zigzag course” and sicklied over by internecine trade-union rivalry. There were two unions: the Rohtas Industries Mazdoor Sangh (the representative union during the relevant period) and the Rohtas Industries Seva Sangh (not a party before the Supreme Court).
  2. A strike in the industry came to an end by virtue of a memorandum of agreement dated 2 October 1957, to which the managements, the two registered unions and two unregistered unions with a lesser following were parties. The agreement was arrived at during conciliation proceedings under the IDA.
  3. Clause 7 of that agreement provided that the employees’ claim for wages and salaries for the strike period and the company’s claim for compensation for losses due to the strike “shall be submitted for arbitration of Sri J.N. Majumdar and Sri R.C. Mitter, ex-High Court Judges and ex-Members of the Labour Appellate Tribunal of India, as joint arbitrators and their decisions … shall be final and binding on all the parties”. The reference fell squarely under §10-A.
  4. The arbitrators — two retired Judges of the Calcutta High Court — held hearings spread over a year and a half and made a lengthy, reasoned award (a “speaking award” both on facts and law). Their award was: (1) the workmen participating in the strike were not entitled to wages/salaries for the strike period; (2) the Company do recover from the workmen participating in the strike compensation — assessed at Rs 80,000 in one case and Rs 6,90,000 in the other — for the loss of profits suffered by the manufacturing business; (3) the workmen do jointly and severally pay one-eighth of the costs of the arbitration.
  5. The workmen were deprived of strike wages on the footing that the strike was illegal (conciliation proceedings were pending: §§23 and 24). Both sides ultimately accepted that finding. The compensation direction, however, was challenged by the Mazdoor Sangh by two writ petitions.
  6. The High Court quashed that part of the award which directed payment of compensation by the workers to the management. Both sides had appealed — 12 years elapsed between the grant of special leave and the final hearing. By then: the generation of workers who struck work two decades earlier had “all but retired”; the representative union had apparently ceased to command representative character; and the managements had disclaimed any intention to recover the huge sums awarded.
  7. Counsel for the appellants told the Court at the outset that his clients were inclined to abandon realisation of the entire compensation even if the Court upheld that part of the award — “He fought a battle for principle, not pecunia.” The appeal therefore became a vehicle for important questions of law.
Issues
  1. Is an award under §10-A (voluntary arbitration) amenable to correction by the High Court under Article 226 — or does it savour of private arbitration and lie outside the writ jurisdiction? If the jurisdiction exists, should the Court exercise it?
  2. Does the compensation part of the award suffer from any recognised ground of interference — in particular, an error of law on the face of the award? Specifically, is an illegal strike, as such, an actionable wrong (the English tort of conspiracy) for which the strikers are liable in damages for the employer’s loss of profits?
  3. Is the High Court’s reasoning correct on (i) the “mixed motives” flaw in the award; (ii) the exclusion of remedies other than prosecution under §26; and (iii) the implied immunity from all legal proceedings against strikers said to arise from §18 of the Trade Unions Act, 1926?
  4. (Obiter — raised and discussed) Is a claim by an employer against his workmen for compensation for loss of profits an “industrial dispute” within §2(k), and therefore arbitrable under §10-A at all?
Arguments
For the appellants (the managements)
  1. Article 226 does not reach a §10-A award: an award under §10-A “savours of a private arbitration” and is not amenable to correction under Article 226.
  2. Even if the jurisdiction exists, discretionary desistance from its exercise is wise and in consonance with the canons of restraint laid down by the Supreme Court.
  3. The compensation awarded by the arbitrators suffers from no vice that is a recognised ground for the High Court’s interference — the arbitrators recorded reasons, discussed the law and marshalled the evidence; the award is a speaking one.
  4. The High Court was wrong on all three of the grounds it adopted — mixed motives, the exclusivity of §26, and the immunity under §18 of the Trade Unions Act.
  5. (Concession) the appellants would not in fact recover the compensation — they sought a ruling on principle.

Supporting citations for the “enforceability of an award outside the Arbitration Act” point: decisions were cited to show that an award falling outside the orbit of the Indian Arbitration Act can be enforced by action in court — the Court held the problem did not arise.

For the respondents (the Staff Union), supporting the High Court — incl. Shri B.C. Ghosh
  1. An “illegal strike” is a creation of the Act, not of the common law. Once the Act creates the right and provides the remedy, the remedy must be sought within the statute. The designated statutory remedy for an illegal strike is prosecution under §26; no other relief can be claimed “on general principles of jurisprudence”.
  2. The compensation awarded is a direct reparation for loss of profits caused by the illegal strike — i.e., a claim de hors the Act and outside the scope of a §10-A reference, which is limited to an “industrial dispute”.
  3. The Industrial Disputes Act is a comprehensive and self-contained Code, as held in Premier Automobiles; the civil courts and other tribunals cannot award relief on rights created by the Act except through its own procedure.
  4. Reliance was placed on the High Court’s reasoning that §18 of the Trade Unions Act, 1926 confers immunity from civil suits for acts done in contemplation or furtherance of a trade dispute — a ground the Supreme Court heard but ultimately declined to rest its judgment upon.
Decision (Holding)
  • The appeals were dismissed — the High Court’s excision of the compensation portion of the award was upheld; there was no order as to costs (the Court recorded the “elegiac” 12-year lapse and the abandonment of recovery).
  • Article 226 is available against a §10-A award. The award of a statutory arbitrator under §10-A is not beyond the “legal reach” of Article 226; indeed it is “not only not invulnerable but more sensitively susceptible to the writ lancet, being a quasi-statutory body’s decision”.
  • The compensation award was bad for a patent error of law: the arbitrators treated the illegality of the strike as per se creating liability in damages for loss of profits — a proposition of law which is erroneous on the face of the award, because the tort of conspiracy (the only possible basis) requires that the predominant object of the combination be to injure the employer.
  • Obiter: the Court expressed concurrence with the view — without pronouncing finally — that a claim for compensation for loss of business is not an “industrial dispute” under §2(k), and that a §10-A reference is restricted to existing or apprehended industrial disputes; “no industrial dispute, no valid arbitral reference”; consent cannot create arbitral jurisdiction. It also noted the asymmetry that §33-C provides for recovery by a workman from an employer, but not the converse.
  • The Court expressly declined to rest its decision on §18 of the Trade Unions Act, although it heard counsel (B.C. Ghosh) in support of the High Court’s wider use of it.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. Writ jurisdiction over §10-A awards: Article 226 is “as wide as the amplitude of the language used”; it can affect any person, even a private individual, and is available even where another remedy exists. It is one thing to affirm the jurisdiction and another “to authorise its free exercise like a bull in a China shop”; the Court must keep the power on a “severely judicious leash”. Applying Engineering Mazdoor Sabha v. Hind Cycles Ltd. (Gajendragadkar J.): Article 226 is “in a sense, wider than Article 136” because it is not confined to orders of courts or tribunals; therefore a writ may lie against the award of a §10-A arbitrator even if he is not a “tribunal” under Article 136. After the 1964 amendment (Act 36 of 1964), the statutory arbitrator can bind even non-parties; he is “part of the methodology of the sovereign’s dispensation of justice, thus falling within the rainbow of statutory tribunals amenable to judicial review”. An award under §10-A can be upset if an apparent error of law stains its face; the distinction between a private award and a §10-A award is “fine, but real”.
  2. Error of law on the face of the award — the doctrine: adopting Halsbury’s Laws of England (4th edn., Vol. 2, para 623, p. 334), an award may be set aside for an error of law appearing on its face, though the jurisdiction is not lightly exercised; there must be found in the award, or in a document actually incorporated with it, “some legal proposition which is the basis of the award and which is erroneous”. The court asks whether the arbitrator has “tied himself down to an obviously unsound legal proposition”. Following Bungo Steel Furniture: if the arbitrator gives no reasons, the award is not for that reason vitiated; but if he gives reasons, they are examinable. Following Champsey Bhara (Lord Dunedin), Hodkinson v. Verne (Williams J.), James Clark (Tucker J.) and Bharat Barrel & Drum, the Court extended the doctrine so that a question of law arising ex facie or sub silentio from the facts found is enough; the award “bears its condemnation on its bosom”; minimal judicialisation by a laconic statement of the governing law is desirable.
  3. Illegality of a strike is not per se an actionable tort: even in English law, combination is actionable only where the real and predominant purpose is to injure the plaintiff; where the object is to advance the defendants’ own lawful interests (here, to bring the employer to terms or to outdo a rival union), there is no actionable conspiracy (Sorrel v. Smith; Mogul Steamship; Allen v. Flood; Quinn v. Leathem; Crofter; Rookes v. Barnard; Salmond). The English tort of conspiracy “is a response to the societal requirements of the industrial civilisation of the 19th Century England” and cannot be “bodily borrowed” into Indian law: “A tort transplant into a social organism is as complex and careful an operation as a heart-transplant into an individual organism … Here, judges are sociological surgeons.” Indian conditions, the Constitution’s guarantee of association, and the history of organised boycotts and mass satyagraha require adaptation. Sabotage — wilful destruction of plant or machinery — remains actionable.
  4. Application: the arbitrators “simply did not investigate or pass upon the object of the strike”; they proceeded on the erroneous proposition that “if the strike is illegal, the tort of conspiracy is made out”. Counsel conceded the object was inter-union rivalry. There was thus “a clear lapse in the law on the part of the arbitrators manifest on the face of the award” — a patent error.
  5. The Act is a self-contained code: the IDA is comprehensive “so far as it speaks”; rights created by it must be enforced through its own procedure (Premier Automobiles Ltd. v. Kamlekar Shantaram, per Untwalia J.). Lord Tenterden’s rule in Doe v. Bridges, and Lord Watson in Barraclough v. Brown: “The right and the remedy are given uno flatu and one cannot be dissociated from the other.” Hence the remedy for an illegal strike — a concept created by §24 — must be sought exclusively in §26.
Principles & Observations (obiter and policy)
  • On the arbitrability of an employer’s claim (obiter): §10-A is restricted to existing or apprehended industrial disputes; “the consent of the parties cannot create arbitral jurisdiction under the Act”. A claim by an employer for compensation for loss of business is not a dispute “connected with the employment or non-employment or the terms of employment or with the conditions of labour”; the Court could not imagine “a tort liability or compensation claim based on loss of business being regarded as an industrial dispute” — having regard to the language, the setting and purpose of the statute and the “industrial flavour” of the dispute between management and workmen. Support: §33-C provides for speedy recovery of money due to a workman from an employer, “but not for the converse case”; “It may be a little startling to find such a provision, having regard to workmen being the weaker section and Part IV of the Constitution being loaded in their favour.”
  • Constitutional interpretative principle: “The new light shed by the benign clauses of Part IV must illumine even pre-Independence statutes in the interpretative process.” (Directive Principles as an aid to construing the IDA.)
  • Policy call for legislative reform: the Court suggested a legislative restatement to silence “possible mischief flowing from the confused state of the law, and remembering how dangerous it would be if long, protracted but technically illegal strikes were to be followed by claims by managements for compensation for loss of profits”. It also noted the criticism of the tort of conspiracy in England (J.T. Cameron, (1965) 28 Modern Law Review 448).
  • On delay and judicial administration: the “elegiac note” — a dispute rooted in 1948 came to the Supreme Court 12 years after leave; “industrial law in India has not fully lived up to the current challenges of industrial life … The legislative and judicial processes have promises to keep if positive industrial peace, in tune with distributive economic justice and continuity of active production, were to be accomplished.”
  • On brevity in judgments: where the superior court is affirming for substantially the same reasons as the court below, “there is no need to give lengthy reasons for dismissing the appeal”.
  • Related authority on the nature of industrial arbitration: Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. Brijnandan Pandey, (1956) SCR 800 — “A court of law proceeds on the footing that no power exists in the courts to make contracts for people … An Industrial Tribunal is not so fettered and may create new obligations or modify contracts in the interests of industrial peace, to protect legitimate trade union activities and to prevent unfair practice or victimisation.”
How to use Rohtas Industries in the exam
  • Judicial review of a §10-A award (PYQ Dec 2024 Q.4(b)): the answer is a three-layer structure — (1) Art. 226 is available, with Hind Cycles and the 1964 amendment reasoning; (2) the ground is error of law on the face, with Halsbury + Champsey Bhara + Bungo Steel + the sub silentio extension; (3) the merits: conspiracy requires a predominant intention to injure; an illegal strike is remediable only under §26.
  • Natural justice / Art. 226 discretion: quote “bull in a China shop” and “severely judicious leash” — examiners reward this.
  • Self-contained code: link to IRC §97 (bar of civil courts) and to Premier Automobiles; the same reasoning explains why compensation/recovery is routed through §33-C (IDA) / §59 (IRC).
  • Employer’s claim against strikers: the obiter is the most “modern” part — use it in any question on the reach of §2(k)/§2(q), on tort claims in industrial relations, or on the “asymmetry” of the Act.
  • Comparative/legal-reform question: cite the Court’s own suggestion of legislative restatement, the criticism of conspiracy as a tort, and the “heart-transplant / sociological surgeons” metaphor.
Precision guards
  • The Supreme Court did not decide the §2(k)/jurisdiction point — it is expressly obiter (“we are not called upon to pronounce conclusively”; “we do not pronounce finally as it is not necessary”). Say so.
  • The Court did not accept the Trade Unions Act §18 ground as part of its ratio; nor did it hold that the compensation claim was barred by §18.
  • The strike was illegal because conciliation was pending (§§23, 24) — the Court said “it is agreed by both sides”. Do not invent the ground.
  • Note the amounts: Rs 80,000 in one case and Rs 6,90,000 in the other, plus one-eighth costs. The total “bonanza” was abandoned by the managements.

D-3The Sirsilk Problem — “Can the Government withhold publication of an award?”

Not one of the two prescribed case numbers, but the principal reading for Unit 3 in the course’s Exam Capsule and the subject of PYQs in all three of the last three papers. Treat it as a prescribed case.

Sirsilk Ltd. v. Government of Andhra Pradesh

(1964) 2 SCR 448 · AIR 1964 SC 160 · Bench: K.N. Wanchoo J. (and others) · Writ petitions from the Andhra Pradesh High Court
Facts
  1. An order referring certain disputes between the appellant (Sirsilk Ltd.) and its workmen was made to the Industrial Tribunal on 6 June 1956.
  2. The Tribunal finished the adjudication and sent its award to the Government in September 1957. Under §17(1), the appropriate Government must publish the award within thirty days of its receipt.
  3. Before the Government could publish the award, the parties to the dispute arrived at a settlement. On 1 October 1957, a letter signed jointly on behalf of the employer and the employees was sent to the Government, intimating that the dispute pending before the Tribunal had been settled and requesting the Government not to publish the award.
  4. The Government expressed its inability to withhold publication, taking the view that §17 was mandatory and that it was bound to publish the award.
  5. The appellants filed writ petitions under Article 226 seeking a direction that the Government not publish the award. The High Court dismissed the petitions, holding §17 mandatory and that it was not open to the Government to withhold publication.
  6. Sirsilk appealed to the Supreme Court.
Issues
  1. Is §17 of the Act, when it provides for the publication of an award, directory and not mandatory?
  2. Where there is a settlement which is binding under §18(1), would it be open to the Government not to publish the award in these special circumstances?
Arguments
For the appellant (Sirsilk / the parties who had settled)
  1. §17(1) is directory: the Government has a discretion in special circumstances to withhold publication; if it were mandatory, a settlement — itself expressly binding under §18(1) — would be defeated by an instrument (the award) which had not yet come into force.
  2. The Legislature could not have intended that a binding settlement under §18(1) be overridden by a conflicting published award under §18(3). The two provisions can be reconciled only by withholding the award.
  3. The main purpose of the Act is to maintain peace in an industrial concern. Where parties who were before the Tribunal have settled, the dispute really comes to an end; publication of an award which may differ from the settlement can only disturb industrial peace.
  4. A settlement is “likely to lead to more lasting peace than an award, as it is arrived at by the free will of the parties and is a pointer to there being goodwill between them”.
  5. If the award is published, the parties are subject to two inconsistent binding instruments — a legal absurdity, since neither can comply with both.
For the Government of Andhra Pradesh
  1. §17(1) uses “shall” — the obligation is mandatory; the Government has no discretion to decline publication. The words “in such manner as the appropriate Government thinks fit” give discretion only as to manner, not as to the duty.
  2. §17(2) — the published award “shall be final and shall not be called in question by any court in any manner whatsoever” — and §17-A, which makes the award enforceable 30 days after publication, both presuppose mandatory publication; they would be meaningless if the Government could refuse to publish.
  3. The Act itself provides the only exceptional mechanism: §17-A(1) permits the Government to declare (in specified cases) that an award shall not become enforceable. The Government cannot invent a further, unspecified power to withhold publication.
  4. If the settlement is later disputed, a published award provides stability and a ready answer.
Decision (Holding)
  • §17(1) is mandatory as to the obligation to publish. The word “shall” was intended to give a mandate to the Government to publish the award within the time fixed. This is “enforced by the fact that sub-section (2) of §17 provides that the published award shall be final and shall not be called in question by any court in any manner whatsoever. Obviously when the legislature intended the award on publication to be final, it could not have intended that the Government concerned had the power to withhold publication of the award.”
  • But the situation before the Court was exceptional, requiring a reconciliation between the mandatory character of §17(1) and the equally mandatory character of §18(1) — under which the settlement arrived at on 1 October 1957, not fixing any date, became operative from that day itself and bound the parties who were also before the Tribunal.
  • The only way to reconcile the two provisions is to withhold publication of the award, “as a binding settlement has already come into force in order to avoid possible conflict between a binding settlement under §18(1) and a binding award under §18(3)”.
  • The Court accordingly allowed the appeals and directed the Government not to publish the awards sent to it by the Industrial Tribunal in these cases, in view of the binding nature of the settlements arrived at between the parties under §18(1).
  • If any dispute arises as to the validity of the settlement (fraud, misrepresentation, etc.), that is a fresh industrial dispute which the Government may refer; and if the settlement is not binding, the withheld award may then be published.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. Two-limb rule: (i) the obligation to publish under §17(1) is mandatory; (ii) yet in the exceptional case where, before publication, the parties jointly inform the Government that a binding §18(1) settlement covering the same matters has come into force, the Government should withhold publication to avoid an irreconcilable conflict between §18(1) and §18(3).
  2. The normal (and better) course is for the parties, if they settle while the dispute is pending, to place the settlement before the Tribunal so that the Tribunal makes an award in terms of the settlement. The exceptional rule applies only where that has not happened and the award is already with the Government.
  3. Policy foundation: the Act’s purpose is industrial peace, and a voluntary settlement is preferable to an imposed award — “The settlement is likely to lead to more lasting peace than an award.”
Conditions for the exception (write these as a check-list)
  • The settlement must be a valid §2(p) settlement — written, signed in the prescribed manner, and (for the second limb) a copy sent to the authorised officer and the Conciliation Officer. Under the IR Rules, 2026 it must be in Form I.
  • It must have come into operation — §19(1)/§58(1): from the agreed date or the date of signing.
  • Both parties must jointly inform the Government. A request by one party alone, or a settlement of doubtful validity, does not attract the exception — the Government should then publish.
  • The settlement must cover the same subject-matter as the award, so that publication would create a real conflict.

The Remington Rand of India Ltd. v. The Workmen

(1968) 1 SCR 164 · AIR 1968 SC 224 · Bench: G.K. Mitter J. (and others) · Appeal against the award of the Industrial Tribunal, Alleppey
Facts
  1. The Industrial Tribunal, Alleppey, made the award. The gazette notification accompanying publication recorded that the Government had received the award on 14 October 1966.
  2. Counsel calculated that the award ought to have been published at the latest on 12 November 1966 — the thirtieth day from receipt. It was in fact published in the Kerala Gazette on 15 November 1966, beyond the thirty-day period (the examination favourite, the “31st-day publication” problem).
  3. The management took the point that the award could not be given effect to at all, because §17(1) was mandatory and the time limit had expired.
Issue

Is the provision as to time in §17(1) — the thirty-day period for publication — mandatory, so that publication beyond thirty days renders the award invalid and unenforceable?

Arguments
For the management (appellant)
  1. “Within a period of thirty days” is a time-bound, mandatory direction.
  2. §17(2) makes only an award “published under sub-section (1)” final — if sub-section (1) has not been complied with, the award is not “published under” it and is not final or binding.
  3. §§17-A and 19 construct a time-based structure (publication → 30 days → enforcement → one year’s operation). Late publication distorts the whole structure.
  4. Sirsilk held §17(1) mandatory; the same logic applies to the time limit.
For the workmen (respondents)
  1. The purpose of the thirty-day limit is to ensure expeditious publication; it is directory, not a condition of validity.
  2. No penalty is provided for late publication — the omission tells against mandatory construction.
  3. Genuine reasons may prevent publication within thirty days (administrative difficulties, a strike in the press). The legislature could not have intended that a strike in a press should nullify an award.
  4. Holding late publication fatal would force a fresh reference and cause “needless harassment” to the parties — a disproportionate consequence.
  5. Sirsilk concerned the obligation to publish (the Government cannot choose to withhold); the period within which to publish is a separate question.
Decision (Holding)
  • The provision as to time in §17(1) is merely directory and not mandatory.
  • §17(1) makes it obligatory on the Government to publish the award; the limit of time “has been fixed as showing that the publication of the award ought not to be held up”; but fixation of the period of thirty days does not mean that publication beyond that time renders the award invalid.
  • Reasons given: circumstances may make publication within thirty days impossible (a strike in the press or other good and sufficient cause); holding the award invalid would attach undue importance to a provision not in the legislature’s mind; it would entail a fresh reference with needless harassment to the parties; and non-publication within the thirty days entails no penalty — another consideration to be kept in mind.
  • The award was upheld (other issues on wages, dearness allowance, gratuity and hours were dealt with on the merits and, where necessary, remitted).
Ratio and the mandatory/directory test
  1. The test: the Court must ascertain the real intention of the legislature, looking at the statute as a whole. Where non-compliance is visited with a consequence (penalty, invalidity), the provision is generally mandatory; where no consequence is provided, it is likely to be directory.
  2. Directory: the 30-day period in §17(1). Mandatory: the obligation to publish (§17(1), per Sirsilk), and the binding effect of the settlement/award under §18.
  3. Effect of late publication: the award survives and becomes enforceable thirty days after publication (§17-A) — i.e., the delay postpones enforceability, it does not destroy the award.
Exam use

Any problem that says “the award was received on X, published on X + 31 days” is testing Remington Rand. Answer: §17(1) has two limbs — obligation (mandatory: Sirsilk) and time limit (directory: Remington Rand); late publication neither invalidates the award nor attracts any penalty; enforceability under §17-A/§55(3) simply runs from the actual date of publication/communication.

E.2 The Three Real Changes — explain these for extra marks

  1. Publication → Communication. Under §17(1) the award was a public act — gazetted, with a 30-day statutory duty. Under §55(2) it is an act of transmission to the parties and the Government. Consequences: the enforceability clock is party-specific (from communication), the fiction of universal notice disappears, and the Sirsilk problem changes shape — an award that has not been communicated is not enforceable, so a settlement reached before communication stands on even firmer ground. But the Code does not codify the Sirsilk exception: it remains a judicial rule, to be read into §55(2) by analogy.
  2. One adjudicatory forum instead of three. Abolition of the Labour Court and the merger of the Industrial Tribunal with an administrative wing changes the character of adjudication: the administrative member brings industry experience (the old §7-B “one person with industrial experience” idea), but the Judicial Member alone may deliver the award. The significance for Unit 3 is that the machinery that produces an award now has a statutory two-member design — and §55(1) deals expressly with who may sign.
  3. Interim relief becomes statutory in dismissal disputes. §50(2) gives the Tribunal/NIT power to grant interim relief in a matter of discharge or dismissal — on the material on record, no fresh evidence. This partly codifies what Tribunals had done under §11-A read with their incidental powers, and it strengthens the argument that an “award” includes an interim determination (§2(e)).

E.3 What the Code did not fix — the criticisms to reproduce

  • §55(3)–(5) reproduces the struck-down §17-A. The Andhra Pradesh and Madras High Courts held the executive’s power to reject or modify an award to be an intrusion on judicial power. The Code re-enacts the same power. No Supreme Court decision exists; a challenge to §55(3)–(5) would present the question again. (This is the single most “current-affairs-savvy” point you can make in a Unit 3 answer.)
  • No codification of the Sirsilk exception — the peace-preserving rule is left to be inferred from §57(1), a fragile basis when the Government is a party with an interest in enforcement.
  • No express finality clause — arguably a drafting change only (as Article 136/226 always remained available), but it deprives the courts of the statutory language they habitually started from.
  • The §57(3) extension to “future workers” continues to make settlements and awards binding on workers who had no voice in them; the protection is only that termination of the settlement/award requires a majority party’s notice (§58(7)) and that the terms must be just and fair (Herbertsons).

E.4 IR (Central) Rules, 2026 — the procedural layer (notified 8 May 2026 under §99 IRC)

Settlements

  • Form I — memorandum of settlement, whether arrived at in conciliation or otherwise than in conciliation.
  • Signed by the employer and the authorised office-bearers of the trade union(s), or by five duly authorised worker representatives where there is no union.
  • Form I is submitted to the Deputy Chief Labour Commissioner (Central) and the Conciliation Officer.
  • The Conciliation Officer maintains a register of settlements: serial number, industry, parties, date of settlement, and whether the settlement was reached in conciliation or by mutual negotiation.

Grievance Redressal Committee & other machinery

  • GRC — mandatory for establishments with 20 or more workers; three-year tenure; an aggrieved worker must apply within one year; if unresolved in 30 days, escalated to the Conciliation Officer within 60 days (Chapter II of the Code read with the Rules).
  • Works Committee — maximum 20 members (Rule 5).
  • Negotiating union / negotiating council — Rule 9: recognition where a union has the support of ≥51% of workers, failing which a council of unions (with representation for registered unions having at least 10% support).
  • Form V — memorandum of agreement for arbitration under §42.
  • Electronic filings, registers and returns are contemplated.
  • The Rules supersede Parts II and V–VIII of the Industrial Disputes (Central) Rules, 1957.
The status question — always answer it accurately

The four Labour Codes came into force on 21 November 2025, and the Industrial Relations (Central) Rules, 2026 alongside the Social Security (Central) Rules, 2026 were notified in May 2026. The course materials (LB-503, 2026 edition) treat Unit 1 as recast under the IR Code. Technically, however, transitional provisions (§§92–94 IRC) save proceedings pending under the IDA and settlements and awards already in force, and a large volume of decided law is IDA law. So: state the IDA position first (it is what carries the marks and the case law), then add the Code as the current statutory frame, and flag where the two differ. That single paragraph signals complete preparation.

G.2 Model Answer — 15 Marks

Q. “The award of an Industrial Tribunal was sent to the appropriate Government on 1 January. Before it could be published, the employer and the workmen’s union arrived at a settlement on the same matters, on terms more favourable to the workmen than the award, and both parties jointly requested the Government not to publish. The Government proposes to publish the award because §17(1) uses the word ‘shall’. Advise the parties.” (Dec 2024 Q.2; cf. Nov–Dec 2025 Q.8(c), May–June 2025 Q.2(b))

1. The issue. The question is whether the Government’s duty to publish an award under §17(1) of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 is absolute, or whether it must yield, in an exceptional case, to a settlement that has already become binding under §18(1). The answer requires (i) the construction of §17(1), (ii) the reconciliation of §17(1) with §18(1) and §18(3), and (iii) the procedural conditions of the exception.

2. The statutory framework. §17(1): every award “shall, within a period of thirty days from the date of its receipt by the appropriate Government, be published”. §17(2): the published award is “final and shall not be called in question by any court in any manner whatsoever”. §17-A(1): the award becomes enforceable on the expiry of thirty days from publication, subject to the Government’s power (in the confined cases) to declare it not enforceable. §18(1): a settlement arrived at otherwise than in conciliation binds the parties to the agreement. §18(3): a settlement arrived at in conciliation, an arbitration award, and the award of a Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal which has become enforceable, bind all the persons enumerated in clauses (a) to (d) — including persons employed on the date of the dispute and all who join later. §19(1): a settlement comes into operation on the agreed date or, where none is agreed, on the date of signing.

3. §17(1): the obligation is mandatory; the time limit is directory. In Sirsilk Ltd. v. Government of Andhra Pradesh, AIR 1964 SC 160, the Supreme Court held, in the first place, that §17(1) imposes a mandatory obligation to publish. Three textual reasons: the word “shall”; §17(2), which makes only the published award final, and therefore presupposes that publication is compulsory; and §17-A and §19, whose entire machinery of enforceability and duration operates from the date of publication. The Government, the Court said, has no general discretion to sit over awards.

But the period of thirty days is a different matter. In The Remington Rand of India Ltd. v. The Workmen, AIR 1968 SC 224, the Court held that the fixation of thirty days is directory: no penalty is provided for late publication, genuine causes of delay are conceivable (including a strike in a press), and holding an award void for a day’s delay would require a fresh reference and cause needless harassment. Late publication therefore neither invalidates the award nor attracts any sanction; it merely postpones the date from which the thirty-day enforceability period under §17-A runs.

4. The one recognised exception — the Sirsilk principle. The Sirsilk case was itself precisely the problem posed here. The Tribunal’s award had reached the Government in September 1957; on 1 October 1957 the parties arrived at a settlement and jointly informed the Government that the dispute had been settled, requesting that the award not be published. The Government declined, relying on the mandatory character of §17(1). The Supreme Court allowed the demurrer: although §17(1) is mandatory, a settlement which has already come into force under §18(1) is equally binding, and the two provisions cannot both be given effect when the settlement and the award cover the same subject-matter. If the award were published, the parties would be subject to two legally binding instruments with conflicting terms — “a state of affairs which it is impossible to contemplate”. The only way to reconcile §17(1) and §18(1) is to withhold publication of the award. The Court emphasised that the object of the Act is to preserve industrial peace; that a settlement arrived at by the free will of the parties “is likely to lead to more lasting peace than an award”; and that once the parties had settled, the dispute “could be considered to have become infructuous” so far as the pending adjudication was concerned.

5. Conditions for the exception. The exception is narrow. On the authorities and the scheme of §2(p) and §19(1), it applies only where: (a) there exists a valid statutory settlement — written, signed in the prescribed manner, with a copy sent to the authorised officer and the Conciliation Officer (under the IR Rules, 2026, recorded in Form I); (b) the settlement has come into operation — from the agreed date or the date of signing; (c) both parties jointly inform the Government; and (d) the settlement covers the same subject-matter as the award, so that publication would create an actual conflict. If only one party requests withholding, or if the settlement is of doubtful validity, the Government should publish — its obligation then revives in full.

6. If the settlement’s validity is later challenged. A dispute about the validity of the settlement (fraud, misrepresentation, undue influence, coercion, or the settlement not being “just and fair”) is itself a fresh industrial dispute which the Government may refer. If it is adjudicated that the settlement is not binding under §18(1), the situation reverts and the Government may then publish the withheld award. This is the safety valve that makes the exception tolerable.

7. The better practice. The Court indicated the course which should ordinarily be followed: where parties settle while a reference is pending, they should place the settlement before the Tribunal and ask it to make an award in terms of the settlement. That course avoids the whole conflict, gives the settlement the additional binding force of an award under §18(3), and keeps the Government out of the dilemma. Withholding is a fallback for the case where the award has already left the Tribunal.

8. Position under the IR Code, 2020. §55(2) now requires the award to be “communicated to the parties concerned and the appropriate Government”, and §55(3) makes it enforceable thirty days from communication. The Government no longer “publishes”; it communicates and (in the specified cases) may declare an award non-enforceable. The Sirsilk exception has not been codified, but it applies with at least equal force: since enforceability now runs from communication, a settlement reached and intimated before communication has an even stronger claim to priority, and the reasoning of §57(1) and §57(3) is identical to that of §18(1) and §18(3). Note also the constitutional cloud over §55(3)–(5), which re-enacts the power struck down by the Andhra Pradesh and Madras High Courts — so a Government that refuses to respect a binding settlement may find its own power under challenge.

9. Advice. The parties should (i) place on record the fact of a valid, operative §2(p)/§18(1) settlement covering the same subject-matter; (ii) make a joint written request to the Government, with a certified copy of the memorandum of settlement, asking that the award not be published/communicated; (iii) require the Government not to act on the award until the request is decided, since publication with conflicting terms would prejudice them irreversibly; and (iv) if the Government proceeds to publish notwithstanding the request, move the High Court under Article 226, where a mandamus/prohibition would lie, Sirsilk being a direct authority on identical facts. The Government’s plea based on the word “shall” in §17(1) is not an answer: Sirsilk is precisely a decision that “shall” does not require publication where a binding settlement has intervened.

G.3 Model Answer — 10 Marks

Q. The award was received by the State Government on 14 October and was published in the Gazette on 15 November — the 31st day. The employer contends that the award is a nullity. Advise. (May–June 2025 Q.2(a))

1. Issue. Whether the thirty-day period in §17(1) is mandatory, so that publication on the thirty-first day renders the award void and unenforceable.

2. The provision. §17(1) contains two distinct requirements: the obligation to publish, and the period within which publication must occur. §17(2) attaches finality to the award “published under sub-section (1)”; §17-A(1) makes the award enforceable thirty days after publication; §19 fixes the period of operation with reference to enforceability. Non-compliance with §17 entails no penalty anywhere in the Act.

3. The obligation is mandatory. In Sirsilk Ltd. v. Government of Andhra Pradesh, AIR 1964 SC 160, the Supreme Court held that §17(1) casts a mandatory duty on the Government to publish: the word “shall”, the finality conferred by §17(2) on the published award, and the dependence of §§17-A and 19 upon publication all point that way. The Government cannot pick and choose among awards. This, however, decides only that it must publish; it does not decide when.

4. The time limit is directory. On the very next occasion, The Remington Rand of India Ltd. v. The Workmen, AIR 1968 SC 224, the Supreme Court drew the distinction. On facts materially identical to these (the award was published on the 31st day), the Court held the time provision directory and not mandatory. Four reasons were given: (i) no penalty is attached to late publication; (ii) circumstances may make publication within thirty days impossible — the Court instanced a strike in the press — and the legislature could not have intended that such an event should nullify an award; (iii) attaching such a drastic consequence to a procedural delay would place undue importance on a provision which the legislature did not intend to be a condition of validity; and (iv) invalidating the award would require a fresh reference and cause needless harassment to the parties. The fixation of the period was meant to ensure that publication is not “held up”; it was not meant to be a trap for the Government.

5. Consequences of late publication. The award remains valid and binding once published; the only effect is temporal — under §17-A(1) it becomes enforceable thirty days from the actual date of publication, and the period of operation under §19(3) runs from that enforceability. Under §55(3) of the IR Code, the position is the same in substance: enforceability runs from the date of communication, whenever that occurs.

6. Test for mandatory/directory (worth stating as a proposition): the court looks to the real intention of the legislature and to the consequence annexed to non-compliance. Where invalidity or a penalty is prescribed, the provision is ordinarily mandatory; where none is prescribed and a strict construction would cause injustice or absurdity, it is directory. Applying the test, the obligation is mandatory, the period directory — a classic illustration of one provision containing both.

7. Advice. The employer’s contention must fail: a one-day delay in publication does not vitiate the award, and no writ would lie merely on that ground (§17(2) and the general principle of Remington Rand). The employer may, however, still challenge the award on the substantive grounds available in law — want of jurisdiction, error of law on the face of the award, breach of natural justice, or perversity — under Article 226 or, with special leave, Article 136, the delay in publication being of no assistance to it on those questions.

Q. An arbitrator’s award under §10-A directed the striking workmen to pay the employer Rs 5 lakh as compensation for loss of profits caused by the strike, which had been held illegal. The workmen move the High Court under Article 226. Advise them, and state the grounds on which such an award may be reviewed. (Dec 2024 Q.4(b) — the Rohtas problem)

1. Availability of review. An award under §10-A is an “award” within §2(b) and is published and enforced like a Tribunal award. In Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. Rohtas Industries Staff Union, AIR 1976 SC 425, the Supreme Court, following Engineering Mazdoor Sabha v. Hind Cycles Ltd., (1963) Supp. 1 SCR 625, held that Article 226 is “in a sense wider than Article 136” because it is not confined to courts and tribunals; hence a writ lies even against the award of a §10-A arbitrator. Since the 1964 amendment, such an arbitrator can bind even non-parties, and the force of his award derives from the statute; he is therefore “part of the methodology of the sovereign’s dispensation of justice, falling within the rainbow of statutory tribunals amenable to judicial review”. But the writ power must be exercised with restraint — not “like a bull in a China shop” — and kept on a “severely judicious leash”.

2. The ground: error of law on the face of the award. An award under §10-A may be set aside where an apparent error of law stains its face. The jurisdiction, as stated in Halsbury’s Laws of England (4th edn., Vol. 2, para 623) and adopted by the Supreme Court, requires that there be found in the award, or in a document incorporated with it, “some legal proposition which is the basis of the award and which is erroneous” (Champsey Bhara & Co. v. Jivraj Balloo). Where the arbitrator gives no reasons and does not indicate the principles of law on which he proceeded, the award is not for that reason bad (Bungo Steel Furniture v. Union of India); but where he does state his reasons, they are examinable. Rohtas added the decisive refinement: the court may find the error sub silentio — “the inscrutable face of the sphinx has no better title to invulnerability than a speaking face which is a candid index of the mind” — provided the error is transparent and not ferreted out by conjecture.

3. Application. The only possible legal basis for a claim of damages against strikers is the tort of conspiracy — and even in English law, a combination is actionable only where its predominant purpose is to injure the plaintiff; where the object is to advance the defendants’ own lawful interests, there is no actionable wrong (Sorrel v. Smith; Allen v. Flood; Quinn v. Leathem; Crofter; Rookes v. Barnard). The illegality of the strike is a statutory concept and is not, by itself, an actionable tort. In Rohtas the arbitrators had assumed that “if the strike is illegal, the tort of conspiracy is made out”, without investigating the object of the strike at all — the object being inter-union rivalry and the pursuit of the strikers’ own interests. That assumption was an erroneous legal proposition on the face of the award, and the High Court was right to excise the compensation direction. (By contrast, sabotage — the wilful destruction of plant or property — remains actionable.)

4. The self-contained-code argument. The Industrial Disputes Act is a comprehensive code so far as it speaks; rights created by it must be enforced through its own machinery, for “the right and the remedy are given uno flatu” (Barraclough v. Brown; Premier Automobiles Ltd. v. Kamlekar Shantaram Wadke). Since it is the Act which makes a strike “illegal”, the remedy for an illegal strike is the one the Act gives — prosecution under §26 — and not a civil claim for damages. This reinforces the conclusion that the compensation award was outside the scope of what the statute was designed to achieve.

5. A further, jurisdictional objection (obiter). The Supreme Court also doubted whether a claim by an employer for compensation for loss of business is an “industrial dispute” under §2(k) at all. A §10-A reference is confined to existing or apprehended industrial disputes; “no industrial dispute, no valid arbitral reference”; and the consent of the parties cannot create arbitral jurisdiction. The Court noted the asymmetry that §33-C enables a workman to recover money due from the employer, but provides no converse remedy — a provision explicable by the workman being the weaker party and by Part IV of the Constitution. It did not pronounce finally on the point, and it is therefore obiter, but it is the most powerful argument available to the workmen.

6. Other grounds on which awards are reviewed. (i) want or excess of jurisdiction — deciding matters outside the reference, or where no industrial dispute exists; (ii) error of law on the face of the award (above); (iii) breach of natural justice — denial of hearing, of the right to lead or cross-examine on evidence, or a procedure that is arbitrary or “despotic” (Bharat Bank); and (iv) perversity or findings unsupported by any evidence. The remedy is ordinarily to quash, with a remand for fresh adjudication, not for the writ court to substitute its own decision (Cochin Port Trust; Cochin Shipyard).

7. Advice. The workmen should succeed. The compensation direction rests on an error of law on the face of the award (illegality of the strike treated as per se creating liability in damages), and additionally lacks arbitral jurisdiction under §10-A because the employer’s damages claim is not an industrial dispute. They may seek certiorari under Article 226 to quash that part of the award, and in an appropriate case Article 136. They should not, however, expect the court to reopen the finding that the strike was illegal or the consequential denial of strike wages — those parts were accepted, and a writ court does not sit as a court of error over concurrent findings of fact.

G.4 Model Answer — 15 Marks

Q. “The Supreme Court’s power under Article 136 extends to the awards of Industrial Tribunals; yet the Court is not a court of error.” Discuss with reference to Bharat Bank Ltd. v. Employees. Would your answer be the same if the challenge were made to the High Court under Article 226 against an arbitration award under §10-A? (May–June 2025 Q.1; Nov–Dec 2025 Q.1)

1. The constitutional text. Article 136(1) confers on the Supreme Court a discretionary power to grant special leave to appeal from any “judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India”. Two expressions do the work here: “tribunal” and “determination”. Article 226, by contrast, is wider in reach (any person or authority) but narrower in remedy (writs and directions), and is exercised by the High Courts.

2. Bharat Bank: the preliminary objection. In Bharat Bank Ltd. v. Employees, AIR 1950 SC 188, the Government and the respondents objected that an Industrial Tribunal’s award could not be appealed under Article 136 at all — because “tribunal” meant a body exercising the judicial functions of the State and giving a final judgment carrying legal sanction of its own force; because the award acquired binding force only from the Government’s declaration under the then §15(2); and because an award which can even be rejected (in Government-employee disputes) cannot be a “determination”.

3. The holding. The objection was overruled. The Court held that (i) the word “tribunal” is wider than “court” and includes bodies “adorned with similar trappings as a court but not strictly coming within that definition”; (ii) the condition is that the body be constituted by the State and invested with part of the judicial functions of the State — not the whole; and (iii) the word “determination” was deliberately chosen to widen the reach of the Article. An Industrial Tribunal satisfies these tests: it decides litigated questions according to law, it is presided over by persons qualified to be Judges, it is deemed a civil court for certain purposes, and disobedience of its award is punishable under §29.

4. Why a confirmatory declaration does not matter. The respondent’s strongest point was that the Government’s declaration “gave the spark of life” to the award. The Court answered from principle and authority: where the Government has no discretion to affirm, modify or reject — and under §15(2) it had none except in the Government-as-party case — the rights of the parties are affected by the adjudication, not by the ministerial act of announcement. The Court cited Rex v. Electricity Commissioners (“I know of no authority which compels me to hold that a proceeding cannot be a judicial proceeding subject to confirmation or approval”) and Rex v. Minister of Health. Article 136 was therefore attracted.

5. “Not a court of error.” This is the second half of the proposition and is what the Court actually did. Although §16 of the Act was then mandatory (all members had to sign) and the Tribunal had been constituted for three members but signed by two without any reconstitution under §8, and although the Tribunal’s award rested on no evidence at all — the Court granted relief; but it expressly refused to interfere with two of the employer’s four grounds (the meaning of “victimization” and the reinstatement of persons said to be guilty of an illegal strike), observing that “this Court is not a mere court of error” and that special leave is to be granted only where the Tribunal has exceeded jurisdiction, approached the questions in a manner likely to result in injustice, or acted contrary to natural justice or “in an arbitrary or despotic fashion”. The remedy, moreover, was to quash and remand for fresh adjudication, not to substitute the Court’s own award.

6. The two grounds on which the Court actually interfered. (i) Natural justice: the Tribunal decided the victimisation issue on the parties’ “statements of case” — not on oath, without cross-examination, without any evidence, ignoring the Bank’s affidavit — a procedure “against all principles of natural justice”. The Court’s dictum is often quoted: where no appeal is provided, there is a tendency to act “like a benevolent despot”; and “benevolent despotism … is foreign to a democratic Constitution”. (ii) Mandatory procedure: an award signed by two of three members, without reconstitution of the Tribunal under §8, “is not the award of the Tribunal constituted by the Government”; “the idea of three persons hearing a case and two of them deciding it is repugnant to all notions of fairness”.

7. The nature of the jurisdiction — the limits. (a) Article 136 is discretionary and extraordinary, to be exercised “in rare and exceptional cases and on well-known principles”; (b) the Court is not a court of error and will not re-appreciate evidence, decide questions of construction de novo, or interfere with findings of fact unless perverse; (c) it will not act where the statutory machinery is self-contained and provides its own remedy; (d) it will not permit the discretionary remedy to be defeated by delay, laches, suppression of facts or the petitioner’s own conduct. What remains is the four-fold ground of review: jurisdictional error, error of law on the face of the award, breach of natural justice, and perversity/non-application of mind — the same grounds on which certiorari lies under Article 226.

8. Would the answer differ under Article 226 for a §10-A award? No — but the route differs, and, if anything, the review is more readily available. In Rohtas Industries (1976) the Supreme Court held that an award under §10-A is within the reach of Article 226: Article 226 is wider than Article 136 in that it is available against any person or authority and even where another remedy exists, and, as Hind Cycles explained, a writ may issue against a §10-A arbitrator even if he is not a “tribunal” under Article 136. After the 1964 amendment, a statutory arbitrator can bind even non-parties, and thus falls “within the rainbow of statutory tribunals amenable to judicial review”. Such an award is “not only not invulnerable but more sensitively susceptible to the writ lancet”; it may be set aside for an apparent error of law on its face — including an error sub silentio. The difference between a private arbitrator’s award and a §10-A award is “fine, but real”. The discipline is the same: the writ is extraordinary and must not be exercised “like a bull in a China shop”.

9. The statutory overlay. §17(2) of the IDA declares the published award “final and shall not be called in question by any Court in any manner whatsoever”; §97 of the IR Code now bars the jurisdiction of civil courts. Neither provision — nor the omitted finality clause in §55 of the Code — can or does touch Articles 226/227 and 136, because the source of those powers is the Constitution and not the statute. The correct formulation is therefore: statutory finality is finality before civil courts and appellate authorities, not before the constitutional courts; and the constitutional courts, while ready to correct jurisdictional excess, patent illegality and breaches of natural justice, will not convert themselves into appellate forums over industrial awards.

10. Conclusion. Bharat Bank answered the question of competence in the affirmative and the question of inclination in the negative: the Supreme Court can review an industrial award under Article 136, but will do so sparingly and only to correct fundamental defects; the same principles, differently packaged, govern Article 226. The twin legacy of the case — the tribunal-status of industrial adjudication and the “not a court of error” restraint — remains the constitutional foundation of Sub-unit (c) of Unit 3.

G.5 Short-note bank (5-markers that can be asked cold)

(a) Duration and termination of a settlement

§2(p) defines a settlement; §19(1)–(2) and (7) fix its life. It operates from the agreed date, else from the date of signing. It binds for the agreed period, or, if none is agreed, for six months, and continues thereafter until two months from the date on which a written notice of termination is given (sixty days under §58(2) IRC). The notice is ineffective unless given by a party representing the majority of persons bound. There is no statutory ceiling on the period of a settlement (unlike the award’s one-year/three-year rule) and no Government power to extend it. A settlement may also be displaced by a valid fresh settlement or award; and it may be challenged on grounds of fraud, misrepresentation, coercion or unfairness, which will be adjudicated as a fresh industrial dispute. Breach is punishable under §29/§86(17)–(18) and money due is recoverable under §33-C/§59.

(b) §17-A / §55(3) — commencement of the award

An award becomes enforceable thirty days after publication (§17-A(1) IDA) or communication (§55(3) IRC). The proviso allows the appropriate Government to declare that the award shall not become enforceable where the award is in a dispute to which the Government is a party, or is an award of the National Industrial Tribunal, and it is inexpedient on public grounds affecting national economy or social justice to give effect to it. Within 90 days the Government may reject or modify the award and must lay it before the legislature; the award then becomes enforceable 15 days after laying, or on the expiry of the 90 days if no order is made. Subject to this, the award operates from the date specified in it or from the date it becomes enforceable. The provision has been held unconstitutional by the AP High Court (1997) and the Madras High Court (2011, 2014) as an exercise of judicial power by the executive; IRC §55(3)–(5) reproduces it, and no Supreme Court ruling settles the question.

(c) §36-A IDA / §95 IRC — interpretation of award or settlement

Where a difficulty or doubt arises as to the interpretation of any provision of an award or settlement, the appropriate Government may refer the question to a Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal (under the Code, to the Industrial Tribunal or the National Industrial Tribunal). The authority decides the question after hearing the parties, and the decision is final and binding on all the parties. The provision is a machinery provision, not a power of review: it makes an award workable where its meaning is ambiguous, but it cannot be used to rewrite, expand or correct an award, nor to reopen the merits. It shows that even the “final” award is subject to a limited, statutory interpretive jurisdiction. Its existence is also a reason why courts insist that an award be capable of being given effect to as it stands — clarity and precision in drafting awards is itself a legal obligation.

(d) Can a minority union terminate a settlement?

No. A settlement under §18(1)/§18(3) binds, and §19(7)/§58(7) provides that a notice of termination has no effect unless given by a party representing the majority of the persons bound by the settlement or award. The purpose is to prevent a splinter group from unravelling a collective bargain on which the workforce has relied. Consistent with this, Herbertsons Ltd. v. Workmen, (1977) 2 SCC 515, held that a settlement signed with the recognised union, if just and fair, binds all workmen, even a small minority who are not its members, and the Tribunal cannot sit in appeal over its terms; P. Virudhachalam v. Lotus Mills, (1998) 2 SCC 625, applied the same principle to §12(3)/§18(3) settlements, holding them binding even on objecting minority unions. The minority’s remedy is to raise a fresh dispute on a matter not covered by the settlement, or after its duration ends, or to challenge its validity for fraud/unfairness — not to terminate it.

(e) Is §17(1) mandatory or directory?

Both, in different limbs. The obligation to publish is mandatory (Sirsilk): the word “shall”, the finality attaching to the published award under §17(2), and the dependence of §17-A and §19 on publication all compel that construction. The thirty-day period is directory (Remington Rand): no penalty for delay, no invalidation, genuine causes of delay may exist, and a contrary view would cause needless harassment and fresh references. The single exception to the obligation is the Sirsilk situation — a binding §18(1) settlement on the same matters jointly intimated to the Government before publication, where publication must be withheld to reconcile §18(1) with §18(3).

(f) Nature of an award — in one paragraph

An award under §2(b)/§2(e) is the interim or final determination of an industrial dispute or of any question relating thereto by a Labour Court, Industrial Tribunal, NIT or a §10-A/§42 arbitrator. It is a quasi-judicial act of a statutory tribunal which enjoys the “trappings of a court” (Bharat Bank); it is constitutive — it may create new obligations or modify contracts, which no civil court could do (Western India Automobile Association; Rohtas v. Brijnandan Pandey); it must be in writing and signed (§16/§55(1)); it becomes binding on all the persons in §18(3)/§57(3) once enforceable (§17-A/§55(3)); it operates for one year, extendable to a maximum of three (§19(3)–(6)/§58(3)–(6)); and it is subject to judicial review on four limited grounds but is not appealable as of right.

H.2 Ten takeaways to recite

  1. Two settlements, two binding effects. §18(1) = parties; §18(3) = everyone, present and future. Settlement in conciliation is always §18(3).
  2. One sentence for §17(1): the obligation to publish is mandatory; the 30-day time limit is directory.
  3. Sirsilk: if a binding §18(1) settlement on the same matter is jointly intimated before publication, the Government must withhold the award — the only exception to the mandatory duty.
  4. Remington Rand: publication on the 31st day does not invalidate the award; the only consequence is that enforceability runs from the actual date.
  5. Award duration: 1 year → extendable to a maximum of 3 years; a settlement has no ceiling but the Government cannot extend it.
  6. Termination of either instrument needs a MAJORITY party’s notice (§19(7)/§58(7)) — a minority union cannot unravel it.
  7. An award is constitutive: it can “add to or alter the terms or conditions of the contract of service” (Western India Automobile Assn; Bharat Bank) and “create new obligations or modify contracts” (Rohtas v. Brijnandan Pandey).
  8. Bharat Bank: an Industrial Tribunal is a tribunal under Art. 136; the Court is not a court of error; natural justice and mandatory procedure are the grounds actually used.
  9. Rohtas: Art. 226 reaches a §10-A award; the ground is error of law on the face (including sub silentio); an illegal strike is remediable only under §26; a damages claim by the employer may not be an industrial dispute at all (obiter).
  10. IRC overlay: publication → communication (§55(2)); enforceability from communication +30 days (§55(3)); the same executive rejection/modification power (§55(4)–(5)); §95 interpretation; §97 civil-court bar; §57/§58 mirror §18/§19.

H.4 The eight errors that cost the most marks

  • Writing “§17(1) is directory” without the two-limb qualification.
  • Saying an award becomes enforceable “when it is passed” — it is 30 days after publication (§17-A) / communication (§55(3)).
  • Confusing §15 (submission), §16 (form), §17 (publication), §17-A (enforceability) — four distinct stages.
  • Saying a settlement also needs “publication” — it needs the §2(p) formalities and filing; only the award had a publication duty.
  • Treating the §17-A proviso as a general Government power — it applies only where the Government is a party or the award is of an NIT, and only on the national-economy/social-justice grounds.
  • Stating that the Government can extend an award indefinitely — the ceiling is three years; and it cannot extend a settlement at all.
  • Describing Rohtas as a case about the tort of conspiracy in Indian law as settled law — the Court refused to transplant the English tort; and the §2(k) point is obiter.
  • Ignoring the IR Code, or, conversely, arguing Unit 3 solely under the Code and forgetting that the case law and the marked ruling provisions are IDA-based.

H.6 Glossary — the nine terms that must be used correctly

Settlement

A written agreement between employer and workers relating to an industrial dispute, given binding force by statute, whether reached in conciliation (§18(3)) or otherwise (§18(1)).

Award

The interim or final determination of an industrial dispute (or a question relating thereto) by a Labour Court/Industrial Tribunal/NIT, or made in arbitration under §10-A/§42.

Publication vs Communication

Publication (IDA §17(1)) = making the award public through the Official Gazette within 30 days, at the Government’s discretion as to manner. Communication (IRC §55(2)) = sending the award to the parties and the Government; the trigger for enforceability.

Enforceable

The status the award acquires 30 days after publication/communication, from which the period of operation (§19(3)/§58(3)) runs. Only an enforceable award binds under §18(3)/§57(3).

Period of operation

The duration for which the settlement or award remains binding — agreed or six months for a settlement; one year (max three) for an award.

Majority party

The party representing the majority of the persons bound by the settlement/award; only such a party may give a valid notice of termination (§19(7)/§58(7)).

Error of law on the face

An erroneous legal proposition which forms the basis of the award and is apparent from the award itself (expressly or sub silentio) — a ground for judicial review.

Self-contained code

The doctrine (Premier Automobiles; adopted in Rohtas) that the IDA supplies both the right and the remedy “uno flatu”, so that a right created by the Act is enforced through the Act — the foundation of §97 IRC.

Final word — the two-sentence version of Unit 3

A settlement is a binding collective agreement whose force comes from the parties and whose reach comes from the statute; an award is a binding quasi-judicial determination whose force comes from the statute and whose reach also comes from the statute — the first is preferred by the law of industrial peace, the second is controlled by a narrow but real power of judicial review under Articles 226 and 136. The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 moves the same scheme into a new house — communication instead of publication, a merged tribunal, statutory interim relief — while leaving the old constitutional questions, including the validity of the executive’s power to reject or modify an award, exactly where Bharat Bank and Rohtas left them: open, and examinable.
