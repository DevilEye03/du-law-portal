0Orientation: What Unit 4 Asks — and How It Fits the Course

Units 1 to 3 of this course build the dispute-resolution machinery (reference, adjudication, settlement, award). Unit 4 asks a different and more immediate question: what may an employer do to his own workman — and by what procedure — when the workman is accused of misconduct? The answer is that the employer’s historic “master-and-servant” freedom survives as a prerogative, but it is hedged in on four sides:

① The shadow of an industrial dispute (§33 / IRC §90)

While a dispute is pending before a conciliation officer, arbitrator, Tribunal or NIT, the employer’s power to punish is either frozen (dismissals connected with the dispute need prior permission) or conditionally released (dismissals unconnected with the dispute need wages for one month plus subsequent approval). Most of the prescribed cases — Kushal Bhan, Tata Oil Mills — are precisely §33(2)(b) approval cases.

② The certified Standing Orders

The employer may punish only for the “misconduct” and only in the manner defined in the standing orders certified under the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 (now Chapter IV, §§28–39 of the IR Code). The standing orders are statutorily binding, override the contract of employment, and supply the very definition of misconduct — which is why Tata Oil Mills turns on “Standing Order 22(viii)”, and Associated Cement on “Standing Order 16”.

③ Natural justice, made operational

There is no statutory code of domestic enquiry procedure in the IDA. Its content is judge-made — charge-sheet, evidence first, cross-examination, defence evidence, an impartial enquiry officer, no reliance on undisclosed material. Associated Cement converts this into a checkable checklist; Prem Nath Bali adds a time limit (six months, ordinarily; one year, outside).

④ Judicial review of the punishment and the finding

Once the dispute comes before a Tribunal, the employer’s discretion is tested against the four classic grounds (Indian Iron & Steel) and, since §11-A (1971), against the Tribunal’s statutory power to reappraise evidence and substitute a lesser punishment — the bridge to Unit 5. The “shocks the conscience” standard (Prem Nath Bali) is the outer check on quantum.

The organising idea of Unit 4

Disciplinary action is a confluence of three sources of law: (i) the contract of employment (master and servant); (ii) the statute (Standing Orders Act, IDA §§33, 33-A, 11-A, IR Code); and (iii) the Constitution and natural justice (Arts. 14, 21; audi alteram partem; nemo judex in causa sua). The unit’s six cases map the movement of Indian law across these sources: from autonomy (Kushal Bhan, 1960) → to procedural discipline (Associated Cement, 1964) → to balance and restraint (Tata Oil Mills, 1965) → to procedural clarity on remedies (Kusheshwar Dubey, 1988) → to appellate correction of discretionary relief (IOB v. Ganesan, 2007) → to time-based accountability of the employer (Prem Nath Bali, 2015).

AManagerial Prerogative — Doctrine, Sources and Limits

A.1 What “managerial prerogative” means

The doctrine in the course’s own words

“Managerial prerogative refers to the rights and powers of the employer to manage and control the affairs of the industrial establishment. This includes hiring, disciplinary control, transfer, promotion, and termination of employees.” — Unit 4 notes in the course materials (Mendiratta), § 1.

The power is inherent in the contract of employment and in the employer’s ownership of the undertaking. At common law it was described as the right to “hire and fire”. In the older industrial-law vocabulary it is the management’s power to “direct its own internal administration and discipline” — Indian Iron & Steel Co. Ltd. v. Their Workmen, AIR 1958 SC 130; and the Supreme Court has repeatedly said that “the right to take disciplinary action and to decide upon the quantum of punishment are mainly managerial functions” — Workmen of Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Management, AIR 1973 SC 1227, principle (1).

Source ① — Contract

The contract of service confers the master’s power to command and the servant’s duty to obey; breach of that duty is “misconduct”, which the employer may punish. The relationship is one of status governed by the contract, but the law of “master and servant” has been heavily overlaid by statute in industrial employment.

Source ② — Property & business freedom

The undertaking is the employer’s property and management is his business (Art. 19(1)(g) Constitution). Discipline is treated as a requirement of “efficient industrial management” and of production; hence courts speak of the employer’s “power to direct its own internal administration and discipline”.

Source ③ — Certified Standing Orders

In industrial establishments, the employer’s punitive power is exercised through the standing orders certified under the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 (IRC, Chapter IV). These are “statutory terms of service” — binding on employer and workman alike — and they define both the misconduct and the procedure.

Legal regulation — why the prerogative is not absolute

The course notes put the “need for legal regulation” squarely: though inherent, the power “is not absolute”; it is regulated through (i) the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947; (ii) the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946; and (iii) the constitutional provisions, in particular Articles 14 and 21. The policy tension — “industrial peace vs. employer autonomy” — is the theme of the unit: the law “strikes a balance between employer’s managerial freedom and the protection of workers’ rights by making disciplinary action subject to procedural safeguards”.

The classic jurisprudential framing (adopted in the course material from Roscoe Pound) is social engineering: the task of industrial law is to balance the competing interests of management in efficient discipline and of labour in security of livelihood — because the worker’s job is not merely a contract but his livelihood, protected by Article 21 and by the Directive Principles (Arts. 39, 41, 43, 43-A).

BDisciplinary Action — Misconduct, the Enquiry, and its Consequences

B.1 “Misconduct” — the gateway concept

Working definition

Misconduct is a conduct — an act or omission — which is inconsistent with the faithful and diligent discharge of the duties of the employee, or which is subversive of discipline in the establishment. In industrial law it is not for the employer to invent the categories: misconduct must be one of the acts or omissions specified in the certified standing orders (or in the contract of service where no standing orders apply). This is the single most important structural point of Unit 4.

Typical misconduct clauses in standing orders / model standing orders

  • wilful insubordination or disobedience, whether alone or in combination with others, of any lawful and reasonable order of a superior;
  • theft, fraud or dishonesty in connection with the employer’s business or property;
  • wilful damage to or loss of the employer’s goods or property;
  • taking or giving bribes or any illegal gratification;
  • habitual absence without leave, or absence without leave for more than the specified days;
  • habitual late attendance; neglect of work or negligence causing damage;
  • drunkenness, fighting, riotous or disorderly or indecent behaviour within or without the factory (the very clause in Tata Oil Mills, S.O. 22(viii) — see also S.O. 16 in Associated Cement);
  • strikes or incitement to strike in contravention of the Act; “go-slow”;
  • disclosure of confidential information; engaging in trade or business without permission;
  • abuse of position, sexual harassment, violence — the modern additions (POSH Act, 2013 policy obligations).

The “rational connection” test for outside-the-gates conduct

Standing orders frequently cover conduct “within or without the factory”. The Supreme Court in Tata Oil Mills Co. Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1965 SC 155, held that such a clause is not to be read as embracing a “purely private and individual dispute” between two employees. To attract the clause, the employer must show that the disorderly conduct had “some rational connection with the employment of the assailant and the victim”.

Application: in Tata Oil Mills itself an assault outside the factory, at a distance, after shift hours, was held linked to employment because the motive was the victim’s support for an Incentive Bonus Scheme — a workplace issue over which two rival unions were arrayed. It was “not the result of a purely individual or private quarrel”.

Contrast: a fist-fight at a private wedding over a personal matter, or an assault arising out of an old family feud, would fall outside the clause — absent facts showing that the quarrel was born of a workplace grievance (production bonus, union rivalry, supervisory rivalry, refusal to do another’s work).

Real examples to give in an answer
  • Banking: a cashier misappropriating funds or a clerk assaulting a colleague inside the branch (the fact pattern of Indian Overseas Bank v. P. Ganesan, 2007 — assault on a rival union’s president within bank premises, plus damage to bank property). The bank’s charge-sheet used precisely the vocabulary of misconduct: “indiscipline, insubordination, unruly and disorderly behaviour, use of filthy language and most shameful abuses as well as murderous assault … causing damage to valuable properties and serious impairment to the bank’s prestige and reputation”.
  • Manufacturing: an assault in the residential colony or canteen by one workman on another over production bonus or a union election — Tata Oil Mills on facts; or shouting slogans and gheraoing officers at the factory gate (as in the incidents of 1952 in Associated Cement).
  • Service/PSU: a quarrel with a colleague over the timely delivery of certified copies and the use of “unwanted words in the office” — the actual charge in Prem Nath Bali; note how modest the incident was, and how severe the eventual penalty — the point of a proportionality argument.
  • Modern workplace: an employee posting defamatory content about a supervisor on social media, or a driver using a company vehicle for an unauthorised purpose — both are routinely tested against the standing-order clause on “subversive of discipline” and the “rational connection” test.

B.2 Penalties — what the employer may impose

Standing orders (and, for government servants, the CCS (CCA) Rules) divide punishments into minor and major penalties:

Minor penaltiesMajor penalties
Censure · warning · fine (subject to the Payment of Wages Act limits) · withholding of increment(s) · withholding of promotion · recovery from pay of the whole or part of any pecuniary loss caused by negligence · stoppage at an efficiency bar. Reduction to a lower stage in the time-scale; reduction to a lower service, grade or post; compulsory retirement (the penalty in Prem Nath Bali); removal from service (without disqualification for future employment); dismissal (with disqualification); discharge simpliciter.
Two technical distinctions that decide cases
  • Dismissal vs. discharge: dismissal is punitive — it rests on proved misconduct and carries the stigma; discharge simpliciter (termination by notice under the contract/standing orders) is not punitive, and its legality is tested by contract/procedure (retrenchment rules under §25-F may then apply if it is really a retrenchment). In Associated Cement, three of the five workmen ultimately left under a consent order treating the dismissal as “an order of discharge simpliciter”, with ₹3,500 each plus gratuity and provident fund.
  • Punishment must be one authorised by the standing orders: an employer may not impose, say, a fine where the standing orders permit only warning, or dismiss for a misconduct listed only as a minor lapse. A punishment outside the standing orders is illegal, not merely harsh.

Natural justice — the two maxims applied to the two stages
  1. Audi alteram partem (“hear the other side”): charge-sheet; notice; evidence in the workman’s presence; cross-examination; opportunity to explain and to lead defence evidence; a considered finding. It is violated as much by relying on undisclosed material as by refusing a hearing — the Associated Cement point about the attendance register and the co-worker’s enquiry.
  2. Nemo judex in causa sua (“no one a judge in his own cause”): the enquiry officer must not be the complainant, the prosecutor, or a witness. Associated Cement held it “desirable that the enquiry should be left to be held by some other person who does not claim to be an eyewitness of the impugned incident”; where officers relied on what “we ourselves saw”, the enquiry became an “empty formality”.
  3. Standard of fairness — not criminal procedure. “Domestic enquiries need not be conducted in accordance with the technical requirements of criminal trials, but they must be fairly conducted and … considerations of fair play and natural justice must govern the conduct of the enquiry officer.” The Court added the worker-protective reason: “in a large majority of cases, employees are likely to be ignorant, and so, it is necessary not to expose them to the risk of cross-examination” at the outset.

B.5 After the enquiry — report, hearing on penalty, and the “prejudice” rule

  • Enquiry report: a reasoned report is the foundation of the punishment order. The rule that the workman should receive the enquiry report before the penalty is imposed, and be heard on it, comes from the service-law line — Managing Director, ECIL v. B. Karunakar, (1993) 4 SCC 727 — and is applied to industrial enquiries as a facet of fair play.
  • The prejudice test: a procedural breach that causes no prejudice will not automatically nullify the action where the rules are “directory”; but where the breach touches the core of natural justice (denial of hearing, denial of evidence), it is fatal — the distinction drawn in State Bank of Patiala v. S.K. Sharma, (1996) 3 SCC 364, and reflected in the industrial cases’ approach of asking whether the enquiry was fair in substance (contrast Associated Cement — vitiated — with Tata Oil Mills — upheld).
  • Reasoned punishment order: stand at the removal of the punishment from the standing orders, on the findings, and on proportionality. Absence of reasons is itself a ground of challenge where the standing orders require them (and, for public servants, under the CCS (CCA) Rules).

B.6 If the enquiry is defective or none is held — the Firestone principles

In Workmen of Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. of India P. Ltd. v. Management, AIR 1973 SC 1227, the Supreme Court summarised the law in nine principles which every Unit 4 (and Unit 5) answer should be able to cite. They are set out in full in Part E.5; the four that matter most for Unit 4 are:

“(2) Before imposing the punishment, an employer is expected to conduct a proper enquiry in accordance with the provisions of the Standing Orders, if applicable, and principles of natural justice. The enquiry should not be an empty formality.
(3) When a proper enquiry has been held … and the finding of misconduct is a plausible conclusion flowing from the evidence … the Tribunal has no jurisdiction to sit in judgment over the decision of the employer as an appellate body. The interference … will be justified only when the findings … are perverse or the management is guilty of victimisation, unfair labour practice or mala fide.
(5) The effect of an employer not holding an enquiry is that the Tribunal would not have to consider only whether there was a prima facie case. … the issue about the merits of the impugned order is at large before the Tribunal … A case of defective enquiry stands on the same footing as no enquiry.
(7) It has never been recognised that the Tribunal should straight away, without anything more, direct reinstatement of a dismissed or discharged employee, once it is found that no domestic enquiry has been held or the said enquiry is found to be defective.” — Firestone Tyre (1973), principles (2), (3), (5) and (7)

Consequence of a defective enquiry in one line: the employer loses the benefit of the domestic findings; the misconduct is at large before the Tribunal; the employer may ask for the opportunity to prove the misconduct by fresh evidence at the appropriate stage (and the Tribunal must grant it), and only if it fails does the order fall — upon which the Tribunal may order reinstatement or a lesser penalty under §11-A.

B.7 Victimisation and unfair labour practice — the employer’s motive under scrutiny

Victimisation

Dismissal is “victimisation” where the employer’s real object is not the misconduct but the employee’s union activity, or where the punishment is a reprisal for asserting rights. It is one of the four grounds of interference recognised in Indian Iron & Steel and a classic ground under §11-A. The leading authority on proof is L. Michael v. Johnson Pumps Ltd., (1975) 1 SCC 574 — the employer must establish the misconduct; where the misconduct is trivial and the punishment crushing, an inference of victimisation may be drawn; the onus does not lie on the workman to prove the employer’s mind.

Unfair labour practice

§2(ra) IDA defines “unfair labour practice” by reference to the Fifth Schedule, which lists — among many items — “to discharge or dismiss workmen … by way of victimisation”, “not in good faith, but in colourable exercise of the employer’s rights”, and “to take disciplinary action against a workman in the guise of an order of discharge”. An unfair labour practice vitiates the action and is itself a ground of interference; the IR Code carries the concept forward (§2(r) and the Second Schedule of unfair labour practices).

B.8 Time — the newest discipline on the employer

Prem Nath Bali (2015) — the guideline

“Every employer (whether State or private) must make sincere endeavour to conclude the departmental inquiry proceedings once initiated against the delinquent employee within a reasonable time by giving priority to such proceedings and as far as possible it should be concluded within six months as an outer limit. Where it is not possible … due to certain unavoidable causes … efforts should be made to conclude within a reasonably extended period depending upon the cause and the nature of inquiry but not more than a year.”

Industrial Relations Code, §38 — the statutory turn

§38(1) Where a worker is suspended pending investigation or inquiry into complaints or charges of misconduct, such inquiry “shall be completed ordinarily within a period of ninety days from the date of suspension”.

§38(2)–(3) The certified/modified standing orders must provide for payment of subsistence allowance to such worker during the period of suspension, at the prescribed rates — which under the Code are 50% of the wages for the first 90 days and 75% thereafter (mirroring §10-A of the Standing Orders Act, 1946).

This is the clearest example of the shift from judge-made to statutory discipline: what Prem Nath Bali laid down as a guideline (six months/one year) and what Fakirbhai and Ram Lakhan worked out on subsistence allowance (Part E.4) now appear as an express statutory command.

Worked example — assembling the whole of Part B in one problem

Facts: A shop-floor operator in a 1,200-worker auto-components plant is charge-sheeted for having abused and pushed a supervisor in the canteen after shift hours. The Plant Manager — who saw the incident — conducts the enquiry; he begins by questioning the operator for two hours about his version; the management’s four witnesses are examined afterwards; the operator’s request for an adjournment to bring two co-workers is refused; the enquiry report relies on the canteen register (never shown to the operator) and on statements recorded in the separate enquiry against another operator. He is dismissed. Meanwhile, a wage revision dispute of the union is pending before the Industrial Tribunal.

Analysis: (i) Misconduct/rational connection: if the altercation arose out of a workplace grievance (supervisory rivalry, production targets), the standing order covering “within or without the factory” applies on Tata Oil Mills; if it was purely personal, it does not. (ii) Enquiry: four Associated Cement infirmities are present — the enquiry officer is an eyewitness; the employee was examined before the evidence; no logic or justification for refusing a short adjournment (contrast Tata Oil Mills, where the failure was the employee’s own); and the findings rest on undisclosed material and on a co-worker’s separate enquiry. (iii) §33: the dismissal is “connected with” the pending wage dispute only if the altercation itself concerns the dispute; if unconnected, one month’s wages had to be paid and approval obtained — and under Jaipur Zila the approval is mandatory. (iv) Remedy: the Tribunal may discard the findings; the employer may seek to prove the misconduct afresh at the appropriate stage (Firestone principle (8)), failing which the dismissal falls; the Tribunal may yet substitute a lesser penalty under §11-A if it finds the punishment harsh, and may award back wages. (v) Time: if the operator was suspended, the enquiry ought to have been completed within 90 days (IRC §38) with subsistence allowance — and any delay not attributable to him counts in his favour.

CSimultaneous Criminal Prosecution and Domestic Enquiry

The question — and why it is entirely judge-made

An employee who is alleged to have committed an offence (theft, assault, criminal breach of trust) may face two proceedings at once: a criminal prosecution in a criminal court, and a domestic/disciplinary enquiry before his employer. Neither the IDA nor the Standing Orders Act contains a single word about whether the employer must wait for the criminal case to end. The rule is therefore wholly judicial, and it has been built up through a line of decisions running from Kushal Bhan (1960) to IOB v. Ganesan (2007). Two of the six prescribed cases — Nos. 10 and 12 — are the foundation, and No. 13 is the most recent refinement; No. 14 supplies the employee’s remedy.

C.1 The doctrine in six propositions

#PropositionAuthority
1There is no absolute bar on simultaneous proceedings. The pendency of a criminal case does not, by itself, disable the employer from holding or concluding a domestic enquiry on the same facts, nor does it make the enquiry mala fide.DCM v. Kushal Bhan, AIR 1960 SC 806; Tata Oil Mills, AIR 1965 SC 155; Kusheshwar Dubey, AIR 1988 SC 2118
2But it is “desirable” and “fair” to stay the enquiry pending the criminal trial — and “advisable” where the case is of a grave nature or involves questions of fact or law which are not simple, “so that the defence of the employee in the criminal case may not be prejudiced”.Kushal Bhan (the very words of Wanchoo J.), reaffirmed in Tata Oil Mills
3Proceeding despite pendency is not per se mala fides or a breach of natural justice. The enquiry is vitiated only if it is otherwise unfair; the employee’s own refusal to participate in a fair enquiry does not help him.Kushal Bhan; Tata Oil Mills (“mere error of law or fact does not make the conclusion mala fide”)
4No strait-jacket formula; the question is fact-specific — and the employee’s remedy is to seek a stay/injunction from a court. Absent such an order, “the disciplinary authority is free to exercise its lawful powers”; wilful breach of a stay order is contempt.Kusheshwar Dubey, quoting Jang Bahadur (Delhi HC) with approval
5The stay test is conjunctive: staying is warranted where (i) the two proceedings rest on identical/similar facts and the same charges, and (ii) the charge is of a grave nature involving complicated questions of law and fact, and (iii) the employee’s defence would be prejudiced. “Not only the charges must be grave but … the case must involve complicated questions of law and fact.”State of Rajasthan v. B.K. Meena, (1996) 6 SCC 417; Capt. M. Paul Anthony v. Bharat Gold Mines, (1999) 3 SCC 679; Kendriya Vidyalaya Sangathan v. T. Srinivas, (2004) 7 SCC 442; IOB v. P. Ganesan, (2008) 1 SCC 650
6Counter-weights to a stay: a stay is never “a matter of course”; the departmental proceedings cannot be unduly delayed; where the criminal case is not proceeding or is being delayed, the enquiry may be resumed; where there are additional charges not forming part of the criminal case, or the employee’s conduct (adjournments, non-participation, dilatory tactics) disentitles him, no stay — and, in the writ context, the relief is refused on the ground of conduct alone.Paul Anthony, principle (v); HPCL v. Sarvesh Berry, (2005) 10 SCC 471; IOB v. Ganesan

C.2 The chain of authority — what each case added

C.3 Standards of proof — why the two proceedings can end differently

Criminal trial

Proof beyond reasonable doubt; the prosecution must establish every ingredient of the offence; the accused has a right to silence and cannot be compelled to disclose his defence; the consequence is conviction, imprisonment and stigma. In Kushal Bhan the criminal court acquitted the peon on 8 April 1958 because “the case against him was not free from doubt”.

Domestic enquiry

Proof on the preponderance of probabilities — a lower standard — and the enquiry is not fettered by the technical rules of the Evidence Act or the Criminal Procedure Code. Hence a criminal acquittal does not automatically nullify a domestic finding (Kushal Bhan), and conversely the departmental finding is not a “finding of guilt” for criminal purposes. Only where the acquittal is honourable and on the merits, and the two proceedings are on identical facts, will it normally be fatal to the disciplinary action (G.M. Tank) — a nuance on which IOB v. Ganesan expressly distinguished that case.

Strategic/error-avoidance notes for problem questions
  • Do not answer “the enquiry must be stayed”. The correct answer is: “there is no legal bar to proceeding; it is desirable/advisable to stay if the charge is grave and involves complicated questions of law and fact and the employee’s criminal defence would be prejudiced; failing which the employee must obtain a stay/injunction” — with Kushal Bhan, Kusheshwar Dubey and IOB v. Ganesan cited in that order.
  • The employee’s own conduct is now decisive. In Kushal Bhan the workman refused to participate and the enquiry was upheld; in IOB v. Ganesan the respondents took adjournments, did not file a show-cause reply, did not cross-examine the management witnesses though they had appointed a defence representative, then rushed to the High Court — the Supreme Court refused relief for that reason alone and awarded ₹25,000 costs.
  • Additional charges change the answer. Where the employer also charges damage to its own property or breach of a specific standing order that is not part of the criminal case, a stay is not warranted (IOB v. Ganesan).
  • Two proceedings, one employee, two defendants’ positions: if the employee is the complainant in a counter-case (as in IOB v. Ganesan, where the respondents filed a counter-FIR against the President of the rival union), the equity of “prejudice” is weaker.
Real examples
  • Bank fraud: a clerk is prosecuted for misappropriation under §§403/408/409 IPC and simultaneously proceeded against departmentally for “dishonesty in connection with the bank’s business”. The charge is grave and the criminal case may involve complicated documentary questions — but banks routinely proceed after framing charges, and courts have upheld this where the departmental enquiry does not depend on the same evidence.
  • Workplace violence: an assault on a supervisor (the facts of Kusheshwar Dubey) — same facts in both proceedings, charge grave → stay generally appropriate if asked for in time.
  • Senior-executive sexual-harassment cases: where parallel criminal proceedings under the penal law and departmental proceedings under the POSH Act/standing orders run together, courts have generally permitted the departmental process to continue in view of its distinct statutory mandate and the “undue delay” caution in Paul Anthony principle (iv).

⑩ Delhi Cloth & General Mills Ltd. v. Kushal Bhan CASE No. 10 · Unit 4

AIR 1960 SC 806 · (1960) 3 SCR 227 · Appeal by special leave under Article 136 from the Industrial Tribunal · Bench: K.N. Wanchoo J. · A §33(2)(b) approval case
Context — why this case is the first page of Unit 4

This is the foundational case on whether an employer must await the outcome of a criminal trial before proceeding with (or completing) a domestic enquiry on the same facts. It is also a case about the limits of the Tribunal’s jurisdiction under §33(2) — the Tribunal is not an appellate forum over the employer’s disciplinary discretion. Every later case in the line begins with a citation of Kushal Bhan.

Facts
  1. The appellant was a company “carrying on the manufacture of textiles”; the respondent, Kushal Bhan, was in the company’s employ as a peon.
  2. On 24 August 1957, the cycle of Ram Chandra, Head Clerk of the Folding Department, was stolen. The matter was reported to the police.
  3. Some time later the cycle was recovered from the railway station cycle stand at the instance of the respondent, who took the police there and picked out the stolen cycle from among 50/60 cycles standing there.
  4. This was brought to the company’s notice in October 1957, whereupon a charge-sheet was served alleging (i) that he had stolen the cycle, (ii) that it had been recovered at his instance, and (iii) that a criminal case was pending against him; he was asked to show cause why he should not be dismissed for misconduct.
  5. He submitted his explanation on 13 October 1957; it was found unsatisfactory and 14 November 1957 was fixed for the enquiry.
  6. At the enquiry he appeared before the enquiry committee but stated that, as the criminal case was pending, he did not want to produce any defence till the matter was decided by the court; he did not want to take part in the enquiry and was not prepared to answer any questions. He eventually left the place.
  7. The company completed the enquiry and directed his dismissal “on the ground that the misconduct had been proved against him”. It then applied under §33(2) of the IDA for the Tribunal’s approval of the action taken (an industrial dispute being already pending).
  8. In the meantime, on 8 April 1958, the respondent was acquitted by the criminal court — “on the ground that the case against him was not free from doubt”. A copy of the criminal judgment was produced before the Tribunal, which refused to approve the dismissal.
  9. The company appealed by special leave.
Issues
  1. Was the company bound to wait for the result of the criminal trial before holding the enquiry and dismissing the workman for misconduct?
  2. Did the workman’s refusal to participate vitiate the enquiry, which was otherwise fairly conducted?
  3. Could the Tribunal refuse approval under §33(2) merely because of the subsequent criminal acquittal — or did it thereby exceed its jurisdiction?
Arguments
For the company (appellant)
  1. The company was not bound to wait for the result of the criminal trial; it could, and did, hold a fair enquiry against the respondent.
  2. If the respondent refused to participate and left the place of enquiry, the company “could do no more than to complete it and come to such conclusion as was possible on the evidence before it”.
  3. The Tribunal, in refusing approval on the strength of the criminal acquittal, lost sight of the limits of its jurisdiction under §33(2) — its role is to see whether a prima facie case for the action existed and whether the enquiry was fair, not to re-try the misconduct.
For Kushal Bhan (respondent)
  1. Principles of natural justice require that an employer should wait at least for the decision of the criminal trial court before taking disciplinary action where the subject-matter of the enquiry is the very same matter as the criminal charge.
  2. Since the employer did not wait, the employee was justified in not taking part in the disciplinary proceedings.
  3. The acquittal — even though “not free from doubt” — demonstrated that the theft charge had not been established, and the Tribunal was entitled to take it into account in refusing approval.
Decision (Holding)
  • Appeal allowed; the Tribunal’s order set aside; approval granted to the order of dismissal.
  • No absolute rule of natural justice requires an employer to wait. “It is true that very often employers stay enquiries pending the decision of the criminal trial courts and that is fair; but we cannot say that principles of natural justice require that an employer must wait for the decision at least of the criminal trial court before taking action against an employee.”
  • But where the case is grave or complex, it is “advisable” to wait: “if the case is of a grave nature or involves questions of fact or law, which are not simple, it would be advisable for the employer to await the decision of the trial court, so that the defence of the employee in the criminal case may not be prejudiced.”
  • On the facts, the case was “of a very simple nature” — the theft of a bicycle — so the employer “cannot be blamed for the course adopted by him”; “there was … no failure of natural justice in this case, and if the respondent did not choose to take part in the enquiry no fault can be found with that enquiry.”
  • The Tribunal “patently erred in not granting approval under §33(2)” and “has completely lost sight of the limits of its jurisdiction under §33(2)”.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. The default is managerial autonomy: the pendency of a criminal case is no bar to a domestic enquiry or to dismissal; participation in the enquiry is not excused by the pendency of criminal proceedings.
  2. The exception is “desirability”, calibrated to gravity and complexity: where the charge is grave or questions of fact/law are not simple, the employer ought, as a matter of fairness, to await the criminal outcome — so that the employee’s criminal defence is not compromised. This is not a rule of natural justice, but a rule of prudence which courts will enforce through the discretion of approval/adjudication.
  3. The two proceedings have different purposes and standards: a subsequent criminal acquittal — especially one founded on “benefit of doubt” — does not invalidate a fairly conducted domestic enquiry, nor does it oblige the Tribunal to refuse approval.
  4. §33(2) jurisdiction is limited: the Tribunal is to satisfy itself about the fairness of the enquiry and the bona fides of the action; it may not act as a court of appeal on the merits so as to substitute its own view of the evidence.
Principles & Observations (obiter)
  • The Court cited with approval the Labour Appellate Tribunal’s approach in Bimal Kanta Mukherjee v. M/s Newsman’s Printing Works — that employers commonly and fairly stay enquiries pending criminal trials, without this being a requirement of natural justice.
  • The “grave nature or complex questions” formula — the phrase the examiner looks for — acquires its later technical meaning through B.K. Meena and IOB v. Ganesan: it is conjunctive (grave and complicated).
  • The case is also authority for the proposition that conduct of the delinquent employee matters: having refused to participate in a fair enquiry, he cannot complain that the enquiry proceeded.
How to use Kushal Bhan
  • Simultaneous-proceedings question (Dec 2024 Q.5): open with this case for the default rule, then add the exception, then Tata Oil Mills, Kusheshwar Dubey and IOB v. Ganesan.
  • §33(2) limit question: quote “completely lost sight of the limits of its jurisdiction under §33(2)” — a ready-made line for the proposition that approval proceedings are not a full adjudication.
  • Effect of acquittal question: pair with G.M. Tank (honourable acquittal, same facts) to show both sides of the rule.
Precision guards
  • The employer did not obtain approval before dismissal: §33(2)(b) requires punishment followed by an application for approval; the case is a model of that procedure.
  • Do not say the Supreme Court held that staying is mandatory in grave cases. The word used is “advisable” — and the Court expressly declined to make it a rule of natural justice.
  • Do not say the acquittal was “honourable”: the Court itself records that it was because the case was “not free from doubt”.

⑪ Associated Cement Co. Ltd. v. Workmen CASE No. 11 · Unit 4

(1964) 3 SCR 652 · AIR 1964 SC 1595 · Appeal by special leave from the award of the Industrial Tribunal, Punjab, Patiala · Bench: P.B. Gajendragadkar J. · Reference under §10(1)(d) IDA
Context — the procedural checklist case

Associated Cement is the authority for the essential procedural requirements of a valid domestic enquiry — in particular (i) that an officer who witnessed the alleged misconduct should not also hold the enquiry, (ii) that evidence must be led before the charged employee is examined, (iii) that the employee should be told in advance when the enquiry will be held, and (iv) that no reliance may be placed on material not put to him. It is the case to cite whenever facts describe a “rough and ready” enquiry.

Facts
  1. The dispute concerned the dismissal of five workmen of the Bhupendra Cement Works, Surajpur: Mehnga Ram (bar bender), Janak Raj Soni (store clerk), Vishwa Nath Bali (painter), Daulat Singh (motor driver) and Malak Ram Khanna (turner). The Government of Punjab referred the dispute to the Industrial Tribunal, Patiala, under §10(1)(d).
  2. Incident 1 — 1 May 1952: the management arranged a cinema show in the Club grounds at Surajpur for its workmen. At about 8 p.m., during the film, confusion was created in the hall by some employees and shouts were raised; among those who shouted was Malak Ram. Owing to the rowdyism, the show had to be cancelled.
  3. Incident 2 — 12 August 1952, 7 a.m.: Mehnga Ram, Janak Raj and Daulat Singh were alleged to have stopped workmen from entering the factory after they had punched their cards, shouted slogans, and caused cessation of work for about half an hour.
  4. Incident 3 — 14 October 1952, 4 p.m.: Mehnga Ram and Janak Raj were alleged to have collected workers in front of the main office building, instigated co-workers to strike and resort to violence, abused officers and disturbed office work.
  5. Incident 4 — 20 October 1952, 7 a.m.: Mehnga Ram, Janak Raj, Vishwa Nath and Daulat Singh were alleged to have stopped workmen at the factory gate and shouted hostile slogans.
  6. Three separate Boards of Enquiry (in each case the Manager, the Assistant Manager and the Chief Engineer) were constituted: the first for the 1 May incident (Malak Ram alone); the second for 12 August (Mehnga Ram, Janak Raj, Daulat Singh); the third for the 14 and 20 October incidents. All five workmen were dismissed on the findings.
  7. Before the Tribunal it was urged that none of the three enquiries was conducted according to the principles of natural justice. The company did not lead any evidence before the Tribunal to justify the dismissals on the merits; it relied on the enquiry proceedings and contended that the Tribunal had no jurisdiction to sit in appeal over the findings.
  8. The Tribunal held all three enquiries to be defective and, by its award, directed reinstatement of the five workmen with continuity of service and full wages from the date of dismissal to reinstatement.
  9. In the Supreme Court, the parties settled the cases of three workmen (Mehnga Ram, Janak Raj, Daulat Singh) by consent: the award was set aside for them and their dismissal was treated as an order of discharge simpliciter, with ₹3,500 each (less amounts already paid under the stay order) plus gratuity and provident fund. The Court therefore decided the cases of Malak Ram and Vishwa Nath.
The two enquiries examined in detail

Malak Ram — the four infirmities

  1. Charge-sheet on 20 May 1952; he did not appear on 22 May; a further notice on 4 June asked him to show cause, adding that his behaviour amounted to misconduct under Standing Order 16(1). He explained on 5 June; on the same day the Manager replied that his explanation was “not borne out by all that we actually saw and also all that was seen by other independent witnesses”, and fixed the enquiry for 11 June.
  2. The enquiry on 11 June 1952 was held by the Manager, Assistant Manager and Chief Engineer. It began with the examination of Malak Ram himself — “some of the questions put to Malak Ram clearly sound as questions in cross-examination”; four witnesses were examined afterwards, and he was asked whether he wished to cross-examine them (he said he did not), then a fifth witness was examined.
  3. The finding recorded was that he was “one of the ring leaders who instigated and took active part in hooliganism and rowdyism during the cinema show”; he was suspended indefinitely from 13 June and later dismissed. The letter of 12 June told him that his version was inconsistent with “the evidence that the Assistant Manager Mr Mohan had been maltreated and with what the enquiry officers had themselves seen”.
  4. The four infirmities: (a) the three enquiry officers claimed to have witnessed the misconduct themselves; (b) the enquiry began with the cross-examination of the workman; (c) no notice of the specific date of the enquiry was given; (d) the officers imported their personal knowledge and the reports of other witnesses into the finding.

Vishwa Nath — the undisclosed material

  1. Charge-sheet on 21 October 1952 alleging that on 20 October 1952 at about 7 a.m. he had stopped workmen at the factory gate, prevented them from going to duty, and shouted hostile slogans — misconduct under Standing Order 16(ix). He explained on 25 October 1952 (denying presence at 7 a.m. and denying the slogans).
  2. An enquiry was held the same day by the Manager and Assistant Manager: Vishwa Nath was first examined and then five witnesses gave evidence for the charge. His request to cite defence witnesses was later allowed and he examined four.
  3. The enquiry officers recorded three reasons for disbelieving his witnesses: (i) some of the witnesses he had cited were shown by the attendance register as absent on 20 October; (ii) one witness (Bakhtawar Singh) said he came to duty between 6.45 and 7 a.m., whereas the record showed him on duty at 3 p.m.; (iii) in a separate enquiry against Daulat Singh, witnesses had admitted that there was a gathering at the gate, contradicting Vishwa Nath’s witnesses.
  4. The vice: the register was used “without notice to Vishwa Nath”; Bakhtawar Singh was disbelieved on a document never put to him — and the Court held that “the rule that a witness should not be disbelieved on the ground of an inconsistency between his statement and another document unless he is given a chance to explain the said document, cannot be treated as a technical rule of evidence. The principle … is one of natural justice”; and reliance on the evidence in Daulat Singh’s separate enquiry, which “was not recorded in Vishwa Nath’s presence” and which he had no opportunity to test by cross-examination, was “very unfair”.
Issues
  1. Whether the domestic enquiries against the workmen conformed to the principles of natural justice.
  2. Whether an enquiry officer who was himself an eyewitness to the alleged misconduct could validly hold the enquiry.
  3. Whether the Tribunal was justified in discarding the enquiry findings and directing reinstatement with full back wages.
Arguments
For the company (appellant)
  1. The Tribunal has no jurisdiction to sit in appeal over the findings of a properly conducted domestic enquiry.
  2. Even if the enquiry officers had seen the incident, that alone does not disqualify them from holding the enquiry.
  3. The procedure adopted, though perhaps not textbook, substantially complied with natural justice; the workman was heard and given the chance to cross-examine (which he declined).
For the workmen (respondents)
  1. Malak Ram: officers who were eyewitnesses should not have held the enquiry — it created bias and pre-judgment, as the Manager’s letter (which rejected his explanation because it conflicted with “what we ourselves saw”) demonstrates.
  2. Cross-examining the charged employee before any evidence was led against him was procedurally unfair — it compelled him, in effect, to commit himself at the outset.
  3. Vishwa Nath: disbelieving his witnesses on the basis of the attendance register and of evidence recorded in a co-worker’s separate enquiry — neither put to him — violated audi alteram partem.
Decision (Holding)
  • The award was set aside for Mehnga Ram, Janak Raj and Daulat Singh in terms of the compromise (discharge simpliciter; ₹3,500 each plus gratuity and provident fund).
  • The award for Malak Ram and Vishwa Nath was confirmed — reinstatement with continuity of service and full wages as compensation for wrongful dismissal.
  • On the enquiry officer as witness: “If an officer himself sees the misconduct of a workman, it is desirable that the enquiry should be left to be held by some other person who does not claim to be an eyewitness of the impugned incident… If an officer claims that he had himself seen the misconduct alleged against an employee, in fairness steps should be taken to see that the task of holding an enquiry is assigned to some other officer.”
  • On the sequence: “in domestic enquiries, the employer should take steps first to lead evidence against the workman charged, give an opportunity to the workman to cross-examine the said evidence and then should the workman be asked whether he wants to give any explanation about the evidence led against him. It seems to us that it is not fair … that at the very commencement of the enquiry, the employee should be closely cross-examined even before any other evidence is led against him.”
  • On notice: failure to intimate the date “may, by itself, not constitute an infirmity”, but “it would be fair if the workman is told as to when the enquiry is going to be held so that he has an opportunity to prepare himself … and to collect such evidence as he may wish to lead”; “it would not be right that the workman should be called on any day without previous intimation and the enquiry should begin straightaway”.
  • On the overall test: domestic enquiries “need not be conducted in accordance with the technical requirements of criminal trials, but they must be fairly conducted and in holding them, considerations of fair play and natural justice must govern the conduct of the enquiry officer”; the enquiry officers’ reliance on personal knowledge made the enquiry “nothing more than a sham or an empty formality”.
  • Consequence: since the company led no evidence before the Tribunal to justify the dismissals on the merits, the Tribunal “had no alternative but to hold that [the] dismissal was unjustified, and that inevitably led to the order of reinstatement and payment of wages during the period of the employee’s enforced unemployment”.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. An enquiry officer who is a witness to the alleged misconduct should not hold the enquiry; where he does so and imports his personal knowledge into the finding, the enquiry is vitiated.
  2. The correct sequence of a domestic enquiry is: evidence against the employee → his cross-examination → his explanation/defence. Beginning with the employee’s examination amounts to an infirmity.
  3. Advance intimation of the date of the enquiry is part of fair procedure; calling a workman on any day without previous intimation and beginning the enquiry straightaway should ordinarily be avoided.
  4. No reliance on undisclosed material: facts drawn from registers, documents not put to the witness, or evidence recorded in another employee’s separate enquiry without the present employee’s participation, cannot form the basis of a finding against him — the rule is one of natural justice, not of evidence law.
  5. Where the enquiry is vitiated and the employer leads no independent evidence before the Tribunal, reinstatement with wages for the period of enforced unemployment follows.
Principles & Observations (obiter — highly quotable)
  • “It is true that domestic enquiries need not be conducted in accordance with the technical requirements of criminal trials, but they must be fairly conducted…”
  • “We have repeatedly emphasised, domestic enquiries must be conducted honestly and bona fide with a view to determine whether the charge framed against a particular employee is proved or not, and so, care must be taken to see that these enquiries do not become empty formalities.”
  • The worker-protective rationale: “in dealing with domestic enquiries held in such industrial matters, we cannot overlook the fact that in a large majority of cases, employees are likely to be ignorant, and so, it is necessary not to expose them to the risk of cross-examination in the manner adopted in the present enquiry proceedings.”
  • The standard for disbelieving a witness on a document: he must first be given the chance to explain it — “[t]he principle on which the said rule is based is one of natural justice”.
How to use Associated Cement
  • Enquiry-validity problem (the highest-probability Unit 4 question): use the four-infirmity checklist and cite paragraphs 12–15 (Malak Ram) and 18–20 (Vishwa Nath).
  • “Enquiry officer was an eyewitness” fact: quote “desirable that the enquiry should be left to be held by some other person who does not claim to be an eyewitness”.
  • “Employee questioned first” fact: quote the evidence-first-then-explanation sequence.
  • “Reliance on attendance records/other enquiry” fact: cite the Vishwa Nath reasoning — the natural-justice character of the explain-the-document rule.
  • Interaction with §11-A: a vitiated enquiry means the domestic findings go; the employer may still try to prove the misconduct before the Tribunal (Firestone) — and in Associated Cement the employer’s failure to do so was decisive.
Precision guards
  • The Court did not hold that a witness-officer’s enquiry is void ipso jure; it said it is “desirable” to assign the enquiry to another officer, and that where personal knowledge is actually imported, the enquiry is vitiated. Read the two propositions together.
  • Three of the five workmen left under a consent order (₹3,500 each + gratuity + PF; discharge simpliciter) — the ratio is drawn from the two contested cases.
  • The penalty phase is not this case’s theme: it is a procedural fairness case, decided before §11-A existed (1971).

⑫ Tata Oil Mills Co. Ltd. v. Workmen CASE No. 12 · Unit 4

AIR 1965 SC 155 · (1964) 7 SCR 555 · Appeal by special leave from the Industrial Tribunal, Ernakulam · Bench: P.B. Gajendragadkar, C.J. (three-Judge Bench) · Both a §33(2) approval case and a fresh §10 reference
Context — three doctrines in one judgment

Tata Oil Mills decides: (i) the scope of a standing-order clause covering misconduct “within or without the factory” — the “rational connection” test; (ii) the standard for holding an enquiry unfair — a fair enquiry is not vitiated by the employee’s own default in producing witnesses; and (iii) a mere error of law or fact is not mala fides. It is also the case that answers the Nov–Dec 2025 fact pattern (a fight outside the factory over a production bonus).

Facts
  1. The appellant, Tata Oil Mills Co. Ltd. — a public limited company in the soap and toilet-articles industry, with three factories and twelve sales offices — dismissed its workman K.K. Raghavan with effect from 14 November 1955. He worked at the Tatapuram factory, Ernakulam.
  2. It was reported that on 12 November 1955 Raghavan and another employee, Mathews, waylaid C.A. Augustine, the Chargeman of the Soap Plant, while he was returning home after his second-shift duty, and assaulted him.
  3. Charge-sheets were issued to both on 14 November 1955. Two officers were appointed to hold the enquiry, but the union represented that justice would not be done unless an officer from outside Tatapuram held the enquiry; accordingly the General Manager appointed Mr Y.D. Joshi, a Law Officer at the Head Office.
  4. Mr Joshi held the enquiry from 27 to 30 December 1955 and reported on 7 January 1956. As an industrial dispute was then pending between the company and its employees, the company applied for approval under §33(2). The Tribunal approved Raghavan’s dismissal but refused approval for Mathews. Acting on that approval, the company dismissed Raghavan with effect from 14 November 1955.
  5. The union then raised a fresh industrial dispute about the validity of Raghavan’s dismissal; it was referred to the Tribunal on 3 December 1958. On this second reference the Tribunal held that the company “was not justified in dismissing Raghavan” and ordered reinstatement — hence the appeal.
  6. The charge under the certified standing orders was Standing Order 22(viii): “without prejudice to the general meaning of the term ‘misconduct’, it shall be deemed to mean and include, inter alia, drunkenness, fighting, riotous or disorderly or indecent behaviour within or without the factory”. The assault had taken place outside the factory and at a considerable distance from it.
  7. The enquiry evidence — of M.M. Augustine and K.T. Joseph — showed that the assault was committed because C.A. Augustine was in favour of the introduction of the Incentive Bonus Scheme, which one set of workmen supported and another opposed, “with the result that the two rival unions belonging to these two sets respectively were arrayed against each other”; Raghavan had called Augustine a “black-leg” (Karinkali) interested in increased production to obtain bonus.
  8. Raghavan had asked the Enquiry Officer to summon two defence witnesses, M.P. Menon and Chalakudi. The officer told him it was not his duty to call them but, “in order to assist” him, wrote to both. Menon replied that he was unable to attend; Chalakudi sent an unsigned letter which reached the officer on 31 December 1955 — the day he was leaving for Bombay — proposing a date three or four days later.
Issues
  1. Whether Standing Order 22(viii) — misconduct “within or without the factory” — could be invoked for an assault committed outside the factory premises.
  2. Whether the domestic enquiry was fair and in accordance with natural justice, given the Enquiry Officer’s refusal to adjourn for the defence witnesses.
  3. Whether the dismissal was actuated by mala fides or victimisation.
  4. Whether the domestic enquiry should have been stayed pending the criminal proceedings against Raghavan.
  5. Whether the industrial Tribunal could reconsider the findings of fact recorded in a fair domestic enquiry.
Arguments
For the company (appellant)
  1. Standing Order 22(viii) expressly covers misconduct “within or without the factory”; the assault, though outside the premises, fell within it.
  2. The assault was not a purely private matter: it arose directly out of a workplace issue — the incentive bonus scheme and the rivalry of two unions.
  3. The enquiry was properly and fairly conducted: an outside, neutral officer was brought in at the union’s own request; Raghavan was given full opportunity; his witnesses failed to appear for reasons not attributable to the employer.
  4. There is no rule requiring an enquiry to be stayed merely because a criminal case is pending; the Tribunal was wrong to treat the absence of a stay as mala fides.
For the workman/union (respondents) — Mr Menon
  1. Standing orders should not be construed so broadly as to bring purely private disputes between individual employees within the employer’s disciplinary jurisdiction; a private assault may attract the Penal Code, not the standing orders.
  2. The Enquiry Officer failed to secure the attendance of the defence witnesses (Menon, Chalakudi) — a denial of reasonable opportunity; the refusal to adjourn for Chalakudi introduced unfairness.
  3. The enquiry should have been stayed because a criminal case on the same assault was pending.
  4. The dismissal was mala fide/victimising.
  5. Even if the domestic enquiry was defective, the employee should be allowed to lead evidence before the Tribunal (relying on Phulbari Tea Estate) — a submission the Court rejected as a converse proposition.
Decision (Holding)
  • Appeal allowed; the Industrial Tribunal’s order was set aside; the reference was answered in favour of the company — the dismissal stood.
  • On off-premises misconduct: “[i]t would … be unreasonable to include within Standing Order 22(viii) any riotous behaviour without the factory which was the result of purely private and individual dispute, and in course of which tempers of both the contestants became hot. In order that Standing Order 22(viii) may be attracted, the appellant should be able to show that the disorderly or riotous behaviour had some rational connection with the employment of the assailant and the victim.”
  • Applied: the assault was “not a purely private or individual matter” — it was “referable to the difference of opinion between the two in regard to the introduction of the incentive bonus scheme on which the two unions were sharply divided”; therefore “if Raghavan assaulted Augustine solely for the reason that Augustine was supporting the plea for more production, that cannot be said to be outside the purview of Standing Order 22(viii)”.
  • On the enquiry’s fairness: the Enquiry Officer “went out of his way to assist Raghavan”; the officer of a domestic enquiry “can take no valid or effective steps to compel the attendance of any witness”; it is not “the right of the charge-sheeted employee to ask for as many adjournments as he likes”. A refusal to adjourn may in a proper case be an infirmity, but not here.
  • On mala fides: the Tribunal’s own finding was that there was no victimisation or unfair labour practice; its inference of mala fides from (i) the non-adjournment and (ii) the allegedly wrong invocation of S.O. 22(viii) was unsustainable — “even if a judge or tribunal may reach an erroneous conclusion either of fact or of law, the mere error of the conclusion does not make the conclusion mala fide”.
  • On criminal proceedings: reaffirming Kushal Bhan — it is desirable to stay the enquiry where a criminal case on the same incident is pending, particularly where the charge is grave, but it is “very different from anything that if an employer proceeds with the domestic enquiry … the enquiry for that reason alone is vitiated and the conclusion reached … is either bad in law or mala fide”.
  • On the converse of Phulbari: where the enquiry is fair, the employee cannot demand a fresh opportunity before the Tribunal merely because his own witnesses did not appear — “if this plea is upheld, no domestic enquiry would be effective and in every case, the matter would have to be tried afresh by the Industrial Tribunal”.
  • On the Tribunal’s role: “Findings properly recorded at such enquiries are binding on the parties, unless … the said findings are perverse, or are not based on any evidence”, and “it was not open to the Industrial Tribunal to reconsider the same questions of fact and come to a contrary conclusion”.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. Rational connection test: conduct outside the factory falls within a standing order covering “within or without the factory” only if it has a rational connection with the employment of the assailant and the victim. A purely private quarrel does not; a quarrel born of a workplace grievance (production bonus, union rivalry, supervision) does.
  2. Fair-enquiry standard: a domestic enquiry officer has no coercive power to compel witnesses; each side must produce its own; the employee’s own default does not vitiate the enquiry. Refusal of a reasonable adjournment may be an infirmity; refusal to grant indefinite adjournments is not.
  3. Mala fides ≠ error: an erroneous conclusion of law or fact by an enquiry officer does not, without more, establish mala fides or victimisation.
  4. Binding force of fair findings: findings recorded in a fair enquiry, supported by evidence believed, bind the parties and cannot be re-opened by the Tribunal unless perverse or unsupported by evidence.
  5. No “second chance” at the Tribunal for an employee who had a fair opportunity before the enquiry officer.
Principles & Observations (obiter)
  • “It is not the right of the charge-sheeted employee to ask for as many adjournments as he likes.”
  • “[H]ow can it be said that the Enquiry Officer did not conduct the enquiry in accordance with the principles of natural justice?” — where the officer wrote to the defence witnesses and the witnesses themselves defaulted.
  • The case is the direct authority for the proposition that an off-premises assault with a workplace motive is actionable misconduct; the Court accepted the domestic Tribunal’s findings on “what the occasion for this assault was and what motive actuated it” unless shown to be perverse.
How to use Tata Oil Mills
  • Off-premises misconduct problem (the Nov–Dec 2025 pattern): state the rational-connection test, apply it to the motive, and conclude — if the fight was over a production/bonus/union issue it is misconduct; if over a private matter it is not.
  • “The enquiry officer refused to adjourn for my witnesses” problem: distinguish Tata Oil Mills (fair — officer assisted, witnesses defaulted) from Associated Cement (unfair — material not put to the employee). Show why the balance tips each way.
  • Mala fides allegation: quote “the mere error of the conclusion does not make the conclusion mala fide”.
  • Simultaneous proceedings: use the last paragraph of the judgment for the proposition that proceeding is not per se mala fides.
Precision guards
  • The assault was outside the factory “and, in fact, at a considerable distance from it” — do not describe it as on the premises.
  • The Court did not hold that any assault outside the factory is covered; the rational connection qualifier is essential, and examiners penalise its omission.
  • The approved dismissal of Raghavan under §33(2) did not preclude a fresh reference on the same dismissal — an important structural point: approval is not adjudication; the dismissal can still be tested in a reference (and later under §11-A).

⑬ Indian Overseas Bank, Anna Salai v. P. Ganesan CASE No. 13 · Unit 4

2007 (13) SCALE 446 · (2008) 1 SCC 650 · Civil Appeal Nos. 6134-6137 of 2006, decided 23 November 2007 · Bench: S.B. Sinha & H.S. Bedi, JJ. · Appeal from the Madras High Court (W.P. Nos. 26176-79 of 2005)
Context — the modern statement of the law

IOB v. Ganesan is the most recent authority in the prescribed list on simultaneous proceedings and the one that examiners expect for the “dilatory employee” and “additional charges” points. It is also, importantly, a case where relief was refused not because the law favoured the employer’s legal theory, but because the employee’s own conduct disentitled him to the discretionary remedy under Article 226. It is the only one of the six cases that is not in the LB-503 extract (it appears only in the case list) and must therefore be prepared from the judgment/reported text.

Facts
  1. The appellant was the Indian Overseas Bank, Anna Salai Branch, Chennai. The respondents were its employees/office-bearers: P. Ganesan (Assistant Manager and General Secretary of the recognised union), S. Vijayakumar, R. Amalraj and A. Dakshinamoorthy, together with a Deputy Chief Officer, P. Rajalingam. (There were also allegations, in a cross-complaint, against R. Balasubramanian, President of the rival union AIOBEU, and Chairman of the Bank’s Staff Welfare Fund.)
  2. On 27 January 2005, an incident of assault and violence took place inside the Bank premises. An FIR (No. 65 of 2005) was registered at the Thousand Lights Police Station, Chennai at the instance of one L. Balasubramanian, alleging that P. Ganesan and three others, along with the Deputy Chief Officer, had assaulted him on the first floor of the Bank premises, intimidated him and damaged the Bank’s properties. The offences alleged included §§341, 323, 324, 427, 307 and 506(ii) IPC.
  3. The dispute was between two rival unions: the respondents were office-bearers of one union; L. Balasubramanian was the President of the rival union. A counter-complaint (cross-FIR) was lodged on behalf of the respondents against L. Balasubramanian, who was also a party to the criminal proceedings.
  4. On 28 January 2005, the Bank suspended the respondents following the registration of the criminal case.
  5. On 21 February 2005, the Bank issued charge-sheets. The charges alleged, inter alia, indiscipline and insubordination, use of filthy/abusive language, murderous assault on a co-employee inside the Bank premises, damage to the Bank’s property, and conduct bringing disrepute to the Bank’s prestige and image. (The charge-sheet thus included at least one charge — damage to the employer’s property — that was not the subject-matter of the criminal case in the same form.)
  6. An enquiry was held over several dates — 18 May, 25 May, 1 June, 8 June, 15 June, 22 June, 6 July and 19 July 2005; the workmen also participated on some dates. The respondents, who had appointed a defence representative, did not file a reply to the show-cause notice, did not cross-examine several management witnesses, and sought successive adjournments; on 8 July 2005 the enquiry officer proceeded ex parte after the respondents failed to appear; the enquiry concluded on 19 July 2005.
  7. On 16 August 2005, the Madras High Court, in the respondents’ writ petitions, granted interim stay of the disciplinary proceedings; that stay was made absolute on 7 December 2005 on the ground that the departmental proceedings were based on the same set of facts as the criminal case and should therefore await its outcome. The Bank appealed to the Supreme Court.
Issues
  1. Whether the departmental proceedings could continue when a criminal case had been registered and was pending on the same incident.
  2. Whether the charges in the two proceedings were the same and, if so, whether the departmental proceedings must be stayed.
  3. Whether the charges were of a grave nature involving complicated questions of law and fact, and whether the employee’s defence would be prejudiced — the only recognised grounds for a stay.
  4. Whether the High Court, in exercising its jurisdiction under Article 226, was justified in granting relief, having regard to the respondents’ own conduct (approbate and reprobate; delay; non-participation).
Arguments
For the Bank (appellant)
  1. There is no legal bar to simultaneous proceedings; the only recognised ground for a stay is prejudice to the employee’s defence in the criminal case, which is not even pleaded, let alone shown.
  2. The two proceedings rest on different charges: the departmental charge-sheet includes breach of the Bank’s discipline, insubordination, damage to the Bank’s property and conduct prejudicial to the Bank’s image — matters not covered by the criminal case.
  3. The employees’ own conduct was contumacious: they had been granted repeated adjournments, appointed a defence representative, and then declined to participate and rushed to the High Court. A party cannot take advantage of its own wrong, nor can the extraordinary jurisdiction under Article 226 be exercised where the party has not approached with clean hands.
For the employees (respondents)
  1. The departmental proceedings are founded on the very same set of facts as the criminal case; the criminal trial is at its threshold stage and forcing the employees to disclose their defence at the enquiry would prejudice them.
  2. The charges are grave (assault, violence, attempt to murder) and the case involves complicated questions of fact; the departmental enquiry must await the criminal outcome.
  3. Once the High Court, on an appreciation of these matters, made the stay absolute, no interference is warranted — the power of the writ court is wide and discretionary.
Decision (Holding)
  • Appeal allowed; the judgment of the High Court was set aside, and the Bank was permitted to continue the departmental proceedings. The respondents were also directed to pay ₹25,000 as costs.
  • Law stated: “Indisputably, the departmental proceedings and the criminal case can go on simultaneously.” The court may stay the departmental proceedings in an appropriate case, but the ground for a stay is confined: the employee must show that his defence in the criminal case would be prejudiced, and this is “hedged in” by the requirement that the case be of a grave nature and involve complicated questions of law and fact.
  • The test is conjunctive and fact-based: “Only because the charges are grave … would not automatically lead to stay; it was also required to be shown that the case involves complicated questions of law and fact.”
  • Same facts matter: what the High Court ought to have considered was not merely the existence of identical facts but also whether the charges in the two proceedings were the same. Here the departmental charge-sheet contained an additional charge — damage to the Bank’s property — which was not the charge in the criminal case, and the departmental proceedings were thus not founded on the same set of facts in their entirety.
  • Standards differ: even on the same facts, the departmental proceeding is not prohibited, because the standards of proof, the nature of enquiry and the purpose are different; the departmental enquiry can proceed independently.
  • Conduct as disentitling: the respondents had been given adequate opportunities; they took adjournments, did not file a show-cause reply, did not cross-examine the management witnesses, and then invoked Article 226. On that conduct, the writ court ought not to have exercised its discretionary jurisdiction — the High Court “should have taken into consideration the conduct of the respondents”.
  • On G.M. Tank: distinguished — that was a case where the employee had been acquitted, whereas here the criminal case was still pending.
  • The enquiry officer’s discretion: if the respondents had made a prayer for cross-examination that was refused, that was a matter to be considered by the Enquiry Officer in accordance with law — not a ground for a stay of the proceedings.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. Simultaneous proceedings are the norm; a stay is an exception requiring proof of prejudice to the criminal defence in a case that is both grave and complex in its questions of law and fact.
  2. For a stay, the charges themselves must be the same — not merely the underlying incident. Where the charge-sheet includes an additional charge (here, damage to the employer’s property/disrepute to the Bank) that is not before the criminal court, there is no identity of charges and no warrant for a stay.
  3. An employee cannot, by his own delay and non-participation, manufacture a ground for stay. Conduct which shows dilatory intent and lack of diligence — including refusing to participate in the enquiry and rushing to the writ court — disentitles him to discretionary relief, and may attract costs.
  4. The writ court cannot exercise its jurisdiction mechanically; it must (i) examine the identity of charges, (ii) examine the gravity/complexity of the case, and (iii) take into account the petitioner’s conduct. A bald recitation of “same facts” will not do.
Principles & Observations (obiter)
  • The Court placed the line of authority — Kushal Bhan, Tata Oil Mills, Kusheshwar Dubey, B.K. Meena, Paul Anthony, T. Srinivas, Sarvesh Berry — in a single synthetic statement, and it is this statement that is now cited as the settled law.
  • The judgment warns against using criminal proceedings as “a shield” for continuing indiscipline, and against the writ court being used to stall disciplinary proceedings on the basis of allegations of prejudice that are unsupported by the record.
  • Even where the employee is acquitted in the criminal case, the departmental proceedings on a different set of facts or on charges not identical to the criminal charge can continue (G.M. Tank distinguished; Ajit Kumar Nag v. GM (PJ), IOCL to the same effect).
How to use IOB v. Ganesan
  • Simultaneous-proceedings question: this is your opening and closing authority. Open with “the law is no longer res integra — departmental and criminal proceedings can go on simultaneously”, then run the conjunctive test, then apply the employee’s conduct.
  • Fact pattern: any problem where the employee takes adjournments and then files a writ — say the relief will be refused on conduct, citing Ganesan and the ₹25,000 costs.
  • Additional charges: whenever the charge-sheet contains a charge not in the criminal case (property damage, breach of a specific standing order, bringing the employer into disrepute), argue that the Ganesan “different charges” point defeats a stay.
  • Article 226 practice point: the writ court must record a finding on identity of charges, gravity/complexity and prejudice; a failure to do so will result in reversal.
Precision guards
  • This case is not authority for the proposition that a stay can never be granted; it reaffirms the exception, while insisting on strict proof.
  • Do not cite it for the proposition that acquittal is irrelevant; the Court expressly distinguished G.M. Tank on the ground of acquittal.
  • The Bench was S.B. Sinha J. and H.S. Bedi J.; the judgment is dated 23 November 2007 and is reported both as 2007 (13) SCALE 446 and (2008) 1 SCC 650 — cite either, but cite both forms if quoting the citation given in the Unit-4 case list.

⑭ Kusheshwar Dubey v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. CASE No. 14 · Unit 4

AIR 1988 SC 2118 · (1988) 4 SCC 319 · Civil Appeal from the Patna High Court · Bench: K.N. Singhal? — no: Ranganath Misra and M.N. Venkatachaliah, JJ. · Dispute over a Munsif’s injunction restraining simultaneous proceedings
Context — the “no strait-jacket formula” case

Kusheshwar Dubey is the case in the prescribed list that answers the remedial question: what remedy does an employee have against simultaneous departmental proceedings? Answer: he has no inherent right to a stay, but he is free to bring a civil suit for an injunction; if the court grants a stay and the employer wilfully violates it, the employer is liable for contempt. The case also carries the classic statement that it is “neither possible nor advisable to evolve a hard and fast, strait-jacket formula” and that “in the absence of a stay order, the disciplinary authority is free to exercise its lawful powers”.

Facts
  1. The appellant, Kusheshwar Dubey, was employed as an electrical helper at the Balihari Colliery of the respondent, Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. — a public-sector coal company.
  2. On 2 November 1986, an incident occurred in which the appellant was alleged to have assaulted S.K. Mandal, a supervising officer (by some accounts, the officer to whom the appellant reported for duty) and to have committed other acts of misconduct.
  3. On the basis of the same incident, both proceedings were initiated: a criminal case against the appellant, and a departmental/disciplinary proceeding by the company.
  4. The appellant filed a title suit (Misc. / Title Suit No. 1986) in the Civil Court/Munsif at Dhanbad seeking a permanent injunction restraining the company from proceeding with the departmental enquiry during the pendency of the criminal case on the same facts. On 6 December 1986 the Munsif granted an ex parte injunction; the company’s application for vacating it was dismissed; and on 31 March 1987 the injunction was made absolute.
  5. The company appealed/revisioned to the Patna High Court, which on 7 July 1987 held that an injunction could not be issued restraining the holding of a disciplinary enquiry and reversed the Munsif’s order.
  6. The workman appealed to the Supreme Court.
Issues
  1. Whether the departmental enquiry and the criminal prosecution, both founded on the same facts, can proceed simultaneously — and whether a civil court can restrain the disciplinary authority from proceeding with the domestic enquiry.
  2. Whether the employee, if aggrieved, has any remedy against simultaneous proceedings, and what that remedy is.
  3. Whether, on the facts, the injunction ought to be restored.
Arguments
For Kusheshwar Dubey
  1. The criminal case and the departmental proceeding arise out of the same facts against the same person; the enquiry will compel the workman to disclose his defence and will prejudice the criminal defence.
  2. The civil court has jurisdiction to grant an injunction where the departmental proceeding is bound to cause irreparable prejudice; the Munsif and the appellate court below were right.
For Bharat Coking Coal Ltd.
  1. There is no statutory or legal bar to holding a domestic enquiry merely because a criminal case on the same facts is pending (Kushal Bhan; Tata Oil Mills).
  2. The employer’s right to discipline is independent of the criminal law; an injunction restraining the enquiry amounts to restraining lawful action and, in effect, an interference with the company’s disciplinary jurisdiction; the High Court was right to set it aside.
Decision (Holding)
  • Appeal allowed; the High Court’s order was set aside and the injunction (as granted by the Munsif and affirmed in appeal) was restored — i.e., on the facts, a stay of the departmental proceedings pending the criminal case was proper.
  • The governing principle: “[i]t is neither possible nor advisable to evolve a hard and fast, straight-jacket formula valid for all cases and of general application, without due regard to the facts and circumstances of a given case.”
  • Proceeding simultaneously is not barred: “[t]here is no legal bar for both proceedings to go on simultaneously, but … there would be no bar to the employer holding an enquiry … [and] merely because the two proceedings are based on the same facts, the departmental enquiry is not rendered invalid.”
  • The employee’s remedy: “The employee … is free to move the court for an order restraining the continuance of the disciplinary proceedings. If such an order is granted, the employer cannot violate it; and if the employer does, it would be liable in contempt of court. On the other hand, in the absence of a stay order, the disciplinary authority is free to exercise its lawful powers.”
  • On the facts: the two proceedings were based on the same facts, and the Court accepted that it would be proper to stay the departmental proceedings pending the criminal case; the injunction was therefore restored. (The Court approved the law laid down in Jang Bahadur v. Baijnath Tewari, Delhi HC, on the remedy and the contempt consequence.)
Ratio Decidendi
  1. No strait-jacket formula; the advisability of a stay depends on the facts of each case — gravity of the charge, complexity of the questions involved, and prejudice to the employee’s criminal defence.
  2. The pendency of a criminal case does not, by itself, invalidate or bar a domestic enquiry; the employer’s right to proceed is intact unless restrained.
  3. Remedy: the employee’s recourse is to the ordinary civil court for an injunction; the disciplinary authority is bound by an order of injunction, and wilful breach entails contempt.
  4. Same facts + criminal trial pending → a stay is ordinarily appropriate where the employee seeks it; the enquiry may be resumed if the criminal case does not proceed.
Principles & Observations (obiter)
  • The Jang Bahadur reasoning, approved by the Supreme Court, is the practical foundation: disciplinary proceedings may continue unhindered; the employee who wants them stayed must go to a civil court and obtain an injunction; an injunction binds the employer on pain of contempt.
  • The case is often cited together with Delhi Cloth and General Mills v. Kushal Bhan: the earlier case supplies the default rule and the exception; Kusheshwar Dubey supplies the remedy and the “no strait-jacket formula” caution.
  • Note the interaction with the Industrial Disputes Act: where a dispute is pending before a Tribunal, §33(2) governs the employer’s power to punish; where no such dispute is pending (as here), the general law of contract/civil procedure and Article 226 govern.
How to use Kusheshwar Dubey
  • Dec 2024 Q.5 pattern (“can a criminal case and departmental enquiry proceed simultaneously?”): the answer’s structure is (i) no bar — Kushal Bhan, Tata Oil Mills; (ii) no strait-jacket formula — Kusheshwar Dubey; (iii) conjunctive test — B.K. Meena, Paul Anthony, IOB v. Ganesan; (iv) remedy — civil suit for injunction, contempt; (v) conclusion on the given facts.
  • Remedy question: this is the only prescribed case giving the employee’s remedy directly — quote “in the absence of a stay order the disciplinary authority is free to exercise its lawful powers”.
Precision guards
  • The High Court is the court whose order was reversed; the Munsif’s injunction (6 December 1986, made absolute 31 March 1987) stood restored. Do not write that the Supreme Court refused the injunction.
  • The Court did not hold that a stay must be granted in every “same facts” case; it held that there is no bar to a grant and that on these facts the injunction was proper.
  • Do not attribute the “three conditions” of B.K. Meena/Ganesan to this judgment; the conjunctive gravity-and-complexity formulation belongs to the later line.

⑮ Prem Nath Bali v. Registrar, High Court of Delhi CASE No. 15 · Unit 4

(2015) 16 SCC 415 · Civil Appeal arising out of SLP against the judgment of the Delhi High Court in W.P.(C) No. 2046 of 2001 dated 21.08.2008 · Bench: J. Chelameswar and Abhay Manohar Sapre, JJ. (judgment delivered by Sapre J.), 16 December 2015 · A CCS (CCA) Rules, 1965 case read across into industrial law
Context — the “delay in the enquiry” case

This is the only prescribed case on the consequences of an inordinately delayed disciplinary enquiry, and it contains the two propositions examiners most want from it: (i) the six-month (outer limit) / one-year (maximum) norm for concluding a departmental enquiry; and (ii) the standard for judicial interference with the quantum of punishment — interference only where the punishment is “wholly unreasonable, arbitrary and disproportionate … thereby shocking the conscious [conscience] of the Court”. It is also a case of moulded relief: the guilt finding and the penalty stood, but the employee partly succeeded on pension and the counting of the suspension period.

Facts
  1. The appellant’s service: Prem Nath Bali joined the office of the District & Sessions Court, Delhi as a Lower Division Clerk on 1 October 1965; he was confirmed with effect from 6 July 1976; he was promoted as Upper Division Clerk (UDC) on 26 July 1986; and in May 1989 he was posted as UDC in-charge of the Copying Agency (Criminal Side) at Patiala House Court, New Delhi.
  2. The trigger — 23 January 1990: while so working, the appellant himself submitted a written complaint against one Smt. Brij Bala, a Window Clerk of the Copying Agency, to the officer in-charge, alleging that she was “not discharging her duty effectively”, that she “often used to close the counter of the Copying Agency before the prescribed time and after lunch … used to resume her duty after the prescribed time”, that litigants therefore complained to him and he had to depute other officials, and requesting her transfer.
  3. The counter-statement — the same day: Smt. Brij Bala made a statement to the superior officer about an incident of the previous day (22 January 1990): after the application register was closed at 1.00 p.m., applications not entered in the register were subsequently entered in the CD2/Dak register and certified copies were prepared of those applications the same date; she was pressurised to deliver the copies the same date at 2.30 p.m.; and “when she refused to deliver the copy, the appellant quarrelled with her and used unwanted words in the office, which were uncalled for”.
  4. The proceedings: the officer in-charge forwarded Smt. Brij Bala’s statement to the District Judge; on the basis of that complaint a preliminary enquiry was made and thereafter a departmental enquiry was held. On 6 February 1990 the appellant was placed under suspension.
  5. The charge memo: a memorandum dated 18 July 1990 was served on the appellant by the office of the District & Sessions Judge, Delhi informing him that the authority proposed to hold an enquiry against him under Rule 14 of the Central Civil Services (Classification, Control and Appeal) Rules, 1965 (the procedure prescribed for major penalties), enclosing the statement of articles of charge and the relevant documents.
  6. The delay: the disciplinary proceedings, which “commenced on 18.07.1990, continued for more than nine years”. Pending the proceedings the appellant sought revocation of his suspension, but the representation was not considered. Only by order dated 1 March 1999 did the District & Sessions Judge, exercising powers under Rule 10(5)(c) of the CCS Rules, revoke the suspension with immediate effect. The question whether the period of suspension was to be reckoned as period on duty was expressly left undecided and directed to be taken up after the conclusion of the proceedings.
  7. The penalty: by two orders dated 27 October 1999 and 28 October 1999, the District & Sessions Judge imposed the major penalty of compulsory retirement; it was further ordered that the appellant “will not be entitled to any amount more than the allowances already paid during the period of suspension”.
  8. The appeals: the appellant’s appeal was dismissed by the Administrative Judge of the High Court of Delhi on 21 August 2000; his writ petition (W.P.(C) No. 2046 of 2001) was dismissed by the Delhi High Court on 21 August 2008; hence the appeal by special leave.
  9. A note of procedural fairness: the appellant appeared in person (he had no legal assistance). The Court requested Mr. Sreegesh, a counsel present in Court, to appear for him so that the appeal could be decided properly, and recorded its appreciation of the “valuable assistance” he rendered — an illustration of the Supreme Court’s anxiety that a party be heard.

Issues
  1. Whether the departmental proceedings were bad for violation of the principles of natural justice — i.e., whether the appellant had a fair opportunity to meet the charges.
  2. Whether the punishment of compulsory retirement was justified, or was disproportionate to the gravity of the charge so as to warrant interference with the quantum of punishment.
  3. Whether the period of suspension (6.2.1990 to 1.3.1999 — nine years and 26 days), spent without any justifiable cause attributable to the employer, ought to be counted for the purpose of qualifying service and pension.
  4. Generally: within what time must a departmental enquiry be concluded, and what is the consequence of an inordinate delay upon the employee’s service benefits?
Arguments
For Prem Nath Bali — Mr. Sreegesh (three-fold, as recorded)
  1. No case made out: “no case whatsoever is made out against the appellant for imposing the punishment of compulsory retirement”, and the manner in which the proceedings were held showed that he did not get a fair opportunity to meet the charges — the proceedings were therefore bad for violating natural justice.
  2. Disproportionate punishment: “in any event the punishment of compulsory retirement … was not commensurate with the gravity of charge and being wholly disproportionate to the nature of charges, this Court should interfere in the quantum of punishment and reduce it”.
  3. Suspension and pension: the appellant “was kept under suspension for a long period of 9 years and 26 days (06.02.1990 to 01.03.1999) without any justifiable cause on the part of the respondents”, yet the respondents excluded this period while calculating his pension; a direction should issue to count the period of suspension for determining pension and other retiral benefits.
For the Registrar, High Court of Delhi / respondents — Mr. Wasim A. Qadri
  1. Uphold the enquiry: a detailed charge-sheet with documents had been served, the appellant filed his reply, and full opportunity was given to both sides to adduce evidence and to cross-examine — nothing more was required in a departmental proceeding.
  2. Punishment proper: the penalty was imposed by the competent authority under the Rules after considering the nature and gravity of the charges, the findings and the entire service record; the courts do not interfere in quantum save in rare cases.
  3. Blame the appellant for the delay: the departmental proceedings “were delayed due to the appellant’s seeking frequent adjournments from time to time”, so he was “not entitled to claim the benefit of the period of suspension for fixing his pension”, which had been rightly fixed after excluding the suspension period.
Decision (Holding)
  • The appeal was allowed in part — “only to the extent … in relation to fixation of appellant’s pension.” The Court found force only in the third submission; the first two were rejected as without substance. No costs.
  • On the enquiry (natural justice — rejected): “We have perused the record of the departmental proceedings and find that the inquiry officer fully observed principle of natural justice while conducting the departmental proceedings.” The charge-sheet with documents was served, a reply was filed, and “the parties were then given full opportunity to adduce evidence and which they availed of by examining witnesses in their support and by cross-examining each of them. What more, in our opinion, is then required in any departmental proceedings?”
  • On quantum (rejected): once the charges are proved, “it is for the appointing authority to decide as to what punishment should be imposed … as per the Rules”; the authority weighs “the nature and gravity of the charges, findings of the inquiry officer, entire service record … and all relevant factors”. “Once such discretion is exercised … then the Courts are slow to interfere in the quantum of punishment and only in rare and appropriate case substitutes the punishment.”
  • The interference standard: the power is exercised only when the employee proves that the punishment is “wholly unreasonable, arbitrary and disproportionate to the gravity of the proved charges thereby shocking the conscious [conscience] of the Court or when it is found to be in contravention of the Rules” — in which case the Court may remit the case to the appointing authority for a different punishment or substitute the punishment itself. The appellant failed to bring his case within any of these grounds: the penalty “appears to be just and proper and does not call for any interference”.
  • On the delay (accepted): “One cannot dispute in this case that the suspension period was unduly long. We also find that the delay in completion of the departmental proceedings was not wholly attributable to the appellant but it was equally attributable to the respondents as well. Due to such unreasonable delay, the appellant naturally suffered a lot because he and his family had to survive only on suspension allowance for a long period of 9 years.” The Court asked “why the departmental proceeding, which involved only one charge and that too uncomplicated, have taken more than 9 years to conclude”, and found the respondents’ explanation (blaming the appellant’s conduct) “not fully justified”.
  • The norm laid down: “every employer (whether State or private) must make sincere endeavour to conclude the departmental inquiry proceedings once initiated against the delinquent employee within a reasonable time by giving priority to such proceedings and as far as possible it should be concluded within six months as an outer limit. Where it is not possible for the employer to conclude due to certain unavoidable causes arising in the proceedings within the time frame then efforts should be made to conclude within reasonably extended period depending upon the cause and the nature of inquiry but not more than a year.”
  • The relief granted: the respondents had fixed the appellant’s pension “after excluding the period of suspension (9 years and 26 days)” from his qualifying service. Holding that “the period of suspension should have been taken into account by the respondents for determining the appellant’s pension”, the Court directed re-determination of pension by including the period 6.2.1990 to 1.3.1999, payment of arrears of the difference from the date he became eligible to claim pension, and continued payment of the re-determined pension — to be done within three months.
Ratio Decidendi — in five lines
  1. The six-month rule: a departmental enquiry must, as far as possible, be concluded within six months of its initiation, and where unavoidable causes intervene, within a reasonably extended period not exceeding one year. It is a directive norm binding in spirit on every employer — State or private (hence usable directly in industrial-law answers).
  2. Priority: where the employee is under suspension, it becomes “all the more imperative” for the employer to conclude the enquiry in the shortest possible time, to avoid “inconvenience, loss and prejudice to the rights of the delinquent employee”.
  3. Delay and prejudice: where the delay is attributable, wholly or partly, to the employer, the employee’s consequent hardship is a relevant and remediable prejudice — relief may be moulded (counting of the suspension period, arrears of pension) even though the finding of guilt stands.
  4. Quantum review: interference with punishment is confined to cases where it is “wholly unreasonable, arbitrary and disproportionate … thereby shocking the conscience of the Court” or in contravention of the Rules — the authority may be asked to re-impose, or the Court may substitute the punishment.
  5. Suspension and qualifying service: suspension is not by itself service, and the period is not automatically counted; but where the delay in the enquiry is the employer’s fault, the period of suspension ought to be counted for pension and retiral benefits.
Principles & Observations (obiter — highly quotable)
  • “Time and again, this Court has emphasized that it is the duty of the employer to ensure that the departmental inquiry initiated against the delinquent employee is concluded within the shortest possible time by taking priority measures.”
  • The explanation for the norm: after the enquiry the employee “invariably pursues the issue in Court to ventilate his grievance, which again consumes time” — so delay multiplies litigation. (This is why the Court spoke of the enquiry and its aftermath as one continuing prejudice.)
  • “In cases where the delinquent is placed under suspension during the pendency of such inquiry then it becomes all the more imperative for the employer to ensure that the inquiry is concluded in the shortest possible time…”
  • The Court’s interference with quantum is not appellate: “the Courts are slow to interfere in the quantum of punishment and only in rare and appropriate case substitutes the punishment”.
How to use Prem Nath Bali
  • “The enquiry took N years / the employee was under suspension for long” problem: this is the controlling authority. State: (i) the six-month/one-year norm; (ii) where suspension is involved the imperative is greater; (iii) delay attributable to the employer is prejudice; (iv) relief may be moulded in service benefits even where guilt is proved; (v) quantum interference only if it shocks the conscience.
  • “Punishment disproportionate” problem: pair this case with Firestone principle (9) (“punishment … cannot be interfered with by the Tribunal except in cases where the punishment is so harsh as to suggest victimisation”) and with Ranjit Thakur v. Union of India (the “shockingly disproportionate” test) — the two lines (industrial and service) converge on the same standard.
  • Suspension/subsistence allowance problem: bring in §38 of the IRC, 2020 (enquiry to be completed within 90 days; subsistence allowance during suspension) and Rule 10 of the CCS (CCA) Rules (deemed suspension and the “period on duty” question left open when suspension is revoked) — the very issue the District Judge left undecided here.
  • Interaction with §11-A: the case is the service-law analogue of the industrial rule that the Tribunal may substitute a lesser punishment; use it to show that the “proportionality” jurisdiction is now common to both systems, while remaining exceptional.
Precision guards
  • Do not write that the proceedings were quashed for delay. The Court upheld the finding of misconduct, upheld the penalty of compulsory retirement, and allowed the appeal in part only on the pension. The correct emphasis: delay does not by itself vitiate the enquiry, but it does generate compensatory relief.
  • Bench and date: Chelameswar and Abhay Manohar Sapre JJ.; the judgment (per Sapre J.) is dated 16 December 2015; the reported citation is (2015) 16 SCC 415. (Some notes give 9 December 2015 — the LB-503 report prints 16 December 2015.)
  • Statutory frame: this is a CCS (CCA) Rules case, not an IDA case; the rule invoked was Rule 14 (major-penalty procedure) and the suspension provisions were Rule 10. Say so, and then explain the transposition: the same principles govern industrial enquiries as facets of natural justice and industrial adjudication.
  • The two numbers: six months = “outer limit”; one year = the maximum reasonably extended period. Do not invert them.

EThe Statutory Apparatus — Section by Section

Unit 4 sits on four statutory structures: (1) the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 — the disciplinary code of the establishment; (2) the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 — the restraints and the remedies (now replaced by the IRC, 2020); (3) the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 — the current statute; and (4) the CCS (CCA) Rules, 1965 — the service-law model that the personnel-manual/standing-orders regimes imitate. Learn the section numbers in their groups: nothing in a Unit-4 answer impresses more than the right section at the right step.

E.1 Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 — the disciplinary code

Why this old Act is central

Misconduct, the enquiry, the penalties, the notice of termination and the redressal of grievances — all of it is “deemed to be misconduct” and regulated only because the certified standing orders say so. The Act does not itself define misconduct; it makes the employer define it, with the Certifying Officer’s sanction. Hence the first question in every Unit-4 problem: What do the certified standing orders of this establishment provide?

Model standing orders (Central Rules, 1946) — the practical checklist

  • The model SOs supply the default clauses (classification, probation, shifts, leave, misconduct and punishment, grievance) until the establishment’s own orders are certified.
  • Typical misconduct clauses in the model: wilful insubordination or disobedience; theft, fraud or dishonesty; wilful damage to employer’s property; taking or giving bribes; habitual absence/late attendance; drunkenness, fighting, riotous or disorderly behaviour, within or without the factory; gambling; sleeping on duty; interference with safety devices; breach of health/safety rules; acts subversive of discipline; habitual negligence. (Tata Oil Mills was decided on the “within or without the factory” clause, S.O. 22(viii).)
  • There is a separate Central Rules Chapter on the procedure for certification and appeals, and on the model grievance procedure.
  • Exam use: quote the model clause and then apply the “fair and reasonable” test of §4 and the rational connection test of Tata Oil Mills.
IESO Act — the four propositions examiners test
  1. Standing orders are statutory, not contractual: once certified they bind the employer as firmly as the workman; the employer cannot act outside them (§12 bars oral evidence to add to or vary them).
  2. Certification is a quasi-judicial process with a fairness-and-reasonableness filter (§§4–5) and an appeal (§6); modification follows the same route (§10) — a point frequently missed in problem answers where the employer “introduces” a new misconduct by circular.
  3. Misconduct must be found within the standing orders; if the clause does not cover the conduct, the punishment cannot stand merely because the employer finds the conduct reprehensible.
  4. Subsistence and time discipline are statutory: §10-A (50%/75% and the 90-day trigger) plus the “six months/twelve months” norm of Prem Nath Bali together form the delay rule in industrial law.

Numbers: 1% of the total workmen, subject to a minimum of five and a maximum of one hundred protected workmen, distributed among unions by rules.

How “connected” and “unconnected” are decided: the test is the nexus of the misconduct with the pending dispute — not the employer’s label. If the employee was punished for conduct arising out of agitating the very dispute that is pending (e.g., participation in the agitation, assault during a strike over the dispute), the misconduct is “connected”; if the misconduct could have arisen in any establishment at any time (theft, drunkenness, assault over a personal matter), it is “unconnected”. This is the single most examinable analytical step in §33 questions.

E.3 The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 — the current replacement

The IRC 2020 consolidates the IDA 1947, the IESO Act 1946 and the Trade Unions Act 1926. Chapter IV deals with standing orders (§§28–39); the disciplinary/pendency discipline is in §90 with the complaint remedy in §91; §50 replaces the §11-A power of the Tribunal to grant relief (along with §11-A-type relief for other matters).

The numbering trap (note for examiners’ sake)

Course notes circulating for this unit describe the IRC pendant-provision as “§82”. That is wrong. The correct numbers are §90 (pendency restraint, successor to §33) and §91 (complaint/adjudication, successor to §33-A). If you cite “§82”, a strict examiner may mark down the whole answer. State the correct numbers, and you may add a one-line remark that “the corresponding provision of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 was §33”, showing you know both statutes.

How the service-law rules are used in industrial answers — three bridges
  1. Bridge 1 — “fair and reasonable”: the CCS framework shows that an enquiry is meant to be a real decision-making process, not a formality; the industrial cases use the same idea (Associated Cement’s “empty formality”; Firestone principle (2)).
  2. Bridge 2 — proportionality: Rule 11’s catalogue and Rule 15’s reasoned decision demonstrate the “choose the penalty to fit the misconduct” discipline, mirrored in §11-A/IRC §50 and the “shocks the conscience” test.
  3. Bridge 3 — time: Rule 14’s six-month norm and Rule 10’s suspension discipline are the direct ancestors of the Prem Nath Bali norm, IESO §10-A and IRC §38.

G.2 Model answer — Dec 2024, Q.5 (10/15 marks)

“Must a domestic enquiry be stayed pending a criminal trial on the same facts? Discuss the law and advise an employee who has been charged departmentally while a criminal case is pending against him.”

Adapted from Dec 2024, Q.5 · Time budget: 22–25 minutes for 15 marks
1. Introduction — locate the gap in the statute

Neither the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 nor the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 contains any provision on whether an employer must await the outcome of a criminal prosecution before acting on the same facts departmentally. The IRC, 2020 is likewise silent. The entire doctrine is therefore judge-made, worked out in a line running from Delhi Cloth & General Mills Ltd. v. Kushal Bhan (1960) through Tata Oil Mills (1965) and Kusheshwar Dubey (1988) to Indian Overseas Bank v. P. Ganesan (2007). The starting point of the doctrine is the managerial prerogative to maintain discipline (Firestone principle (1)); the qualification is the employee’s right to a fair defence.

2. The foundational rule — DCM v. Kushal Bhan, AIR 1960 SC 806

The company dismissed a peon charged with the theft of a co-employee’s cycle, after an enquiry which he refused to attend because his criminal trial was pending. The Supreme Court granted the §33(2) approval that the Tribunal had refused, holding:

  • “It is true that very often employers stay enquiries pending the decision of the criminal trial courts and that is fair; but we cannot say that principles of natural justice require that an employer must wait for the decision … of the criminal trial court before taking action against an employee.”
  • “If the case is of a grave nature or involves questions of fact or law, which are not simple, it would be advisable for the employer to await the decision of the trial court, so that the defence of the employee in the criminal case may not be prejudiced.”
  • On the facts (a bicycle theft — “a very simple nature”), the employer was not to be blamed; “if the respondent did not choose to take part in the enquiry no fault can be found with that enquiry”; and the Tribunal had “completely lost sight of the limits of its jurisdiction under §33(2)”.

Take-away: the default is no stay; the exception is “advisable”, and it must be calibrated to gravity and complexity.

3. Reaffirmation — Tata Oil Mills, AIR 1965 SC 155

The Court held that an employer who proceeds departmentally while a criminal case on the same assault is pending does not, for that reason alone, act mala fide: “to say that domestic enquiries may be stayed pending criminal trial is very different from anything that if an employer proceeds with the domestic enquiry … the enquiry for that reason alone is vitiated.” The case also repeats the Kushal Bhan caution — where the charge is grave, a stay is “desirable”.

4. The remedy and the “no strait-jacket” rule — Kusheshwar Dubey, AIR 1988 SC 2118
  • “It is neither possible nor advisable to evolve a hard and fast, strait-jacket formula valid for all cases and of general application, without due regard to the facts and circumstances of a given case.”
  • “There is no legal bar for both proceedings to go on simultaneously.”
  • “The employee … is free to move the court for an order restraining the continuance of the disciplinary proceedings. If such an order is granted, the employer cannot violate it; and if the employer does, it would be liable in contempt. On the other hand, in the absence of a stay order, the disciplinary authority is free to exercise its lawful powers.” (Approving Jang Bahadur, Delhi HC.)
  • On the facts — assault on a supervising officer, same facts in both proceedings — the injunction was restored.
5. The modern refinement — IOB v. P. Ganesan, (2008) 1 SCC 650

The Court restated the doctrine as a conjunctive test and refused relief on the facts:

  1. Same charges — “Only because the charges are grave … would not automatically lead to stay; it was also required to be shown that the case involves complicated questions of law and fact,” and the court must see whether the charges in the two proceedings are the same. In that case the bank’s charge-sheet contained an additional charge of damage to the Bank’s property not forming part of the criminal case.
  2. Different standards of proof — departmental proceedings are decided on preponderance of probabilities; the criminal trial demands proof beyond reasonable doubt; hence both may proceed.
  3. Conduct disentitles relief — the employees had taken adjournments, filed no show-cause reply, declined to cross-examine and then rushed to the High Court under Article 226; the Court set aside the High Court’s absolute stay and awarded ₹25,000 costs.
6. The synthesis — a five-line statement of the law
PropositionAuthority
No absolute bar on simultaneous proceedingsKushal Bhan; Kusheshwar Dubey; IOB v. Ganesan
A stay is “desirable/advisable” where the charge is grave and the questions of fact/law are complicatedKushal Bhan; B.K. Meena; Paul Anthony; T. Srinivas (Ganesan)

7. Advice to the employee
  1. There is no automatic right to a stay; the employer may proceed with the enquiry and even punish, subject to §33/IRC §90 if a dispute is pending.
  2. If the criminal case and the departmental charges arise from the same facts, and the charge is grave and complex (e.g., a complicated fraud requiring documentary proof in both proceedings), apply at once to the civil court for an injunction restraining the enquiry; a granted injunction binds the employer on pain of contempt.
  3. Participate in the enquiry in the meanwhile — take every opportunity, cross-examine the management witnesses, lead defence evidence, and claim the enquiry report before the penalty: non-participation and delay are now decisive against relief (Ganesan).
  4. If a dispute is pending before a Tribunal and the employer punishes for connected misconduct without express permission under §33(1)/IRC §90, or for unconnected misconduct without one month’s wages and approval under §33(2)(b) (mandatory: Jaipur Zila), or acts against a protected workman without permission under §33(3), the remedy is a §33-A/IRC §91 complaint, on which the authority adjudicates the complaint as if it were a dispute referred to it.
  5. If the punishment is imposed, the dismissal can be challenged in a §10/§2-A reference; if the enquiry was fair the findings bind, but the Tribunal may still reappraise the evidence and reduce the punishment under §11-A (IRC §50); a subsequent honourable acquittal on the same facts strengthens the case (G.M. Tank, distinguishing a “benefit of doubt” acquittal as in Kushal Bhan).
8. Conclusion

The law balances two interests: the employer’s managerial prerogative to maintain discipline without waiting on the criminal courts, and the employee’s right to a criminal defence that is not prejudiced by a premature disclosure of his case. The balance is struck by a rebuttable default — autonomy now, a stay only on judicial intervention, calibrated by the gravity, complexity and identity of the charges, and always subject to the employee’s own diligence.

G.3 Model answer — Nov–Dec 2025, Q.3 (problem on off-premises misconduct)

“An altercation at a public park: two workmen of ‘Happy International’ fought over the production bonus scheme; the employer invoked the standing order on misconduct ‘within or without the factory’ and dismissed them. The Industrial Tribunal found the fight to be ‘purely private’. Advise / Discuss.”

Nov–Dec 2025, Q.3 (facts as recorded in the Cornell notes) · Tata Oil Mills is the direct authority
1. Issues
  1. Does the certified standing order covering “fighting, riotous or disorderly behaviour within or without the factory” extend to an altercation in a public park?
  2. Did the employer conduct a fair enquiry consistent with Associated Cement?
  3. Was the Tribunal entitled to substitute its own appreciation of the motive? What is the scope of review?
  4. If the enquiry was valid, can the punishment be interfered with?
2. Rule — the rational-connection test

In Tata Oil Mills Co. Ltd. v. Workmen, AIR 1965 SC 155, the Supreme Court read down the identical clause: “It would … be unreasonable to include within Standing Order 22(viii) any riotous behaviour without the factory which was the result of purely private and individual dispute, and in course of which tempers of both the contestants became hot. In order that Standing Order 22(viii) may be attracted, the appellant should be able to show that the disorderly or riotous behaviour had some rational connection with the employment of the assailant and the victim.” And the test was satisfied there because the assault arose out of the victim’s support for the incentive bonus scheme and the rival-union politics around it — “if Raghavan assaulted Augustine solely for the reason that Augustine was supporting the plea for more production, that cannot be said to be outside the purview of Standing Order 22(viii)”.

Corollaries: (i) the standing order must first be read on its terms (IESO Act §4 “fair and reasonable”; §12 — no oral evidence to add to the certified code); (ii) the clause is not to be stretched to police private morality; (iii) but a workplace motive is enough — the fight need not be on the premises.

3. Application

If the fight was over the bonus scheme / union rivalry

It has a rational connection with the employment of both combatants — exactly the Tata Oil Mills situation (a quarrel about production bonus and rival unions). The standing order applies; the Tribunal’s finding that it was “purely private” would be erroneous and liable to be set aside, being contrary to the law laid down by the Supreme Court on an identical clause.

If the fight was over a wholly personal matter (the “wedding” variant)

The standing order does not reach it (the predicted contrast question: a fight at a wedding over a personal matter). The dismissal then fails for want of proved statutory misconduct — the employer cannot punish for conduct outside the certified code; and if the punishment was for union activity it may also be an unfair labour practice (Fifth Schedule, Item 1) and victimisation.

4. The enquiry
  • Specific charge-sheet with particulars of the date, place, participants and the standing-order clause invoked (Punjab National Bank, quoted in Firestone).
  • Independent enquiry officer — not one who witnessed the fight (Associated Cement: “desirable that the enquiry should be left to be held by some other person who does not claim to be an eyewitness”).
  • Sequence — evidence against the workmen first, cross-examination, then their explanation; not the reverse (Associated Cement, Malak Ram).
  • All material put to the workmen — CCTV footage, witness statements of other workers, gate registers; no reliance on a co-worker’s separate enquiry (Associated Cement, Vishwa Nath).
  • Advance notice of the enquiry date and a real opportunity to lead defence witnesses (but no right to indefinite adjournments: Tata Oil Mills).
  • Reasoned report furnished to the workmen with an opportunity to represent before the penalty (Karunakar; S.K. Sharma’s prejudice test).
5. The Tribunal’s review — what it may and may not do
  1. If the enquiry was fair and the finding plausible, the Tribunal cannot sit in appeal (Firestone principle (3)); interference only for perversity, victimisation, unfair labour practice or mala fides.
  2. If the enquiry was defective or not held, the whole issue is at large: the employer may prove the misconduct afresh at the appropriate stage and the workman may lead evidence contra (Firestone principles 4–8; Motipur; Ritz Theatre).
  3. The Tribunal reappraises the evidence (and not the punishment alone) under §11-A; it may order reinstatement or award a lesser punishment, but may not take fresh evidence of its own (proviso to §11-A).
  4. Where the misconduct is proved, the quantum can be interfered with only if “so harsh as to suggest victimisation” (Firestone principle (9)) or, on the service-law analogy, “shocking the conscience of the Court” (Prem Nath Bali).
6. Conclusion (advice)

The employer’s dismissal is sustainable if (a) the certified standing order covers off-premises fighting; (b) the evidence establishes that the fight arose out of the bonus scheme/workplace rivalry; and (c) the enquiry satisfied the Associated Cement requirements. The Tribunal’s bare characterisation of the fight as “private” would not survive, because the question is not where the fight occurred but whether it had a rational connection with the employment. Conversely, if the fight was over an unrelated personal matter, the workmen should succeed — and the correct sequence in the answer is to address misconduct first, enquiry second, punishment third.

G.4 Model answer — the §11-A questions that use Unit-4 law

“The enquiry was held ex parte; the workman was later acquitted in the criminal case. What is the Labour Court’s power under §11-A? Can it reduce the punishment?”

Based on Dec 2024 Q.3; May–June 2025 Q.3; Nov–Dec 2025 Q.6 (bank clerk) · Unit 5 territory, answered with Unit-4 case law
Skeleton, with the Unit-4 anchors marked
  1. Framework: a dismissal for misconduct is a “discharge/dismissal” within §2-A read with §11-A; the Tribunal’s jurisdiction is triggered by a reference. Earlier, the Tribunal’s power was confined by Indian Iron & Steel, AIR 1958 SC 130 (no appellate review; interference only for want of good faith, victimisation/ULP, basic error or violation of natural justice, perverse finding).
  2. The 1971 change: §11-A (w.e.f. 15.12.1971) and Firestone (AIR 1973 SC 1227, paras 36–38) — where a proper enquiry was held, the Tribunal may reappraise the evidence: “what was once largely in the realm of the satisfaction of the employer, has ceased to be so; and now it is the satisfaction of the Tribunal that finally decides the matter”. The proviso confines it to the materials on record.
  3. Ex parte enquiry: an ex parte enquiry is not void per se if notice was properly served and the workman chose not to appear (contrast Associated Cement, where the defects were the employer’s own: eyewitness officer, wrong sequence, undisclosed material). But if the workman was denied the report or a hearing on it, the Karunakar prejudice test applies, and the matter is ordinarily remitted rather than quashed.
  4. Criminal acquittal: does not automatically vitiate a fair enquiry on the same facts (Kushal Bhan: different standards of proof). It becomes decisive only where the acquittal is honourable and on the merits and the proceedings are on identical facts (G.M. Tank), which is why Ganesan distinguished that case.
  5. Relief under §11-A: if not satisfied that the dismissal was justified, the Tribunal may set it aside and direct reinstatement on terms, or give such other relief including a lesser punishment. Quantum interference is restrained: punishment only if “so harsh as to suggest victimisation” (Firestone 9) or shocking the conscience (Prem Nath Bali); the discretion between reinstatement and compensation is judicial (Panitole Tea Estate, Firestone 10).
  6. Concluding balance: the employer’s discipline is protected where the enquiry was fair; but the Tribunal is no longer a mere spectator — it decides, on the record, whether the order was justified and, if not, what relief fits.

G.5 Short notes — the five that keep recurring

Short noteContent in 120–150 words
Managerial prerogative & its limitsThe right to discipline and to choose the punishment is a managerial function (Firestone 1; Indian Iron). It is not absolute: the action must be bona fide, after a fair enquiry, on evidence, and it is reviewable for victimisation, unfair labour practice, mala fides, perversity and breach of natural justice. Since §11-A (1971)/IRC §50, the Tribunal may even reappraise the evidence; under L. Michael the English master-and-servant doctrine of absolute termination has no place in Indian industrial law; under DTC arbitrary termination offends fairness. Conclusion: a regulated prerogative.
§33(1) vs §33(2) IDA (and IRC §90)§33(1): during pendency, no alteration of service conditions connected with the dispute, and no punishment for connected misconduct, without express permission in writing — permission is prior. §33(2): for misconduct unconnected with the dispute the employer may punish, but only after paying one month’s wages and applying for approval — approval is subsequent and, under Jaipur Zila (CB, 2002), mandatory: failure to apply or withdrawal makes the dismissal void and inoperative. §33(3) adds the protected-workman shield. Contravention → §33-A complaint (IRC §91).
Protected workmanUnder §33(3) read with the Explanation and §33(4), a protected workman is a workman who, being a member of the executive or an office-bearer of a registered trade union connected with the establishment, is recognised as such in accordance with the rules. Numbers: 1% of the total workmen, minimum five, maximum one hundred, distributed among unions by rules. No action altering his service conditions or punishing him can be taken during a pending proceeding without the express permission in writing of the authority. The device prevents victimisation of union representatives — the practical guarantee behind the right to collective bargaining.
The rational-connection testWhere a standing order extends misconduct to conduct “within or without the factory”, Tata Oil Mills (AIR 1965 SC 155) requires a rational connection with the employment of the assailant and the victim. A purely private quarrel — even a violent one — is outside the standing orders; a quarrel arising from workplace matters (production bonus, union rivalry, supervision) is within them. The test protects employees from an employer policing their private lives while preserving discipline over conduct that undermines the workplace. Applied in the Nov–Dec 2025 “Happy International” hypothetical.
The 6 months / 1 year normPrem Nath Bali (2015) 16 SCC 415 holds that every employer — State or private — must make a sincere endeavour to conclude a departmental enquiry within six months as an outer limit, and, where unavoidable causes intervene, within a reasonable extension not exceeding one year; where the employee is under suspension, promptness is “all the more imperative”. It supplements the statutory clocks: IESO §10-A (subsistence: 50% for 90 days, then 75%), IRC §38 (enquiry in 90 days) and Rule 14, CCS (CCA) (six months). Delay attributable to the employer is remediable prejudice — e.g., by counting the suspension period for pension.

8The employee’s own delay and non-participation disentitle him to relief — and can attract costs (Ganesan: ₹25,000).
9A subsequent criminal acquittal does not automatically invalidate a fair domestic finding — different standards of proof.
10An honourable acquittal on the merits, on identical facts, is decisive (G.M. Tank).
11An enquiry officer who witnessed the misconduct should not hold the enquiry (Associated Cement).
12The correct sequence is evidence → cross-examination → the employee’s explanation; never cross-examine first.
13No reliance on undisclosed material or on evidence from a co-worker’s separate enquiry (Associated Cement, Vishwa Nath).
14An enquiry must be fair in substance — it must not be an “empty formality”.
15Off-premises misconduct is punishable only if it has a rational connection with the employment (Tata Oil Mills).
16Where the enquiry is fair, its findings bind; only perversity, victimisation, ULP or mala fides open it (Ritz Theatre; Firestone 3).
17Where there is no enquiry or a defective enquiry, the whole issue is at large and the employer may lead evidence for the first time at the appropriate stage (Firestone 4–8; Motipur).
18§33(2)(b) approval is mandatory; its absence or withdrawal makes the dismissal void and inoperative (Jaipur Zila, CB 2002).
19Enquiries should conclude within six months, at the most one year (Prem Nath Bali); statutory clocks add 90 days (IRC §38) and three months for the approval order (§33(5)).
20Interference with quantum is the exception — “shocking the conscience” (Prem Nath Bali) or “so harsh as to suggest victimisation” (Firestone 9) — and §11-A/IRC §50 now let the Tribunal substitute a lesser punishment on the record.
Closing frame — the whole unit in four sentences

An employer’s power to discipline is a prerogative rooted in the contract and the ownership of the undertaking, but in industrial law it is exercised inside a statutory code — the certified standing orders — and inside a procedural morality — natural justice. The courts protect that power from being second-guessed on the merits where the enquiry was fair, but they insist that the enquiry be real: a specific charge, an unbiased officer, evidence before explanation, all material disclosed, and a reasoned, proportionate penalty. The employer’s freedom is further fenced by the pendency restraints of §33/IRC §90 and by the employee’s direct remedies (§33-A/IRC §91, injunctions, references and §11-A/IRC §50 relief). And since Prem Nath Bali, the clock itself has become a discipline: the prerogative must be exercised quickly or compensate in service benefits.
