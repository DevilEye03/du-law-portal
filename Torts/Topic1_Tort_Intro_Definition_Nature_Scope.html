Origin & Development of the Law of Torts in England

Syllabus (a) — Forms of action; specific remedies from case to case.

1.1 The term “Tort”

The word “tort” is the French equivalent of the English word “wrong” and corresponds to the Roman law term delict. It was introduced into English law by the Norman jurists after the Norman Conquest (1066). It derives from the Latin tortum, meaning “to twist” — i.e., conduct that is twisted, crooked or deviating from the straight path. The law of torts thus concerns civil wrongs (as distinct from criminal wrongs) — a wrong committed by one private person against another, which is enforced not by the police but by the injured citizen (the plaintiff) suing in a court.

1.2 Case-by-case growth — the foundation of the Common Law

Before the development of a central legal system, disputes in England were settled case-by-case according to local customs. After the Norman Conquest, the King appointed itinerant judges who travelled around the realm on circuits, hearing cases and applying the best of local village customs. These judges, drawing on their own earlier rulings, began to refer to previous decisions — and when a ruling was relied upon often enough, it hardened into precedent. This is the origin of the common law: law made not by Parliament but by the decisions of courts, built incrementally from case to case. Tort law is therefore quintessentially a judge-made branch of law.

1.3 Forms of Action and Writs

The procedural engine of the early common law was the writ — a royal command issued by the King’s Court (later the Court of Chancery) authorising a person to bring an action. Each writ was a distinct “form of action”, each with its own pleading, its own procedure, and its own remedy. The two most important for tort were:

Form of actionNature & use
Writ of TrespassFor direct, immediate and forcible interference — e.g., trespass to land, battery, false imprisonment. Lay at the root of assorted “trespass” torts.
Action on the Case (trespass on the case)For harm arising indirectly or consequentially, and for wrongs not causing direct force. This was the great remedial device from which negligence, nuisance, deceit and many other torts grew.
The writ/forms-of-action system: a plaintiff could sue only if his complaint fell within an existing form.

1.4 Specific remedies from case to case

Because each form of action prescribed a specific remedy, the scope of tortious liability was for centuries confined to recognised “nominate torts.” If a harm could not be slotted into an existing writ, the plaintiff left court without a remedy. It is this historical fact that gave rise to the famous pigeon-hole theory of Sir John Salmond (see §5), and the opposing view that tort law is a single general principle of liability (Winfield). This “closed” character was progressively loosened by the great remedial writ of action on the case, which allowed the courts to create new causes of action to meet new wrongs.

1.5 Modern development

  • Abolition of forms of action: The Common Law Procedure Act, 1852 and the Judicature Acts, 1873–75 abolished the forms of action as procedural formalities. Substance triumphed over form. Lord Atkin famously cautioned against the “ghosts of the past … clanking their medieval chains” standing “in the path of justice.”
  • New torts judicially created: negligence as a distinct tort (19th c.), strict liability in Rylands v. Fletcher (1868), deceit (Pasley v. Freeman), inducement of breach of contract (Lumley v. Gye), intimidation (Rookes v. Barnard), and the neighbour principle in Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932).
  • Statutory reform: Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act, 1945; Defamation Act etc.; Unfair Contract Terms Act, 1977 (limits exclusion clauses).
Exam point: The historical link between “forms of action” and the modern debate is that the writ system produced a set of distinct nominate torts (law of torts), while the broadening of action on the case supports the law of tort theory. Lord Macmillan: “The categories of negligence are never closed.”

Evolution of the Law of Torts in India

Syllabus (b) — Uncodified and judge-made; advantages and disadvantages.

2.1 Reception of English common law

The law of torts in India is practically English law. It came with the establishment of British courts — beginning with the Mayor’s Courts established under the Royal Charters. The Englishmen administering these courts drew upon the common law and statute law of England “according to justice and right.” The law of torts is thus administered in India as rules of justice, equity and good conscience — that is, Indian courts adopt English common-law principles only to the extent they are consistent with Indian conditions and suited to Indian society.

The Supreme Court in Rajkot Municipal Corporation v. Manjulben (see Case No. 4) expressly recognised this: since there is no statutory law in India regulating tortious damages, the courts “selectively applied the common law principles evolved by the courts in England on grounds of justice, equity and good conscience.”

Not a blind import: Indian courts first test whether a rule of English law suits Indian circumstances before applying it — e.g., the doctrine of sovereign immunity was rejected (State of Rajasthan v. Vidhyawati) and the rule of strict liability was broadened into absolute liability in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India.

2.2 Uncodified and judge-made

Unlike most branches of Indian law (contract, property, crime), the law of torts is largely uncodified. There is no single “Indian Torts Code” or “Indian Damages Act.” It is a judge-made law, developed through precedents. Tortious liability is codified only to a very limited, fragmentary extent:

Statutory areaStatute
Workmen’s injury / employmentEmployees’ Compensation Act, 1923; Workmen’s Compensation Act
Motor accidentsMotor Vehicles Act, 1988 (incl. no-fault & hit-and-run compensation)
Consumer protectionConsumer Protection Act, 2019
Environment / hazardous activityEnvironment (Protection) Act, 1986; Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991
Defamation (criminal), Railways, etc.Indian Penal Code; Railways Act; specific statutes

The Law Commission of India has repeatedly recommended codification (e.g., its First Report on the Liability of the State in Tort, 1956), but no general codification has followed. The House of Lords’ intended reform of damages law in Lonrho-type legislation has been described as a proposal that “died still-born,” leaving India to rely on judge-made principles.

2.3 Advantages of an uncodified, judge-made law of torts

  • Flexibility & adaptability: It can grow to meet new circumstances, technological change and industrial hazards without waiting for Parliament.
  • Capacity to create new torts: The courts can recognise new wrongs as society evolves — e.g., constitutional torts, absolute liability in M.C. Mehta, compensation for violation of Article 21 (Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar; Bhim Singh v. State of J&K).
  • Justice in individual cases: A judge can do justice on the specific facts instead of being bound by rigid statutory categories.
  • Principles based on equity & good conscience: The courts can mould rules to Indian conditions — e.g., rejecting feudal sovereign immunity while expanding State liability in a welfare State.
  • No piecemeal rigidity: Old maxims like ubi jus ibi remedium keep the law responsive to violations of rights that attract no statutory remedy.

2.4 Disadvantages

  • Uncertainty & unpredictability: Because there is no code, a litigant cannot be sure whether a particular harm is actionable until a court decides. This risk is high in a country with costly litigation.
  • Dependence on victims coming forward: A judge-made law develops only if victims litigate; it is reactive, not forward-looking.
  • Cost & delay: The poor and marginalised cannot easily access courts, so the law remains under-developed in areas that are not litigated.
  • Reliance on English law: Indian courts borrow English principles, some of which may not fit Indian social/economic realities.
  • Slow, uneven growth: Development is haphazard and incremental — the law that a court creates in one State or one case may not be adopted uniformly.
  • Judicial activism not always a substitute: Courts can fill gaps, but detailed prospective rules (e.g., compensation schemes) require legislation.
Two faces of the same coin: the flexibility that makes tort law adaptable is also the source of its uncertainty.

Meaning & Function of the Law of Torts

Syllabus (c) — Prescribing standards of human conduct; redressal of wrongs by payment of compensation; injunction.

The law of torts performs two broad tasks that together define its nature and scope:

3.1 Prescribing standards of human conduct

Tort law sets a minimum standard of behaviour that reasonable persons must observe towards others. It is not a code of morality — it prescribes a standard of reasonable care and instructs people not to invade the legal rights of others (e.g., not to trespass, not to defame, not to be negligent). When a person falls below this standard and thereby injures another, tort law steps in. In this sense tort law is “the law of duties” — duties fixed by law, not by the consent of parties.

3.2 Redressal of wrongs by payment of compensation

The primary function is compensation (restorative, not punitive): the wrongdoer (tort-feasor) is made to pay damages to the injured party. Damages in tort are unliquidated — fixed by the court, not pre-agreed. Tort compensation serves to place the victim, so far as money can, in the position he would have been in but for the wrong. However, tort law is not merely compensatory — in cases of malicious or outrageous conduct, exemplary/punitive damages may also be awarded (e.g., Ashby v. White, Bhim Singh v. State of J&K).

3.3 Injunction

Where damages would be inadequate — e.g., a continuing or threatened wrong — the court may grant an injunction, an equitable remedy that restrains the defendant from doing, or requires him to undo (mandatory injunction), a particular act. In Seetharamayya the plaintiffs framed their whole relief around mandatory and permanent injunctions; in Prof. Imtiaz Ahmad v. Durdana Zamir and Rustom K. Karanjia injunctions were sought against further publication.

Definition of Tort

Syllabus (d).

4.1 Salmond’s definition

“A civil wrong which is not exclusively a breach of contract or breach of trust or other merely equitable obligation.”

This is a negative / residual definition. It tells us what a tort is not — it is a civil wrong that is neither a breach of contract nor a breach of trust nor a merely equitable obligation. It does not affirmatively state the basis of tortious obligation. The remedy for a tort is a common-law action for unliquidated damages.

4.2 Winfield’s definition

“Tortious liability arises from the breach of a duty primarily fixed by law; this duty is towards persons generally; and its breach is redressible by an action for unliquidated damages.”

Winfield’s positive/affirmative definition isolates the three essential attributes of a tort:

  1. Breach of a duty primarily fixed by law — the duty is imposed by law itself, independent of any contract, agreement or consent between the parties.
  2. The duty is towards persons generally — it is owed to the world at large, a right in rem, not merely to a particular individual (a right in personam).
  3. Breach is redressible by an action for unliquidated damages — damages are not pre-determined but fixed by the court.

4.3 Fraser’s definition

“A tort is an infringement of a right in rem of a private individual, giving a right of compensation at the suit of the injured party.”

4.4 Common elements distilled

Taken together, definitions converge on the following: a tort is a civil wrong consisting of a wrongful act or omission which is a breach of a legal duty (a duty fixed by law, owed to persons generally), which causes legal damage, and for which the remedy is unliquidated damages at the suit of the injured party.

Why both definitions matter: Salmond’s definition (a civil wrong) is the one DU most often expects and is quoted in the Atul Bhardwaj mind-map. Winfield’s definition is preferred for examining the nature of tortious liability and for distinguishing tort from contract and crime.

“Is it the Law of Tort or the Law of Torts?”

Foundation of tortious liability — Winfield & Salmond’s competing theories.

Professor Salmond posed the question: does the law of torts consist of a fundamental general principle that it is wrongful to harm others absent justification, or does it consist of a number of specific rules prohibiting certain named harmful activities, leaving all else outside liability?

(A) Larger theory — the Law of Tort (Winfield & Pollock)

“All injuries done by one person to another are torts, unless there is some justification recognised by law.”

  • Every unjustifiable harm is tortious.
  • Courts may create new torts; a plaintiff need not find a label.
  • Supported by negligence as a new tort, Rylands v Fletcher, Donoghue v Stevenson, Ashby v White (ubi jus ibi remedium).
  • Lord Macmillan: “The categories of negligence are never closed.”
  • Holt CJ in Ashby: “If man will multiply injuries, actions must be multiplied too.”
(B) Narrower theory — the Law of Torts (Salmond)

There is no law of tort, only a law of torts — a set of pigeon-holes, each containing a labelled tort.

  • Liability arises only if the wrong falls into one of the recognised (“nominate”) torts.
  • “Just as the criminal law consists of rules establishing specific offences, so the law of torts consists of rules establishing specific injuries.”
  • No general principle of liability; the plaintiff must prove the case falls within a specific rule.
  • Based on the early writ/forms of action practice (no writ → no remedy).
  • Dr Jenks supports Salmond but adds courts can create new torts (though substantially similar to existing ones).
Winfield’s broader view vs Salmond’s pigeon-hole view.

Reconciliation

The correct approach is a middle ground. There are a number of nominate torts, but a person sustaining a loss does not have to find a label to obtain compensation — courts have granted relief for new claims (e.g., nervous shock). Winfield himself modified his position: “From a narrow and practical point of view, Salmond’s theory will suffice, but from a broader outlook, his theory is valid.”

An Indian decision, Lala Pannalal v. Kasturi Chand Ramaji (AIR 1946 Mad 147), holds that there is no exhaustive classification of torts beyond which courts should not proceed; new invasions of rights devised by human ingenuity may give rise to new torts (e.g., malicious house-search). Indian judiciary has favoured Winfield’s wider theory (see M.C. Mehta — “we have to evolve new principles … which will adequately deal with new problems which arise in a highly industrialised economy”).

Constituents of Tort

Syllabus (e) — Wrongful act, legal damage and remedy.

For an action to lie in tort, three ingredients must co-exist:

  1. Wrongful act (or omission) — The defendant must have committed a legal wrong — a positive act (misfeasance) or a failure to act where a legal duty to act exists (non-feasance). An act that is merely a moral or social wrong, or that causes harm without violating any legal right, is not tortious.
  2. Legal damage (violation of a legal right / injuria) — There must be an infringement of a right recognised by law. Under the ubi jus ibi remedium principle, where there is a legal right there must be a remedy. The damage must be one the law will redress; mere loss or inconvenience the law does not regard is not injuria.
  3. Legal remedy / damnum (actual damage, or actionable per se) — Either the plaintiff has suffered actual loss (damnum), or the act is actionable per se without proof of damage (in case of injuria sine damnum). The remedy is usually unliquidated damages or an injunction.
Three-step structure of tortious liability.

Injuria sine damnum & Damnum sine injuria

Syllabus (e) — the two crucial maxims about “damage.”

7.1 Injuria sine damnum (legal injury without damage)

Injuria = violation/infringement of a legal right; sine = without; damnum = actual loss or damage (money, comfort, health). Together: infringement of a legal right without any actual loss or damage to the plaintiff.

If a legal right is violated, the violation is actionable even though the plaintiff has suffered no actual loss. The plaintiff need only prove the infringement of the right — the action is actionable per se.
  • Examples: trespass to land (actionable even if no damage); wrongful deprivation of the right to vote (Ashby v. White; Municipal Corpn. of Agra v. Asharfi Lal); wrongful detention of an MLA violating Article 21 (Bhim Singh) — in the last case the Supreme Court awarded exemplary damages of ₹50,000.
  • Remedy: nominal damages are awarded to vindicate the right; if the violation is malicious or wicked, exemplary/punitive damages may be given.
  • Statutory recognition: Under Section 42 (specific relief analogous) and the principle is also reflected in ubi jus ibi remedium.

7.2 Damnum sine injuria (damage without injury)

Actual and substantial loss suffered without any infringement of the plaintiff’s legal right. The harm may be real, even serious, but the law gives no remedy because no legal injury (injuria) has been done.

“Law does not take into account all harms suffered by a person which caused no legal injury. Damage so done is called damnum sine injuria.” — Town Area Committee v. Prabhu Dayal
  • Examples: loss caused by lawful competition (Gloucester Grammar School); use of one’s own property however motivated (Mayor of Bradford v. Pickles); protection of one’s land against floodwater (Seetharamayya); demolition of an illegally-built structure (Town Area Committee v. Prabhu Dayal); trade combinations in furtherance of business (Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor Gow & Co.); interception of percolating underground water (Acton v. Blundell; Chasemore v. Richards); loss from a negligently-delivered telegram to a stranger (Dickson v. Reuter’s Telegraph Co.); unlawful competition or hurt to one’s property from exercise of another’s lawful right.

The often-quoted test (from Town Area Committee): a legal act, though motivated by malice, does not make the actor liable; the plaintiff can get compensation only if he proves he suffered injury due to an illegal act of the defendant.

Four-fold matrix of the two maxims (the pattern used in the DU revision flowchart).

Ubi Jus Ibi Remedium

“Where there is a right, there is a remedy.”

“If the plaintiff has a right, he must of necessity have a means to vindicate and maintain it, and a remedy if he is injured in the exercise or enjoyment of it; and indeed it is a vain thing to imagine a right without a remedy; for want of right and want of remedy are reciprocal.” — Lord Holt CJ, Ashby v. White (1703)

This maxim is the jurisprudential basis of injuria sine damnum and the whole remedial character of tort law. Every violation of a legal right must be redressed. The purpose of the law is served — and the violation of a legal right should not remain without a remedy — by awarding at least nominal damages.

Its limits: The maxim operates only where there is a legal right. It does not apply to mere damage without any violation of a legal right (damnum sine injuria) — e.g., loss from lawful competition in the Gloucester Grammar School case, or loss from a harm where the defendant exercised a legal right (Pickles).

Relevance of Intention, Motive & Malice

Syllabus (g).

10.1 Intention

Intention (the defendant’s deliberate desire that the consequence follow) is relevant in torts that are intentional — e.g., battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass, deceit, malicious prosecution, conspiracy. It distinguishes an intentional tort from negligence or an accident. Intention is also relevant to the quantum of damages — a deliberate wrong may attract unliquidated and exemplary damages. But intention is not an essential element of tortious liability generally; liability may arise without any wrongful intention (see strict & absolute liability below).

10.2 Motive

Motive (why the act was done) is, as a general rule, irrelevant in tort. The law asks what the defendant did, not why he did it. A good motive does not justify an act otherwise illegal; a bad motive does not make wrongful an act otherwise legal.

“No use of property which would be legal if due to a proper motive can become illegal because it is prompted by a motive which is improper or even malicious.” — Lord Watson, Mayor of Bradford v. Pickles

“In such a case, motives are immaterial; it is the act, not the motive for the act, that must be regarded. If the act apart from the motive gives rise merely to damage without legal injury, the motive, however reprehensible, will not supply that element.” — Lord Macnaghten, same case.

Similarly in Allen v. Flood (1896): an act lawful in itself is not converted into an unlawful act by a malicious motive. And in Town Area Committee v. Prabhu Dayal: “A legal act, though motivated by malice, will not make the actor liable … Mere malice cannot disentitle a person from taking recourse to law.”

10.3 Malice — two senses

Malice in law

Means more than mere evil motive. It signifies either (a) the intentional doing of a wrongful act without just cause or excuse, or (b) an action determined by an improper motive. It is presumed from an unlawful act.

“Malice in common acceptation means ill-will against a person, but in its legal sense it means a wrongful act done intentionally without just cause or excuse.” — Bayley J, Bromage v. Prosser (1825)

Malice in fact (evil motive)

Means an evil motive — a feeling of ill-will. When a wrongful act is done with ill-will, it is done “maliciously.”

Relevant in torts where a good motive is the justification — e.g., malicious prosecution, and in defamation where malice in fact defeats qualified privilege (see Rustom K. Karanjia — malice found; and Prof. Imtiaz Ahmad — no cause of action). It may escalate damages (exemplary) where the wrong is malicious (Ashby, Bhim Singh).

Malice in law (presumed from the unlawful act) vs malice in fact (evil motive).

10.4 When fault is relevant & when irrelevant

Fault relevantFault irrelevant (liability without fault)
Intentional torts — assault, battery, false imprisonment, deceit, malicious prosecution, conspiracy, trespassTort of conversion; defamation; vicarious liability; strict liability (Rylands v. Fletcher); absolute liability (M.C. Mehta)

The 8 Prescribed Case Laws — Full Analysis

Each case is set out as: Facts · Issues · Arguments of both sides · Decision · Principle evolved. Quotations are from the judgments.

1. White v. John Warrick & Co. Ltd.

(1953) 2 All ER 1021 (CA)
Facts

The plaintiff, a newsagent and tobacconist at Canonbury, entered into a written contract with the defendants (owners) that they would supply him a tradesman’s tricycle (with a large carrier in front) for delivering newspapers. The contract was on the defendants’ printed form. By cl. 1 the owners let the carrier cycle for three years at 5s per week; by cl. 2 the owners agreed to maintain the machines in working order and to supply spare carriers. The machine was in use for a considerable period. Towards the end of May 1950 it needed repair. On Saturday, 3 June, a representative of the owners went to the plaintiff’s shop, took away the faulty machine and left a spare tricycle in its place (i.e., purporting to perform cl. 2). The plaintiff did not examine it; he mounted it immediately for work. After about a quarter of a mile the saddle went forward, he was thrown off and injured (an injury to his knee — synovitis — requiring hospital care). The evidence (incl. the plaintiff’s employee Anthony) showed the saddle was loose and the nuts under the seat were rusted and could not be moved. Parker J. made a provisional assessment of £505.

Issues

(1) Was the defendants’ conduct negligent in supplying a defective tricycle? (2) Did the contract contain a breach of warranty? (3) Did the exemption clause (cl. 11) bar the plaintiff’s claim?

Arguments

Plaintiff: (i) The owners were in breach of warranty/contract — bound to supply a tricycle reasonably fit for its purpose; (ii) the owners owed a duty of care and were negligent — had they examined the tricycle they would have found the rusted nuts and loose saddle. Even if cl. 11 barred the contract claim, it did not bar an action for negligence.

Defendant (owners): They denied negligence, breach of duty and breach of contract. They pleaded cl. 11: “Nothing in this agreement shall render the owners liable for any personal injuries to the riders of the machines hired nor for any third party claims nor loss of any goods, belonging to the hirer, in the machines.” Counsel conceded that cl. 11 barred the contract claim, but argued that the alleged negligence arose out of the performance of the contract, so the cause of action was founded on the agreement; since the same facts gave rise to both, the plaintiff should not recover merely by framing his action in tort.

Decision

Court of Appeal (Singleton & Denning LJJ) held that cl. 11 exempted the owners only from strict (contractual) liability, not from liability for negligence. The claim for negligence was founded on tort, not contract.

“… negligence apart from contract gives a right of action to the party injured by that negligence … The fact that there is a contractual relationship between the parties … does not exclude the co-existence of a right of action founded on negligence … though arising out of the relationship.” — relying on Lord Macmillan in Donoghue v. Stevenson

“If a person desires to exempt himself from a liability which the common law imposes on him, he can only do so by a contract freely and deliberately entered into by the injured party in words that are clear beyond the possibility of misunderstanding … if there are two possible heads of liability, one for negligence and the other a strict liability, an exemption clause will be construed, so far as possible, as exempting the defendant only from his strict liability and not from his liability for negligence.” — Denning LJ

Principle evolved: (i) The same set of facts can give rise to both a breach of contract and a tort; the plaintiff may choose to sue in tort. (ii) An exemption clause is construed strictly — it will not be read as excluding liability for negligence unless the words are clear beyond misunderstanding; where there are two heads of liability, it exempts only the strict (contractual) one, leaving the tortious (negligence) claim intact. (iii) A duty of care owed to a third party’s servant shows a fortiori a like duty to the hirer himself.
Exam linkage: This is the authority for the DU 2024 PYQ on whether an exclusion clause in a contract restricts liability in tort for negligence.

2. Town Area Committee v. Prabhu Dayal

AIR 1975 All 132
Facts

The plaintiff had constructed 16 shops on the old foundations of a building called “Garhi.” The defendant Town Area Committee (through its Chairman and Vice-Chairman, defendants 2 and 3) allegedly demolished these constructions illegally, causing a loss of Rs. 1,000. The plaintiff contended that the notice under s. 186 of the U.P. Municipalities Act gave him only two hours to demolish, not the reasonable time contemplated by s. 302; that the notice was served when the plaintiff was out of station; and that the action was mala fide.

Defendants’ plea: The constructions were made without notice under s. 178 and without sanction under s. 180 of the Act. The alleged mala fide was denied; notices had been given on 18 December, the District Magistrate directed action under s. 186, a second notice was given on 21 December, and only then was the building demolished in accordance with law.

Issues

(1) Was the demolition unlawful? (2) Is malice relevant to liability? (3) Did the plaintiff suffer injuria (a legal wrong) entitling him to compensation? (4) Was the damage damnum sine injuria?

Arguments

Plaintiff: The demolition was mala fide and illegal because the notice gave only two hours; he had a fundamental right to hold and enjoy property even if built without sanction, and since the officer acted maliciously, the action was bad.

Defendants: The building abutted a public street and was erected without prior notice/sanction — an offence under s. 185; the plaintiff therefore had no right to maintain it, and the demolition was lawful; hence no damages.

Decision

Hari Swarup J held that the plaintiff could get compensation only if he proved injury from an illegal act of the defendant. The lower appellate court had misdirected itself by concentrating on malice instead of the legality of the action.

“A legal act, though motivated by malice, will not make the actor liable to pay damages … Merely because some officer has malice against a citizen who has committed a wrong will not render the action of the authority invalid if it is otherwise in accordance with law.”

“Law does not take into account all harms suffered by a person which caused no legal injury. Damage so done is called damnum sine injuria … The term ‘injuria’ is to be understood in its original and proper sense of wrong.”

The plaintiff had himself committed the wrong — he built without notice (s. 178) and sanction (s. 180), an offence under s. 185, and s. 186 empowered the Board to demolish. The demolition of an illegally constructed building does not amount to injuria to the owner. “No person has the right to enjoy the fruits of an act which is an offence under law.” Appeal allowed; the lower appellate court’s decree was set aside and the trial court’s restored.

Principle evolved: Damnum sine injuria — damage without a legal wrong is not actionable. Malice is irrelevant where the act itself is legal — “a legal act, though motivated by malice, will not make the actor liable.” Compensation requires proof of injury caused by an illegal act, not merely loss. This is also a damnum sine injuria illustration and a concrete application of ubi jus ibi remedium’s converse.

3. P. Seetharamayya v. G. Mahalakshmamma

AIR 1958 AP 103
Facts

The parties owned adjacent lands. The appellants owned S. Nos. 86 & 87/2 (shown in the plan as plots A and A-1); the respondents owned plots B and B-1, along the western boundary of which ran a vagu (a stream). The fifth defendant constructed a bund on her land to protect it from flood-water entering through a breach in the embankment of the vagu; defendants 1–4 dug a trench and constructed another bund as additional safeguards. The effect was that the rain/flood water flowed into the appellants’ plot A, washing away their variga and groundnut crops. The appellants twice built bunds (about 150 ft) but each was washed away. They sought a mandatory injunction to demolish the bunds and fill the trench, a permanent injunction against future bunds/trenches, and Rs. 300 damages.

Issues

(1) Were the defendants entitled to erect bunds/trenches to protect their land from flood-water? (2) Did the damage arise from normal rain-water (which the lower owner may not obstruct) or from flood-water (a “common enemy” against which each may defend himself)? (3) Was this damnum sine injuria?

Arguments & authorities

Appellants: The bunds/trench on the defendants’ lands diverted water into plot A causing crop loss; the defendants’ acts were wrongful, so mandatory injunction and damages should be granted.

Defendants: They were protecting their lands against flood-water — an apprehended danger — which is a lawful exercise of the right to self-protection.

Leading authority — Nield v. N.W. Rly. (1874): “The flood is a common enemy against which every man has a right to defend himself … The law allows … a kind of reasonable selfishness … he who puts up a barricade against a flood is entitled to say to his neighbour who complains of it ‘why did not you do the same.’” — Lord Bramwell B.

Beven on Negligence: The distinction is between water coming on land in the normal way (an incident of property from which a man may not relieve himself at his neighbour’s expense) and water coming abnormally (a common enemy, against which each may take precautions without regarding his neighbour — though he may protect his land, he may not, once the evil has befallen him, shift it to his neighbour’s land). Other authorities: Gerrard v. Crowe (PC — right to build fence against overflow), Gopal Reddi v. Chenna Reddi (no actionable wrong where no other practicable protection), M.&S.M. Rly. v. Maharaja of Pithapuram (a landholder must not, once flood-water is on his land, actively turn it onto a neighbour’s land), Sheik Hussain Sahib v. Subbayya (a lower owner cannot obstruct the natural surface-flow from a higher land), Kasia Pillai (agriculturist’s customary right to drain).

Decision

Mohammed Ahmed Ansari J held that the damage was caused by flood-water — the stream (vagu) being turbulent in flood season, the water entered through the breach, and the defendants were within their rights to protect their land. Once the right to protect against flood is ceded, the owner also enjoys the power of reasonably choosing how to protect his land. There was no evidence that the fifth defendant negligently chose the means or diverted accumulated water, nor that the vagu was obstructed.

“Therefore, this case is a clear one of damnum sine injuria, and the plaintiffs must adopt their own protective measures against the flood water.”

Both appeals dismissed with costs.

Principle evolved: A landowner has a right to protect his land against flood-water (the “common enemy”) even though, in doing so, the water is diverted to and damages the neighbouring land — such damage is damnum sine injuria and not actionable. Key distinction: flood-water (abnormal) may be warded off; normal surface drainage may not be obstructed to the neighbour’s prejudice; and water already on one’s land may not be actively cast onto a neighbour’s land.

4. Rajkot Municipal Corporation v. Manjulben Jayantilal Nakum & ors.

1997 (9) SCC 552
Facts

On 25 March 1975, the deceased Jayantilal, a clerk in the office of the Director of Industries, Rajkot, was walking on a footpath in the Kothi compound of the Collectorate on his way to work when a road-side tree suddenly fell on him. He sustained injuries to his head and body and died in hospital. The respondents — his widow and children — sued the appellant Municipal Corporation for Rs. 1 lakh. The trial court decreed Rs. 45,000, holding that the Corporation had failed in its statutory duty to check the health of the trees and to protect the deceased. The Division Bench confirmed, holding there was a statutory duty to plant and maintain trees, that the tree fell due to decay, that the Corporation failed to prove no negligence, and that it had not made periodical inspections. The Corporation appealed by special leave.

Issues

(1) Did the Corporation owe a duty of care to the deceased (or the class of pedestrians) in respect of the road-side trees? (2) Was there a breach of that duty, and was the breach the cause of the death? (3) Was the damage reasonably foreseeable and the causation sufficiently proximate, or was it too remote? (4) Does breach of a statutory duty ipso facto create tortious liability? (5) Is the standard of care such that the Corporation must inspect every tree?

Arguments

Appellant (Corporation — Mr. T.U. Mehta): There is no unqualified/absolute duty to maintain trees; the burden is on the respondents to prove breach of duty. It is difficult to inspect every tree; the standard of care is not as high as in breach of a statutory duty. The Corporation could not foresee that a tree would suddenly fall; there was no proximity or foreseeability as per the “neighbour” principle in Donoghue. Following Murphy v. Brentwood D.C. (overruling Anns v. Merton), a breach of statutory duty does not ipso facto entail liability; the duty must be owed to the plaintiff himself or a class, which was not pleaded/proved. Knowledge of likely harm is a pre-requisite. The duty to plant trees is owed to the public at large, not an individual; there is no sanction for omission; the legislature did not intend a private cause of action. The duty is discretionary.

Respondents: There is a statutory duty to plant and maintain trees; the tree fell due to decay, so the Corporation failed to prove the occurrence happened without its negligence; the statutory obligation being absolute, the respondents need not prove the exact negligence; the Corporation failed to make periodic inspections and seasonal treatment.

Decision

K. Ramaswamy J held that each case must be judged on its special circumstances — the existence of a duty of care, the class of persons owed, breach, and resultant damage. Public authorities discharge obligations to the public at large and owe a common-law duty of care to avoid present or imminent danger. However:

“But when the defendant was not in know of the discoverable defect or danger and it caused the damage by accident like sudden fall of the tree, it would be difficult to visualise that the defendant had knowledge of the danger and he omitted to perform the duty of care … There would be no special relationship between the statutory authority and the plaintiff who is a remote user of the foot-path … the defendant by his non-feasance is not responsible for the accident … since admittedly there was no visible sign that the tree was affected by disease.”

“… the Corporation or the authority is not liable to be sued for tort of negligence since the causation is too remote. Novus actus inconveniences snaps the link … It was difficult to foresee that a tree would fall on him.”

The tree fell suddenly, in still weather, with no visible sign of decay/disease; it was impossible to foresee; the conditions in India do not permit a Corporation to keep constant vigilance by testing every tree. Foreseeability alone does not create a duty; to impose an obligation of constant vigil would put an intolerable burden on the authority, detract from its normal duties, and cause a heavy financial burden. Appeal allowed; the trial court’s decree, as affirmed by the High Court, was set aside.

But see the court’s contrasting illustrations (where liability would attach): (i) a branch/tree gradually falling and the authority failing to act; (ii) a boy climbing a dangerously leaning tree and getting hurt — defect apparent, negligence obvious; (iii) a road being repaired with no warning/signal and a cyclist falling into the ditch at night. In all these the defect is apparent and the damage foreseeable, so the authority is liable.

Principle evolved: For negligence the plaintiff must prove (i) a duty of care (fixed by the neighbour principle), (ii) breach, and (iii) damage caused by the breach. Foreseeability and proximity are essential; foreseeability alone is not sufficient, and remote causation — where the harm is not reasonably foreseeable to a reasonable prudent person — negates liability. A public authority is not liable for a sudden, unforeseen fall of a healthy-looking tree where no defect was discoverable; but it is liable when the danger is apparent (a tree visibly falling, a defective structure, an unmarked excavation). The case also clarifies that breach of statutory duty does not automatically create tort liability — a duty owed to the public at large does not necessarily create a private right of action.

5. Ashby v. White

(1703) 2 Lord Raym 938
Facts

The plaintiff was a qualified voter in a parliamentary election. The defendants, the returning officers, wrongfully prevented him from exercising his right to vote. The candidate for whom the plaintiff wanted to vote nevertheless won the election, so no particular “cause” was lost. The plaintiff nevertheless brought an action claiming damages for the malicious prevention of his statutory right to vote.

Issues

Is the wrongful deprivation of a legal right to vote actionable even where the plaintiff has suffered no actual loss or damage? That is, does the action lie under injuria sine damnum / ubi jus ibi remedium?

Arguments

Defendants: The plaintiff suffered no loss; the candidate he favoured won anyway, so there was no damage.

Plaintiff: There was an infringement of a legal right vested in him — the right to vote; that alone is actionable.

Decision

Lord Holt CJ allowed the plaintiff damages, holding that the violation of a legal right is itself actionable, irrespective of proof of actual loss. The action is actionable per se.

“If the plaintiff has a right, he must of necessity have a means to vindicate and maintain it, and a remedy if he is injured in the exercise or enjoyment of it; and indeed it is a vain thing to imagine a right without a remedy; for want of right and want of remedy are reciprocal.”

“If man will multiply injuries, actions must be multiplied too: for every man who is injured ought to have recompense.”

(The principle was echoed in an English municipal-election case in 1957, Tozer v. Child.)

Principle evolved: Injuria sine damnum — the violation of a legal right is actionable even without actual damage; it is actionable per se, and the plaintiff is entitled at least to nominal damages, which may become exemplary if the conduct is malicious. It is the leading authority for ubi jus ibi remedium and for the Winfieldian “law of tort” theory.
Indian parallel — Bhim Singh v. State of J&K (AIR 1986 SC): An MLA was wrongfully detained while on his way to the assembly session, deprived of his constitutional right to attend, and Article 21 was violated. The Supreme Court held there was a violation of a legal right due to a mischievous and malicious act and awarded exemplary damages of ₹50,000 — a modern Indian application of injuria sine damnum.

6. Municipal Corpn. of Agra v. Asharfi Lal

AIR 1921 All 202 · (1922) ILR 44 All 202 · F.A. 47 of 1921
Facts

The plaintiff, Asharfi Lal, a duly qualified person entitled to be on the electoral roll / candidates’ list of the Agra Municipality, was wrongfully omitted or misdescribed on the candidates’ list. As the result of acts done officially in the course of business, or wrongly with intention in the course of business, or as a result of the revengeful and malicious interference of some individual (a member or employee of the Board), the plaintiff’s name on the list was so put and his description so fabricated as to represent him to be somebody other than the person he was known to be. Consequently, when he presented himself at nomination (between 31 January and 8 March), the Nomination Officers had to reject his nomination, because the person before them was not the son of the father recorded in the candidates’ list. The plaintiff sued for correction of the list (specific relief) and damages for the wrong done, later adding a claim that, because of the tampering, the whole constitution of the Board was invalid.

Issues

(1) Does the wrongful omission/misdescription of a duly qualified citizen on the electoral roll or candidates’ list amount to a legal wrong (injuria) entitling him to damages, even without proof of loss? (2) Against whom are damages recoverable — the Municipality as principal or the individual members? (3) Is the constitution of the Board invalid because of one wrongful entry? (4) What is the scope of discovery against a public body?

Arguments

Defendants (Municipal Board & members): The plaintiff abandoned the claim for wrongful omission; there were too many documents for discovery and some might be lost; individual members cannot be liable in their own pocket unless they took part in / authorised the wrongful act; liability, if any, depends on the principles of principal and agent.

Plaintiff: He did not know who was the author of his grievance, but it was impossible to resist the conclusion that his name was intentionally removed/replaced; he had the right to insist on production of the relevant documents by the public body.

Decision

Walsh J (Allahabad High Court) held that the law in India on the question is the same as the law in England. Under the common law, if a duly qualified citizen is wrongfully omitted from the electoral roll (depriving him of the right to vote) or misdescribed (so that his nomination is rejected), he has suffered a legal wrong — he has been deprived of a right recognised by law — and has a remedy, what has always been called “an action on the case”, for nominal damages for the right he has lost. The damages may be made punitive or exemplary if the conduct is the result of a malicious and wicked intention, and he also recovers any pecuniary expenses reasonably incurred.

“… he has suffered a legal wrong; he has been deprived of a right recognised by law and he has against the person so depriving him a remedy by what has always been called ‘an action on the case’ for nominal damages for the right that he has lost, which may at the discretion of the court be punitive or exemplary if the conduct is the result of some malicious and wicked intention …”

On liability: The liability must be decided according to the ordinary general principles of principal and agent. The Municipal Board, as principal, is liable for the acts of its servants/agents done in the course of their employment; an individual member is liable in his own pocket only if he expressly or indirectly encouraged, incited, directed, approved of, or aided and abetted the wrongful act. On the invalid-constitution point: “the ground on which the case was remanded, namely … whether the Board should be declared to be altogether invalid and improperly constituted because one of the names … is wrongly entered, is one which no court ought to have entertained at all,” and should be struck out. On discovery: The court directed discovery under Order XI, Rule 12 CPC, explaining that a notice to produce is not a subpoena and is not part of discovery machinery; discovery should be limited to relevant documents, and the affidavit on behalf of the Board should be made by the Chairman/Executive Officer.

Principle evolved: Injuria sine damnum applied in India to the right to vote and to stand for election: wrongful deprivation of an electoral right is a legal wrong actionable per se, even without proof of pecuniary loss; damages are nominal, becoming exemplary where the omission is malicious. The liability of a public body and its members is fixed on principal and agent principles; a public body is liable for its servants’ acts within the scope of employment, while individual members are liable only if they aided and abetted the wrong. Foreshadows modern vicarious liability of public authorities and the right of discovery as an aid to justice against public bodies.
Important: This is the Indian illustration of injuria sine damnum corresponding to Ashby v. White — and is distinguished from damnum sine injuria cases such as Town Area Committee, Gloucester and Pickles. The status of the right to vote/stand has since been reinforced by Bhim Singh v. State of J&K.

7. Mayor of Bradford Corporation v. Pickles

(1895) AC 587 (HL)
Facts

The Bradford Corporation derived its water supply partly from water springs and from water percolating through the soil in undefined channels under the land of Pickles. Pickles was annoyed at the Corporation’s refusal to purchase his land at the price he demanded in connection with its scheme of water supply. Actuated by feelings of revenge, Pickles sank a shaft on his own land and abstracted the water which percolated through the soil of his land in unknown and undefined channels, thereby diminishing and discolouring the water flowing to the Corporation’s land. The Corporation sued for an injunction to restrain Pickles from diminishing or polluting their water, contending that his conduct was unlawful because it was dictated by an evil motive.

Issues

Is an act that is otherwise lawful — the exercise of a legal right over one’s own land — made actionable merely because it was done with a malicious/bad motive? Does malice supply the element of injuria where there is none?

Arguments

Corporation (plaintiffs): Pickles’ conduct was unlawful because its sole purpose was to injure them, since they had not bought his land at an exorbitant price. They sought an injunction. It was already settled law that the interception of underground (percolating) water is not an actionable wrong, even if done intentionally (Chasemore v. Richards, 1859) — but the Corporation attempted to establish an exception where the motive was malicious.

Pickles (defendant): He was acting within his legal rights throughout — doing what he pleased with his own land; his motives were irrelevant.

Decision

House of Lords held that no action lies. An act lawful in itself is not converted into an unlawful act by a bad motive. Pickles was not liable.

“No use of property which would be legal if due to a proper motive can become illegal because it is prompted by a motive which is improper or even malicious.” — Lord Watson

“In such a case, motives are immaterial; it is the act, not the motive for the act, that must be regarded. If the act apart from the motive gives rise merely to damage without legal injury, the motive, however reprehensible it may be, will not supply that element.” — Lord Macnaghten

“Mr. Pickles has acted within his legal rights throughout; and is he to forfeit those rights and be punished for their legal exercise because certain motives are imputed to him? If his motives were the most generous and philanthropic in the world, they would not avail him when his actions were illegal. If his motives are selfish and mercenary, that is no reason why his rights should be confiscated when his actions are legal.” — Lord Ashbourne

Principle evolved: Motive/malice is irrelevant in tort. A lawful act done with a malicious motive is not tortious; where the act gives rise merely to damage without any legal injury, the motive however bad will not supply the missing injuria — this is a classic damnum sine injuria case and the leading authority on the irrelevance of motive. (Reinforced by Allen v. Flood (1896): an act lawful in itself is not made unlawful by a malicious motive.)

8. Gloucester Grammar School Case

(1410) Y.B. 11 Hen. IV 47
Facts

The plaintiff, a school master, kept a school. The defendant set up a rival (new) school in the same area, and as a result drew away the plaintiff’s pupils, causing the plaintiff to lose his fees/profit. The plaintiff sued for the loss.

Issues

Is the loss of profit caused by lawful competition (the exercise of one’s own right to carry on business) actionable, where no legal right of the plaintiff has been violated?

Arguments & the analogy

The harm (loss of pupils and fees) is real, but it is the ordinary consequence of fair competition, not of any wrongful act. The rule was stated (in the analogous terms used by Hankford J) that if a man has a mill and his neighbour builds another mill, the diminishing of the first man’s profit is not actionable unless some wrong such as a nuisance is caused. Mere diminution of gain/profit by lawful means is damnum absque injuria.

Decision

No action lay. The damage suffered was mere damnum absque injuria (damage without legal injury). The defendant had merely exercised his own lawful right to set up a business, and the resulting loss to the plaintiff was not actionable.

Principle evolved: Loss caused by lawful competition or the lawful exercise of one’s own rights, without violation of the plaintiff’s legal rights, is damnum sine injuria and is not actionable. This case is the foundation of the rule that trade competition, however damaging, is not a tort absent unlawful means — subsequently applied in Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor Gow & Co. (1892), where a combination to monopolise the China tea trade by reducing freights was held no cause of action as it involved no unlawful means, being “a justifiable measure of self-protection.”

Previous Year Questions & Model Answers

Includes the actual DU (LB-103) December 2024 paper questions relevant to this topic, plus the classic Topic-1 questions DU regularly asks.

DU · December 2024 · Q1 · 20 marksABC Textiles purchases a machine from TexaMach Ltd. A defect in the machine, caused by faulty assembly at TexaMach Ltd.’s factory, leads to an accident in which a worker loses a finger. ABC Textiles claims compensation from TexaMach Ltd. for the injury caused by their negligence. However, TexaMach Ltd. invokes the exclusion clause in the contract to deny liability. The clause reads: “The supplier shall not be held liable for any personal loss, damage, or injury arising from the use of the machinery supplied, regardless of fault.” Does the exclusion clause in the contract restrict/exclude the liability of TexaMach Ltd. in torts for their negligence? Discuss with the help of decided cases.
Approach (structure)

1. State the rule (contra proferentem / strict construction of exemption clauses): A clause exempting a party from the consequences of its own negligence is construed very strictly. A party may escape liability for negligence only if the clause is freely and deliberately entered into and is expressed in words clear beyond the possibility of misunderstanding.

2. The two-head rule: Where a defendant is liable under two heads — one a negligence (tort) liability and the other a stricter (contractual) liability — the clause will, so far as possible, be construed as exempting only the strict liability and not the liability for negligence. 3. Co-existence of contract and tort on the same facts. 4. Application & conclusion.

Model Answer

The liability of TexaMach Ltd. for the worker’s injury may arise on two heads: (i) a contractual liability (breach of the implied warranty that the machine is reasonably fit for the purpose, or breach of a warranty of quality), and (ii) a liability in tort for negligence (breach of a duty of care to take reasonable care in assembling/supplying a safe machine). The exclusion clause relied upon purports to exclude liability “regardless of fault.”

The governing authority is White v. John Warrick & Co. Ltd. (1953) 2 All ER 1021, where the plaintiff hirer of a defective tricycle was injured and the owners relied on a clause that said “nothing in this agreement shall render the owners liable for any personal injuries to the riders.” The Court of Appeal (Singleton & Denning LJJ) laid down two principles:

“If a person desires to exempt himself from a liability which the common law imposes on him, he can only do so by a contract freely and deliberately entered into … in words that are clear beyond the possibility of misunderstanding. The second is that, if there are two possible heads of liability on the defendant, one for negligence, and the other a strict liability, an exemption clause will be construed, so far as possible, as exempting the defendant only from his strict liability and not as relieving him from his liability for negligence.” — Denning LJ

The court held the clause exempted the owners only from their contractual (stricter) liability, not from their liability for negligence, because the same facts can give rise to both a breach of contract and a tort, and the plaintiff may frame his claim in tort even where a contract exists (quoting Lord Macmillan in Donoghue v. Stevenson — “negligence apart from contract gives a right of action to the party injured by that negligence”; the presence of a contractual relationship does not exclude the co-existence of a right of action founded on negligence).

Applying this to the facts, the clause in the TexaMach contract is not expressed in language clear enough to exclude liability for the suppliers’ own negligence, even though it uses the words “regardless of fault.” Following White v. John Warrick, the clause will be construed as exempting the supplier only from its contractual liability and not from its torious liability for negligence. Since the defect arose from the supplier’s own faulty assembly — a failure to take reasonable care — TexaMach Ltd. cannot exclude its tortious liability for negligence by this clause. (See also Grant v. Australian Knitting Mills, Donoghue v. Stevenson for the duty of care of the manufacturer; and the statutory limit in England via the Unfair Contract Terms Act, 1977.)

Conclusion

The exclusion clause restricts only the contractual liability of TexaMach Ltd.; it does not exclude the action in tort for negligence. The claim for negligence is founded on tort and will not be barred.

Classic DU Topic-1 long question“Is the law of tort a law of tort or a law of torts?” Discuss the rival theories of Winfield and Salmond, and state which theory prevails in India. 20
Model Answer

Introduction. The question posed by Prof. Salmond is whether tortious liability rests on a general principle (that it is wrongful to harm another absent justification) or consists of a closed set of specific, nominate torts. Two theories answer it.

1. The wider theory — Law of Tort (Winfield, supported by Pollock). “All injuries done by one person to another are torts, unless there is some justification recognised by law.” Every unjustifiable harm is actionable; the plaintiff need not give the wrong a name. Courts can create new torts: negligence as a distinct tort, Rylands v. Fletcher (strict liability, 1868), deceit, inducement of breach of contract, intimidation (Rookes v. Barnard), and the neighbour principle in Donoghue v. Stevenson. Lord Macmillan: “The categories of negligence are never closed.” Holt CJ in Ashby v. White: “If man will multiply injuries, actions must be multiplied too.”

2. The narrower (pigeon-hole) theory — Law of Torts (Salmond). There is no law of tort, only a law of torts — a set of pigeon-holes, each containing a labelled tort. Liability arises only if the wrong fits one of the recognised torts; otherwise there is no tort. Salmond: “Just as the criminal law consists of rules establishing specific offences, so the law of torts consists of rules establishing specific injuries.” It is founded on the historical writs/forms of action, under which no writ meant no remedy. Dr. Jenks supports Salmond but adds courts can create new torts substantially similar to existing ones.

3. Reconciliation. A middle ground: there are nominate torts, but a claimant who has suffered a genuine legal wrong need not find a label. Winfield himself conceded, “From a narrow and practical point of view, Salmond’s theory will suffice, but from a broader outlook, his theory is valid.” In India, Lala Pannalal v. Kasturi Chand (AIR 1946 Mad 147) holds there is no exhaustive classification of torts; new invasions of rights may create new torts (e.g., malicious house-search).

4. Position in India. Indian courts have favoured Winfield’s broader theory, evolving new principles and norms for a changing society — absolute liability in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, constitutional torts and compensation for violation of Article 21 (Rudul Sah, Bhim Singh), and the expansion of State liability in a welfare State (State of Rajasthan v. Vidhyawati).

Conclusion. It is a question of approach. For practical purposes the pigeon-hole idea is useful, but jurisprudentially the wider “law of tort” view better describes a body of law that is steadily growing, and it is the view Indian courts have adopted.

Classic DU Topic-1 long questionDefine a tort. Explain the constituents of a tort, and distinguish between injuria sine damnum and damnum sine injuria with the help of case laws. 20
Model Answer

Definition. (Give Salmond and Winfield’s definitions — see §4.) A tort is a civil wrong, consisting of a breach of a duty fixed by law, owed to persons generally, redressible by an action for unliquidated damages.

Constituents. (i) Wrongful act/omission; (ii) Legal damage — violation of a legal right (injuria); (iii) Remedy (unliquidated damages or injunction) — governed by ubi jus ibi remedium.

Injuria sine damnum — violation of a legal right without actual loss (actionable per se). Authorities: Ashby v. White (wrongful refusal of vote — damages though no loss); Municipal Corpn. of Agra v. Asharfi Lal (wrongful omission/misdescription of the plaintiff’s name on the municipal candidates’ list, causing rejection of his nomination — held a legal wrong actionable for nominal damages, punitive if malicious); Bhim Singh v. State of J&K (wrongful detention of an MLA violating Article 21 — ₹50,000 exemplary damages); trespass to land.

Damnum sine injuria — actual loss without violation of a legal right (not actionable). Authorities: Gloucester Grammar School (loss of pupils by rival school — no action); Mayor of Bradford v. Pickles (interception of percolating water by an act lawful in itself, however malicious the motive); P. Seetharamayya v. G. Mahalakshmamma (diversion of flood-water onto neighbour’s land by lawful self-protection — damnum sine injuria); Town Area Committee v. Prabhu Dayal (demolition of an illegally-constructed building — no injuria); Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor Gow (trade competition by lawful means); Acton v. Blundell, Chasemore v. Richards (percolating water).

Conclusion. The distinction is whether a legal right (not merely a factual interest) has been violated. Where it has, the action lies even without damage; where it has not, no action lies however real the loss.

Classic DU Topic-1 long questionThe law of torts in India is uncodified and judge-made. Discuss its advantages and disadvantages. 20
Model Answer

Introduction. Tort law in India is essentially the English common law, received and administered as rules of justice, equity and good conscience. It remains largely uncodified, being developed by the courts through precedent; only fragmented areas (workmen’s compensation, motor accidents, consumer protection, environment, EPI Act) are codified.

Judge-made & uncodified — supported by Rajkot Municipal Corporation v. Manjulben (1997) where the Court noted that since there is no statutory law regulating tortious damages in India, it “selectively applied the common law principles evolved by the courts in England on grounds of justice, equity and good conscience.”

Advantages: flexibility & adaptability; capacity to create new torts (absolute liability, constitutional torts, Art. 21 compensation); justice in individual cases; principles moulded to Indian conditions (rejection of feudal sovereign immunity in a welfare State); responsiveness without waiting for Parliament.

Disadvantages: uncertainty and unpredictability; dependence on litigants (reactive, not proactive); cost and delay exclude the poor; reliance on English rules that may not fit Indian realities; slow, uneven and haphazard growth; judicial activism is not a substitute for legislative schemes (e.g., compensation for hit-and-run, mass disasters).

Conclusion. The flexibility of an uncodified, judge-made law is its greatest strength and simultaneously its greatest weakness. The Law Commission has repeatedly recommended codification, but until then the courts must balance development of law against certainty.

Classic DU short/long questionDistinguish between tort and crime, and between tort and breach of contract. Can the same act give rise to liability in tort as well as in contract and crime? 20
Model Answer

Tort vs crime: tort is a private wrong (civil), crime a public wrong. Tort is enforced by the injured party (plaintiff) for compensatory/unliquidated damages; crime is prosecuted by the State for punishment. A tort concerns a right in rem (duty to persons generally) and may be settled; a crime generally is not compoundable. (See the full table in §9.1.)

Tort vs breach of contract: in tort the duty is fixed by law and owed to persons generally (right in rem, no privity); in contract the duty is fixed by the parties’ agreement and owed only to the contracting party (privity, right in personam). Tort damages are unliquidated; contract damages are liquidated.

Co-existence: The same facts can give rise to both a tort and a breach of contract. White v. John Warrick holds that an exemption clause barring contractual liability does not bar a claim in tort for negligence; the claim in negligence is founded on tort. Lord Macmillan in Donoghue: a contractual relationship does not exclude the co-existence of a right of action founded on negligence. A railway passenger may sue for breach of contract of safe carriage or negligence; a dentist (Edwards v. Mallan) or a surgeon (Pippin v. Sheppard) may be liable concurrently in contract to one person and in tort to another.

Co-existence of tort and crime: Assault, defamation, nuisance and conspiracy are both tort and crime; the civil and criminal remedies are concurrent, not alternative — the victim may sue for damages and the State may prosecute.

Conclusion. Though tort, contract and crime are distinct branches, a single wrong may generate liability in more than one; the plaintiff may not recover twice over but may choose the appropriate remedy.

Classic DU short question (10 marks)Discuss the relevance of intention, motive and malice in the law of torts. 10–20
Model Answer

Intention is relevant in intentional torts (battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass, deceit, malicious prosecution, conspiracy) and to the quantum of damages; but it is not an essential element of tortious liability (strict/absolute and vicarious liability arise without intention).

Motive is generally irrelevant. The law asks what a person did, not why. A good motive does not justify an illegal act; a bad motive does not make a lawful act wrongful. Mayor of Bradford v. Pickles: “No use of property which would be legal if due to a proper motive can become illegal because it is prompted by a motive which is improper or even malicious” (Lord Watson); “motives are immaterial; it is the act, not the motive for the act, that must be regarded” (Lord Macnaghten). Town Area Committee v. Prabhu Dayal: “A legal act, though motivated by malice, will not make the actor liable.” Allen v. Flood: an act lawful in itself is not converted by a malicious motive into an actionable wrong.

Malice has two meanings. Malice in law = a wrongful act done intentionally without just cause or excuse, presumed from an unlawful act (Bayley J, Bromage v. Prosser). Malice in fact = an evil motive or ill-will, relevant where good faith is a defence — e.g., malicious prosecution, and in defamation where it defeats qualified privilege (Rustom K. Karanjia) — and material to exemplary damages (Ashby, Bhim Singh).

Conclusion. Motive is ordinarily irrelevant; intention and malice matter only in defined situations — for intentional torts, to negative good-faith defences, and for the measure of damages.

Classic DU short questionWrite a short note on ubi jus ibi remedium and its application. 10
Model Answer

The maxim ubi jus ibi remedium means “where there is a right, there is a remedy.” Every violation of a legal right must be redressed; where law confers a right it also provides a means to vindicate it. Lord Holt CJ in Ashby v. White: “If the plaintiff has a right, he must of necessity have a means to vindicate and maintain it, and a remedy if he is injured in the exercise or enjoyment of it; and indeed it is a vain thing to imagine a right without a remedy; for want of right and want of remedy are reciprocal.”

It is the basis of injuria sine damnum and of the remedial character of tort law. Its limit: it operates only where a legal right exists. Where there is no legal injury — only factual damage (damnum sine injuria) — there is no remedy (Gloucester Grammar School, Pickles, Seetharamayya, Town Area Committee).

Key Maxims & Terms — Quick Glossary

  • Tort — French for “wrong”; a civil wrong independent of contract, remedy = action for unliquidated damages.
  • Tort-feasor — the wrongdoer.
  • Injuria — violation/infringement of a legal right.
  • Damnum — actual loss/damage (money, comfort, health).
  • Injuria sine damnum — infringement of a legal right without damage; actionable per se (Ashby, Asharfi Lal, Bhim Singh).
  • Damnum sine injuria — actual damage without infringement of a legal right; not actionable (Gloucester, Pickles, Seetharamayya, Town Area, Mogul).
  • Ubi jus ibi remedium — where there is a right, there is a remedy (Holt CJ, Ashby).
  • Volenti non fit injuria — consent negatives liability (a voluntary assumption of risk); subject to rescue cases & UCTA 1977.
  • Actio personalis moritur cum persona — a personal action dies with the person (crime; tort has exceptions).
  • Res ipsa loquitur — “the thing speaks for itself” (evidentiary rule in negligence).
  • Novus actus interveniens — a new intervening act breaking the chain of causation (see Rajkot/Murphy discussion).
  • Malfeasance / misfeasance / non-feasance — wrongful act / negligent performance / omission to act.
  • Right in rem — right against the world at large (tort). Right in personam — right against a specific person (contract).
  • Malice in law — wrongful act done intentionally without just cause or excuse. Malice in fact — evil motive/ill-will.
  • Exemplary/punitive damages — damages to punish, awarded for malicious/wicked conduct (Ashby, Bhim Singh).
