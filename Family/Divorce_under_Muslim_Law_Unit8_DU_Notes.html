PART 1

Foundations — How Muslim Law Handles Divorce

The starting point. Under Muslim law, marriage (nikah) is a civil contract, not a sacrament. Because it is contractual in nature, it can be dissolved — but Islam treats divorce as an evil of last resort. The Prophet is reported to have said that divorce is "the most disliked of lawful things in the sight of God", and that God created nothing He detests more than divorce. Every mode of dissolution therefore carries built-in brakes: reasons, waiting periods (iddat), and mandatory attempts at reconciliation by arbiters (hakam) drawn from both families.

1.1 The statutory anchor — Section 2, Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937

Both Masroor Ahmed and Mulla open the topic with s. 2 of the Shariat Act, 1937. It is an injunction upon the courts: where the parties are Muslims, questions of marriage and its dissolution must be decided by the Shariat, notwithstanding any custom or usage to the contrary.

Statute — Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937, s. 2

"Notwithstanding any customs or usage to the contrary, in all questions (save questions relating to agricultural land) regarding intestate succession, special property of females, including personal property inherited or obtained under contract or gift or any other provision of Personal Law, marriage, dissolution of marriage, including talaq, ila, zihar, lian, khula and mubaraat, maintenance, dower, guardianship, gifts, trusts and trust properties, and wakfs (other than charities and charitable institutions and charitable and religious endowments) the rule of decision in cases where the parties are Muslims shall be the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat)."

Exam point (Masroor Ahmed): The phrase "dissolution of marriage, including talaq, ila, zihar, lian, khula and mubaraat" gives statutory recognition that talaq is only ONE of several modes of dissolution. Islam considers divorce odious yet permits it on grounds of pragmatism — an elaborate lattice of modes exists "with differing amplitude and width under the different schools" (Badar Durrez Ahmed J.).

1.3 The two conceptions of Muslim law (from class notes)

① Shariat — the revealed law

Wider, broader orbit covering all human actions with various grades of approval; God and the Prophet "laid down the path of shariat".

② Fiqh — science of jurisprudence

Narrower — legal acts (legal / illegal); the whole structure erected by human agency through ijtihad of the learned (ulema).

PART 2

Extra-judicial Divorce — I : TALAQ (Divorce by the Husband)

Mulla, para 307–308: A Muslim marriage may be dissolved (1) by the husband at his will without court intervention; (2) by mutual consent without court intervention; (3) by judicial decree. "Any Mahomedan of sound mind who has attained puberty may divorce his wife whenever he desires without assigning any cause" — the classical Hanafi statement. Zohara Khatoon v. Mohd. Ibrahim (1981) 2 SCC 509 (quoted in Masroor Ahmed): "A divorce given unilaterally by the husband is especially peculiar to Mahomedan law. In no other law has the husband got a unilateral right to divorce his wife by a simple declaration..."

But the modern judicial view (Shamim Ara, Yousuf Rawther, Masroor Ahmed) reads the Quran to discipline this power: talaq must be for a reasonable cause, preceded by an attempt at reconciliation by two arbiters, actually pronounced, and communicated.

2.1 Mechanics of talaq — the complete checklist (Mulla paras 308–315)

Who may pronounce?

Any Muslim of sound mind who has attained puberty (presumed at 15). He need not give any reason (classical Hanafi view).

Form — oral or written

Talaq may be (1) oral, or (2) in writing — a talaqnama. No particular form of words is prescribed (Hanafi). Express words (saheeh) need no proof of intention; ambiguous words (kinayat) need proof of intention.

Presence & naming

Need not be pronounced in the wife's presence nor even addressed to her — but the wife must be named (Calcutta: bare "talaq" before a family council without naming her = invalid). Shia law: must be pronounced orally, in Arabic, before two aadil (competent, male, Muslim) witnesses.

Compulsion, jest, intoxication

Hanafi: talaq in express words is valid even if pronounced under compulsion, in jest, or voluntary intoxication — "a talak actually pronounced under compulsion or in jest is valid and effective" (Privy Council, Rashid Ahmad v. Anisa Khatun). Shia law: invalid in all such states. Masroor Ahmed adds the modern qualification: talaq in extreme anger (loss of self-control) is not effective.

Contingent / conditional talaq

May take effect on a future event (e.g., "if you go to your father's house..."). Not recognised by Shia law. A divorce on contingency can also operate as delegation to the wife.

Talaqnama (written)

In absence of contrary words, writing operates as an irrevocable talak-i-bain, effective from execution even if not communicated (Bombay, Sarabai's case). Communication is nonetheless necessary for dower/maintenance purposes and her right to pledge the husband's credit.

Consequences of a revocable talaq during iddat (Masroor Ahmed, para 35)

  • Marriage does not terminate at pronouncement; it stays suspended — wife retains right of residence and maintenance; she cannot be disturbed from where she was residing.
  • She remains a wife throughout the iddat — status analogous to divorce a mensa et thoro (judicial separation) in English ecclesiastical law; dissolution only on completion of iddat without revocation (a vinculo matrimonii).
  • The iddat is the period meant for review and reconciliation — hence Masroor Ahmed J.: where talaq is revocable, the reconciliation attempt may even take place after pronouncement, so long as it precedes the end of iddat.
  • Her deferred dower, custody rights, and right to pledge the husband's credit for subsistence all turn on knowing about the talaq — the policy reason communication is essential.
PART 3

Extra-judicial Divorce — II : Khula, Mubarat, Ila, Zihar, Lian, Tafweez

3.1 KHULA — divorce at the instance of the wife (Mulla, paras 319–320)

Definition (Mulla): "A divorce by khula is a divorce with the consent, and at the instance of the wife, in which she gives or agrees to give a consideration to the husband for her release from the marriage tie." The terms are a matter of bargain — she may release her mahr (dower) and other rights, or make any other agreement for the husband's benefit.

Etymology (Baillie): "Khula means to put off, as a man is said to khula his garment when he puts it off. In law it is the laying down by a husband of his right and authority over his wife for an exchange."

Prophetic root (Bukhari, quoted in Yousuf Rawther & Masroor Ahmed): the wife of Thabit ibn Qais came to the Prophet: not angry with Thabit's temper or religion, but afraid she could not observe the bounds of God. The Prophet asked: "Will you give back to Thabit the garden he gave you?" — "Yes." — "Take your garden and divorce her at once." Asma, one of the Prophet's wives, was likewise released at her own request. Lesson: the wife can seek release even without fault against the husband.

Masroor Ahmed's gloss: Khula is a divorce which proceeds from the wife "which the husband cannot refuse, subject only to reasonable negotiation" as to what she offers in return. No reason need be specified — it operates on a no-fault/no-blame basis.

3.2 ILA and ZIHAR — constructive forms (virtually obsolete in India)

Ila — vow of continence (Mulla 317)

A species of constructive divorce: the husband vows abstinence from sexual intercourse for not less than four months; on expiry (Hanafi view) it operates as divorce. Shafii law: the vow gives the wife a right to demand judicial divorce. Cases are "virtually non-existent in India" (Masroor Ahmed), but ila is named in s. 2 of the Shariat Act.

Zihar (Mulla 318)

An inchoate form: the husband compares his wife to his mother or any other female within prohibited degrees. The wife may refuse herself to him until he performs expiation; in default she may sue for a judicial divorce. "Cases of zihar are unknown in India", doubted whether enforceable — but it received statutory recognition in s. 2, Shariat Act, 1937.

3.3 LIAN — divorce for false charge of adultery (Mulla 333; Masroor Ahmed para 17)

Fig. 6 — Procedure of Lian

Lian under the DMMA umbrella: s. 2(ix) DMMA preserves lian as a ground "recognised as valid under Muslim law"; a wife suing for dissolution on lian succeeds only if the charge is proved false, and no suit lies if the marriage was merely irregular.

3.4 TALAQ-E-TAFWEEZ — delegated divorce (Mulla 314)

The power to divorce belongs primarily to the husband, but he may delegate it to the wife (or a third person) — absolutely or conditionally, for a period or permanently. Stipulations in the nikahnama giving the wife a right to divorce herself in specified contingencies (e.g., husband takes a second wife, ill-treats her, doesn't pay dower) are valid if reasonable and not opposed to Muslim law policy. Once triggered, it operates "to the same extent as if a talak has been pronounced by the husband". Temporary delegation is irrevocable; permanent delegation may be revoked; the wife's stipulated power is not revocable and survives even a suit for restitution.

From Itwari v. Asghari: a Muslim wife can stipulate for the power to divorce in case the husband takes another wife — and where such power exists "she is not bound to exercise her option at the very first moment" — "a second marriage is not a single but a continuing wrong to the first wife" (Ayatunnessa Beebee v. Karam Ali, ILR 36 Cal 23). A contract restraining the husband from a second marriage is not void under s. 23, Contract Act.

Why tafweez matters: It is called "the most powerful weapon in the hands of a Muslim wife" — it gives her an out-of-court divorce equal to talaq, restoring parity with the husband's unilateral power (echoed by Krishna Iyer J. in Yousuf Rawther: equalisation of divorce rights).
PART 4

Judicial Divorce — The Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939

Background & Statement of Objects and Reasons (as set out in the course pack)

Before 1939, a Hanafi wife had practically no judicial remedy: the Hanafi school permitted judicial divorce only on limited grounds (impotence, lian), and courts hesitated to apply the more liberal Maliki law. The SOR recorded that "the absence of such a provision has entailed unspeakable misery to innumerable Muslim women in British India", and that "the Hanafi Jurists have clearly laid down that in cases in which the application of Hanafi Law causes hardship, it is permissible to apply the provisions of the Maliki, Shafii or Hambali Law."

Masroor Ahmed: the 1939 Act introduced "a very salutary principle" — applying beneficial provisions of one school to adherents of other schools; and it enabled Muslim women of all sects to seek dissolution through court.

  • Long title: "An Act to consolidate and clarify the provisions of Muslim law relating to suits for dissolution of marriage by women married under Muslim law and to remove doubts as to the effect of the renunciation of Islam by a married Muslim woman on her marriage tie."
  • Act No. 8 of 1939 — passed 17 March 1939; extends to the whole of India (J&K exception omitted w.e.f. 31-10-2019).
  • Nature: consolidating; not wholly declaratory (Mulla) — s. 4 in particular altered the law on apostasy.
Bare Act — full text (Act 8 of 1939, as amended)

S. 1. Short title and extent. (1) This Act may be called the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939. (2) It extends to the whole of India.

S. 2. Grounds for decree for dissolution of marriage. — A woman married under Muslim law shall be entitled to obtain a decree for the dissolution of her marriage on any one or more of the following grounds, namely:

(i) that the whereabouts of the husband have not been known for a period of four years;
(ii) that the husband has neglected or has failed to provide for her maintenance for a period of two years;
(iii) that the husband has been sentenced to imprisonment for a period of seven years or upwards;
(iv) that the husband has failed to perform, without reasonable cause, his marital obligations for a period of three years;
(v) that the husband was impotent at the time of the marriage and continues to be so;
(vi) that the husband has been insane for a period of two years or is suffering from a virulent venereal disease (the words "leprosy or" omitted by Act 6 of 2019);
(vii) that she, having been given in marriage by her father or other guardian before she attained the age of fifteen years, repudiated the marriage before attaining the age of eighteen years: Provided that the marriage has not been consummated;
(viii) that the husband treats her with cruelty, that is to say —
(a) habitually assaults her or makes her life miserable by cruelty of conduct even if such conduct does not amount to physical ill-treatment, or
(b) associates with women of evil repute or leads an infamous life, or
(c) attempts to force her to lead an immoral life, or
(d) disposes of her property or prevents her exercising her legal rights over it, or
(e) obstructs her in the observance of her religious profession or practice, or
(f) if he has more wives than one, does not treat her equitably in accordance with the injunctions of the Quran;
(ix) on any other ground which is recognised as valid for the dissolution of marriages under Muslim law.
Provided that — (a) no decree shall be passed on ground (iii) until the sentence has become final; (b) a decree passed on ground (i) shall not take effect for six months from its date, and if the husband appears in person or through an authorised agent within that period and satisfies the Court that he is prepared to perform his conjugal duties, the Court shall set aside the decree; and (c) before passing a decree on ground (v) the Court shall, on application by the husband, order him to satisfy the Court within one year that he has ceased to be impotent — if he does, no decree shall be passed on that ground.

S. 3. Notice to be served on heirs of the husband when the husband's whereabouts are not known. — In a suit under cl. (i): (a) names and addresses of persons who would be the husband's heirs had he died on the date of filing the plaint shall be stated; (b) notice shall be served on them; (c) they have the right to be heard. Provided that the husband's paternal uncle and brother, if any, shall be cited as parties even if not heirs.

S. 4. Effect of conversion to another faith. — Renunciation of Islam by a married Muslim woman, or her conversion to a faith other than Islam, shall not by itself dissolve her marriage; but thereafter she may sue for dissolution on any s. 2 ground. Provided further that this section does not apply to a woman converted to Islam from some other faith who re-embraces her former faith (such re-embracement does dissolve the marriage).

S. 5. Rights to dower not to be affected. — Nothing in the Act affects any right which a married woman has under Muslim law to her dower (or part thereof) on dissolution.

S. 6. [Repeal of s. 5 of the Shariat Act, 1937.] — Rep. by the Repealing and Amending Act, 1942.

① Saiyid Rashid Ahmad v. Mt. Anisa Khatun Bridge case — Unit VII/VIII junction
AIR 1932 PC 25 · Privy Council · Lord Thankerton · (course pack pp. 233–236)
❖ Facts

Suit concerning succession to the estate of Ghiyas Uddin, a Muslim who died on 4 April 1920 leaving considerable property. His brother and sister (appellants) claimed that respondents 1–6 — Anisa Khatun and her children — were not his lawful widow and legitimate offspring. The matrimonial history had four stages: (1) Anisa Fatima's marriage to Manzur Husain (1901); (2) her divorce by Manzur early 1905; (3) her marriage to Ghiyas Uddin on 28 Aug 1905; (4) her divorce by Ghiyas Uddin on/about 13 September 1905 — a triple talaq pronounced in the presence of witnesses but in the wife's absence, with Rs 1,000 paid as dower the same day (registered receipt) and a talaqnama dated 17 September 1905 which narrated that the three divorces were given "in the abominate form", i.e., bidaat. The Subordinate Judge held the divorce effective and the children (all born after the divorce) illegitimate; the Allahabad High Court reversed, treating the divorce as "fictitious... a mock ceremony performed... to satisfy his father, without any intention that it should be real".

⚖ Issues
  1. Was the triple talaq of 13.9.1905 legally valid and effective?
  2. Could the husband's secret mental intention (that the ceremony be a sham) nullify a formally pronounced talaq?
  3. If divorced irrevocably, could the subsequent offspring be legitimised by the husband's acknowledgments and conduct?
❖ Arguments of the parties

Appellants (heirs of Ghiyas Uddin's collaterals): concurrent findings below establish the triple talaq and the genuine talaqnama; the divorce was in the bidaat form and therefore immediately irrevocable; intention is irrelevant; conduct for 15 years thereafter cannot undo a completed divorce; the legal bar to remarriage prevents any presumption of legitimacy.

Respondents (Anisa Khatun's children): the divorce was a mock ceremony — no genuine intention, hence inoperative; reliance on the husband's 15-year treatment of Anisa Fatima as wife and of the children as legitimate, and on the acknowledgment dicta in Habibur Rahman Chowdhury v. Altaf Ali Chowdhury (AIR 1922 PC 159), to raise a presumption of intervening marriage and legitimisation; the burden lay on the appellants to displace that presumption.

✓ Decision

Appeal allowed; High Court reversed; Subordinate Judge's decree restored. Lord Thankerton adopted Wilson's Digest of Anglo-Mahomedan Law (5th ed. 136): talaq may be bain (irrevocable) or raja (revocable); bain may be effected (a) once + abstinence through iddat — ahsan; (b) three times in successive periods of purity — hasan; (c) three times at shorter intervals or even in immediate succession; (d) once by words showing immediate irrevocability — (c) and (d) are bidaat, "sinful, but... regarded by Sunni lawyers as legally valid". Here the words — "I divorce Anisa Khatun for ever and render her haram for me" — repeated thrice, plus the talaqnama, showed the bidaat form: the divorce "at once becomes irrevocable, irrespective of the iddat". The wife's presence was unnecessary. "A talak actually pronounced under compulsion or in jest is valid and effective" — the husband's mental reservation could not undo it; that view of the High Court was "contrary to all authority". The acknowledgment presumption fails on the face of the legal bar created by the triple divorce; children born after the divorce were illegitimate and could not inherit.

Principle evolved Form prevails over intent: a triple talaq in the bidaat form is, in classical Hanafi law as applied by the PC, immediately operative and irrevocable irrespective of iddat, communication or the husband's true intention; compulsion or jest is irrelevant. This is the classical position — the very position progressively demolished by Shamim Ara (2002) and finally by Shayara Bano (2017), which is exactly the judicial arc an examiner loves to see narrated.
② Ghulam Sakina v. Falak Sher Allah Bakhsh
AIR 1950 Lah 45 · Lahore High Court · Mohd. Sharif J. · Second appeal · (course pack pp. 255–258)
❖ Facts

The plaintiff (a Sunni girl) sued for dissolution of her marriage. Her case: she was never validly given in marriage as the defendant proclaimed; if any marriage took place during her infancy, she never approved it and had repudiated it; and (a plea not pressed at trial) she a Sunni and the defendant a Shia. The defendant answered that the marriage was performed by her father when she was five years old, as an exchange (watta-satta) marriage — his sister marrying the plaintiff's uncle in return — that the parties lived as husband and wife and the marriage was consummated. The marriage register (Ex. D-2) showed the marriage was performed by the father as guardian on 23 November 1932; her birth entry showed she was born on 13 November 1931 (about a year old at marriage); the nikah khawan deposed she was then 3–4 months old and the defendant 5–7 years old; the doctor estimated her at about 17 at examination. The suit was instituted on 29 August 1945 — before she attained 18 on either reckoning. The trial court found no credible proof of consummation and decreed dissolution; the District Judge, Mianwali reversed on the question of consummation and dismissed the suit; the plaintiff came in second appeal.

⚖ Issue

The sole question: whether the plaintiff had repudiated her marriage in accordance with s. 2(vii), DMMA 1939 — i.e., marriage by father/guardian before 15, repudiation before 18, and the statutory proviso that "the marriage has not been consummated" — the entire battle being whether the marriage stood "consummated".

❖ Arguments of the parties

Defendant (husband): consummation was proved — two respectable witnesses produced by the plaintiff herself (his brother P.W. 2 and his father P.W. 7) admitted it; and the plaintiff's pointed refusal to submit to a medical examination (to test virginity) raised "a very strong presumption" of consummation — exactly the reasoning accepted by the courts below and reflected in Atkia Begum v. Muhammad Ibrahim (36 IC 20), where refusal was treated as showing the respondent's bona fides. A rapprochement about December 1944, a second marriage party, and her going to his house were alleged.

Plaintiff (wife): the evidence of consummation was of "very unsatisfactory character" — the defendant had even scored out the words "about one year ago" from his statement and evaded the court's direction to specify when consummation allegedly occurred; the correspondence (letters between the fathers) showed dissension since 1944 and her continued residence in her father's house; the two witnesses were examined only with reference to letters and were the husband's own father and brother; her refusal of a lady doctor's examination could not prove consummation — the Privy Council in Atkia Begum itself disapproved drawing such an inference. Any cohabitation during minority cannot extinguish an option that accrues only on puberty.

✓ Decision

Appeal accepted; District Judge's decree set aside; trial court's decree restored; plaintiff's suit for dissolution decreed with costs throughout. Steps in the reasoning: (1) consummation "should have been proved as a fact on the consideration of the entire evidence"; refusal to be examined by a lady doctor "could not... be taken to be a proof of the consummation" — the PC in Atkia Begum did not agree with the contrary remarks. (2) The true significance of the option of puberty: marriage being a contract needs "free and unfettered consent"; guardians contract for minors; before 1939 a minor girl given in marriage by the father or father's father could not repudiate — the Act changed this, putting such a contract "on no higher footing than that of any other guardian", so the minor may repudiate or ratify after puberty. (3) Puberty is presumed on completion of 15 years unless earlier puberty is proved (burden on the person pleading it). (4) "Anything done by the minor during the minority would not destroy the right which could accrue only after puberty" — cohabitation of a minor girl does not end the option; assent must come after puberty (express or implied, e.g., by cohabitation); the girl must be aware of the marriage to exercise the option; and consummation, to bar the option, must have been without her consent after majority (Baillie 1.59; Abdul Karim v. Amina Bai, AIR 1935 Bom 308). (5) On the facts she was below 15 at the alleged consummation; the suit — one mode of proclaiming the option — was brought within the three-year window (before 18). Option preserved; decree granted.

Principle evolved Option of puberty under s. 2(vii) DMMA: (i) consummation must be proved as a fact — no adverse presumption from refusal of medical examination; (ii) puberty presumed at 15; (iii) ratification or consent is effective only after puberty; (iv) cohabitation/consummation during minority does not destroy the option; the girl must know of the marriage before she can be expected to ratify it. Adopted with approval by Krishna Iyer J. in A. Yousuf Rawther.
③ Itwari v. Asghari
AIR 1960 All 684 · Allahabad High Court · S.S. Dhawan J. · Second appeal · (course pack pp. 269–275)
❖ Facts

Itwari married Asghari about 1950. Things went wrong; the wife left to live with her parents; the husband took no steps to bring her back and married another woman. The first wife then applied for maintenance under s. 488 CrPC; thereupon the husband sued for restitution of conjugal rights (impleading her father and two brothers). The wife alleged she had been turned out, that the husband formed an illicit union with the woman he later married, beat her, deprived her of ornaments, caused physical and mental pain, and never paid her dower. The Munsif decreed the husband's suit: cruelty unproved; a second marriage raises no presumption of ill-treatment; if aggrieved the wife "should have obtained a decree for dissolution" instead. The District Judge, Rampur reversed: the suit was a "counter-blast" to the maintenance claim; the husband's years of silence showed he "never really cared for her"; her evidence of ill-treatment was believed; a deserted wife would "not find peace with him after another woman had already been installed as his wife." Itwari appealed.

⚖ Issues
  1. Is the husband's taking of a second wife a ground for the first wife to refuse cohabitation / for dismissing his RCR suit?
  2. What degree of cruelty is needed to defeat a Muslim husband's suit for restitution of conjugal rights — must it render it "unsafe for the wife to return to his dominion" (Mulla, 14th ed. 246, borrowed from the Privy Council in Shamsunnissa Begum)?
  3. Is a court of equity bound to enforce the husband's right to consortium merely because polygamy is lawful?
❖ Arguments of the parties (Mr. N.A. Kazmi, for the husband)

(1) Cruelty defeating RCR is a mixed question of law and fact, so the High Court in second appeal may re-examine the evidence. (2) A Muslim has a personal-law right to take up to four wives — the mere fact of a second marriage is no proof of cruelty. (3) To defeat restitution there must be cruelty "of such a character as to render it unsafe for the wife to return to her husband's dominion" — mere second marriage falls far short.

(Wife's case as accepted below): the suit was mala fide, filed only to escape maintenance; years of desertion; specific acts of cruelty believed; equity should not compel her to share the husband with another woman.

✓ Decision

Appeal dismissed (under O. 41 R. 11 CPC). The husband's refusal of relief affirmed. The reasoning proceeds in five movements:

  1. RCR is equitable relief. Muslim marriage being a civil contract, an RCR suit merely enforces the right to consortium; disobedience is enforceable by imprisonment or attachment (Moonshee Buzloor Ruheem v. Shumsoonissa Begum, 11 Moo IA 551; Abdul Kadir v. Salima, ILR 8 All 149 FB) — but courts act "as mixed Courts of equity", taking the plaintiff's conduct into account: unclean hands, ulterior motives, or injustice in compelling cohabitation justify refusal.
  2. Polygamy is tolerated, never encouraged. Koran IV.3 ("marry... by twos, or threes, or fours... if ye fear that ye cannot be equitable, then only one") "was really a restrictive measure... it imposed a ceiling on conjugal greed"; the injunction to be impartial is "an impossible condition according to several Muslim jurists".
  3. The law already arms the first wife. She may stipulate for delegated divorce on the husband's second marriage (Badu Mia v. Badrannessa, AIR 1919 Cal 511; Ayatunnessa Beebee v. Karam Ali, ILR 36 Cal 23 — a second marriage is "a continuing wrong" to the first wife, giving a continuing right to exercise the option); a contract restraining a second marriage is not void under s. 23, Contract Act. A law that permits all this "prefers the breaking up of the first marriage to compelling the first wife to share her husband with the second".
  4. Cruelty is a universal, evolving standard. There is no 'Muslim' cruelty distinct from English law (PC in Shamsunnissa Begum); cruelty = conduct causing danger to life, limb, health (bodily or mental) or reasonable apprehension of it (Rayden); courts must heed "prevailing social conditions" — Muslim jurisprudence itself accommodates changing conditions (Abdur Rahim) — and the DMMA 1939 itself (s. 2(viii)(f): inequitable treatment of co-wives) proves the legislature's ameliorative intent (Sinha J. in Mt. Sofia Begum v. Zaheer Hasan, AIR 1947 All 16). Under modern conditions, "the importing of a second wife into the household ordinarily means a stinging insult to the first"; a husband "will be presumed to intend the natural consequences of his own conduct" (Simpson v. Simpson, 1951); hence "the very act of taking a second wife, in the absence of a weighty and convincing explanation, raises a presumption of cruelty to the first", with the onus on the husband to rebut (e.g., marriage at the first wife's suggestion).
  5. Equity can refuse even absent proof of cruelty where compulsion would be unjust (Hamid Hussain v. Kubra Begum, AIR 1918 All 235; Nawab Bibi v. Allah Ditta, AIR 1924 Lah 188; Khurshid Begum v. Abdul Rashid, AIR 1926 Nag 234). Dismissing the suit does not deny his right to polygamy — no bigamy prosecution, valid second marriage, legitimate children — but he cannot, "in his conjugal greed... like Oliver, ask[ ] for more". Here the suit was mala fide and specific cruelty believed: no decree.

Principle evolved The presumption of cruelty rule: taking a second wife, absent weighty explanation, raises a presumption of legal cruelty to the first wife, disentitling the husband to a decree for restitution of conjugal rights — a court of equity will not use its process to compel the first wife to cohabit. The standard of cruelty is universal-humanitarian and responsive to changing social conditions. (This case supplies the Muslim-law answer to the RCR question and is the classic companion to s. 2(viii)(f) DMMA.)
④ A. Yousuf Rawther v. Sowramma
AIR 1971 Ker 261 (= 1970 KLT 477) · Kerala High Court · V.R. Krishna Iyer J. · (course pack pp. 259–268)
❖ Facts

Sowramma, a Hanafi girl of around 15 (who had attained puberty before marriage), was married in 1962 to Yusuf Rawther, "nearly twice her age". The couple spent barely days together: the husband left for Coimbatore the very next day for his radio business; after about a month the girl returned to her parents — each side blaming the other. For over two years of separation the defendant admittedly failed to maintain her; his defence was that she wrongfully refused to return at the instance of her father. He moved the mosque committee (Exts. D2, D4) which reported divorce was the only solution. The wife sued for dissolution invoking s. 2(ii), (vii) and (ix) DMMA. The trial court dismissed; the Subordinate Judge decreed dissolution; the husband appealed. Concurrent findings: she was 15, had attained puberty, the marriage was consummated, the husband failed to maintain her for two years — and, crucially, "it was through her own conduct that she led her husband to stop maintenance for a period of 2 years". Krishna Iyer J. records his vain attempt at reconciliation and the subsequent events (his second marriage; her remarriage after the decree).

⚖ Issues
  1. Whether under s. 2(ii) the wife is entitled to dissolution on the mere fact of non-maintenance for two years — or only where, under general Muslim law, the husband was bound to maintain her (i.e., where her own conduct has not disentitled her to maintenance)? the seminal issue
  2. Whether s. 2(vii) (option of puberty) was made out — the courts found no proof she was under 15 at marriage and that the marriage was consummated.
  3. Whether s. 2(ix) preserved other Islamic grounds (khula) independent of s. 2(ii).
❖ Arguments of the parties

Appellant (husband): the claim rests on the failure of a wilfully erring wife to be maintained — a wife who wrongfully left the matrimonial home cannot take advantage of her own wrong; s. 2(ii) must be read with the general law whereunder the duty to maintain arises only if she lives with him ("she can only be maintained at the place where she ought properly to be" — Mudholkar J., Nagpur). Reliance: AIR 1951 Nag 375; Rajasthan DB AIR 1956 Raj 102; Peshawar & Allahabad views (AIR 1944 All 23; AIR 1947 All 3 — Allsop Ag. C.J.: the Act does not oblige the husband "to follow his wife wherever she may go and force money... upon her"). On s. 2(vii): Rabia Khatoon v. Mohd. Mukhtar Ahmad, AIR 1966 All 548, against her.

Respondent (wife) / amicus: the Act is beneficent social legislation for a weaker section and must be read in the light of the Islamic ethos; the Sind view (Tyabji CJ in Noor Bibi v. Pir Bux, AIR 1950 Sind 8, following Hajra's case; Beckett J. AIR 1943 Sind 65) and Manak Khan v. Mulkhan, AIR 1941 Lah 167 (Abdul Rashid J.) give s. 2(ii) its "plain ordinary grammatical meaning" — the conspicuous omission of the words "without reasonable cause" (which appear in cl. (iv)) shows that whatever the cause, two years' non-maintenance suffices; dissolution under the Act is not a punishment for the husband or a remedy enforcing maintenance — it recognises that the marriage has in reality ceased ("suspension of the marriage"), the state Islam abhors (Quran II:229, 231; IV:129; the Jameela/Thabit ibn Qais precedent in Bukhari).

✓ Decision

Appeal dismissed; decree for dissolution under s. 2(ii) affirmed. Krishna Iyer J.'s reasoning: (1) Beneficent construction — legislation protecting women must be read with "social perspective and purpose". (2) Islamic realism about divorce: marriage is a civil contract; the Quran forbids capricious divorce ("if they obey you, then do not seek a way against them" — IV:34); the Prophet called divorce "the most disliked of lawful things"; divorce is permissible "only in cases of extreme emergency" after failed reconciliation; Islam for the first time in Arabia gave women the right to seek divorce (the Thabit ibn Qais khula). (3) The split of authority resolved for the Sind view: Tyabji CJ — "the question whether there was a failure to maintain was a pure question of fact"; the principles of maintenance during marriage and dissolution are entirely different; dissolution is allowed "when a cessation of the state of marriage has in reality taken place, or the continuance of the marriage has become injurious to the wife"; prolonged failure to maintain is "an instance where a cessation or suspension of the marriage had occurred", so "the wife's disobedience or refusal to live with her husband does not affect the principle on which the dissolution is allowed". (4) Floodgates answer: quoting Bertrand Russell (Marriage and Morals) — "The easiest divorce laws by no means always produce the greatest number of divorces" — and the Marumakkathayam analogy: "The law has to provide for possibilities; social opinion regulates the probabilities." (5) Holding: "a Muslim woman, under s. 2(ii)... can sue for dissolution on the score that she has not as a fact been maintained even if there is good cause for it." (6) s. 2(ix) is "a statutory preservation of prior Islamic rights"; the khula claim was left undecided as unnecessary — the wife having succeeded under s. 2(ii). He adds the equity note: the wife's success restores "roughly equal rights" between the spouses, matching the talaq technique with khula under judicial supervision.

Principle evolved s. 2(ii) DMMA turns on the fact of non-maintenance, not on the wife's conduct or the husband's culpability: two years' failure to maintain — even due to poverty, or caused by the wife's own delinquency — entitles the wife to a decree. A marriage which has ceased to be a reality should not be kept alive. Heavily relied upon by the Supreme Court in Shamim Ara (2002) for its Quranic conception of divorce.
⑤ Shamim Ara v. State of U.P. Landmark — proof of talaq
2002 Cri LJ 4726 (SC) = AIR 2002 SC 3551 = (2002) 7 SCC 518 · Supreme Court · R.C. Lahoti J. · (course pack pp. 237–241)
❖ Facts

Shamim Ara married Abrar Ahmed in 1968 under Muslim Shariat law; four sons were born. On 12.4.1979 she applied under s. 125 CrPC for herself and her minor children, alleging desertion and cruelty. The Family Court, Allahabad (order dt. 3.4.1993) refused her maintenance holding she "was already divorced", allowing only Rs 150/month for one minor son. Crucially, the husband's written statement dated 5.12.1990 had taken a bald additional plea: "he had divorced the appellant on 11.7.1987 and since then the parties had ceased to be spouses" — no particulars of the talaq were pleaded; he also claimed the protection of the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986 (house delivered in lieu of mehar). In evidence he embellished: triple talaq on 11.7.1987 "at 11 a.m. in the presence of Mehboob and other 4-5 persons" — a fact nowhere in the written statement. The Family Court upheld "a strange story of divorce totally beyond the case set up", founding itself on an affidavit dated 31.8.1988 filed by the husband in some other civil suit (to which the wife was not a party) stating he had divorced her "15 months before". The High Court in revision invented a communication theory: though not pronounced in her presence, the divorce "would stand completed on 5.12.1990" — the date of filing the written statement — so maintenance ran only from 1.1.1988 to 5.12.1990 (Rs 200). The wife appealed to the Supreme Court.

⚖ Issue

"The singular issue... is whether the appellant can be said to have been divorced and the said divorce communicated to the appellant so as to become effective from 5.12.1990, the date of filing of the written statement by the respondent No. 2." (With the broader question: what constitutes a valid talaq under Muslim law as applied in India?)

❖ Arguments of the parties

Husband/Respondent: on the authority of rulings noted in Mulla (including an A.P. case, (1975) 1 APLJ 20) and Dr. Tahir Mahmood's commentaries, a statement by the husband in pleadings filed in answer to a maintenance petition that he had already divorced the wife operates as a divorce; the plea plus the 1988 affidavit corroborated the divorce; under classical Hanafi law no form, witnesses, or cause is needed and talaq in the wife's absence is effective.

Wife/Appellant: no divorce was ever pleaded with particulars, proved, or communicated; the affidavit was in proceedings not inter partes; the "plea-in-written-statement" doctrine rests on a misconception of the Quranic law of talaq; the High Court's deemed-communication theory has no warrant in any text.

✓ Decision

Appeal allowed. No divorce; the husband remains liable to maintain. R.C. Lahoti J.'s four-step analysis, now the settled law:

  1. The Quranic discipline of talaq. No scripture mentions divorce by recital in pleadings. The Court adopts the Gauhati High Court's statement of "the correct law of talaq as ordained by the Holy Quran": (i) talaq must be for a reasonable cause; (ii) it must be preceded by an attempt at reconciliation between husband and wife by two arbiters — one chosen by the wife from her family, the other by the husband from his — failing which talaq may be effected (Jiauddin Ahmed v. Anwara Begum, (1981) 1 GLR 358, Baharul Islam J.; approved by the DB in Rukia Khatun v. Abdul Khalique Laskar, (1981) 1 GLR 375, which expressly dissented from the Calcutta and Bombay views). The "whimsical and capricious divorce... good in law though bad in theology" formula treats women as chattel — which the Quran "does not brook".
  2. Endorsement of Krishna Iyer J. — Yousuf Rawther is quoted at length ("It is a popular fallacy that a Muslim male enjoys, under the Quranic law, unbridled authority to liquidate the marriage..."); so too V. Khalid J. in Mohammed Haneefa v. Pathummal Beevi (1972 Ker LT 512) quoting Pathayi v. Moideen (1968 Ker LT 763), deploring "this tyranny" over Muslim wives; and Bai Tahira v. Ali Hussain (AIR 1979 SC 362) — the beneficent, Art. 15(3)/38/39-informed reading of protective law.
  3. Talaq must be pronounced — and proved. "The talaq to be effective has to be pronounced": 'pronounce' means "to proclaim, to utter formally, to utter rhetorically, to declare, to articulate". There was no proof of talaq on 11.7.1987: no particulars, no evidence beyond the husband's own testimony, no substantiated reasons, no plea or proof of any reconciliation effort. "A mere plea taken in the written statement of a divorce having been pronounced sometime in the past cannot by itself be treated as effecting talaq on the date of delivery of the copy of the written statement to the wife. The respondent... ought to have adduced evidence and proved the pronouncement... if he failed in proving the plea raised in the written statement, the plea ought to have been treated as failed." The Court expressly disagreed with the Mulla/Tahir Mahmood line of cases treating an unsubstantiated plea of previous talaq as proof of divorce.
  4. The affidavit was worthless: a self-serving statement in previous proceedings not inter partes "could not have been read in evidence as relevant and of any value". Neither the marriage stood dissolved on 5.12.1990 nor did the maintenance liability end.
Principle evolved The Shamim Ara triple-test: for a talaq to be effective where contested, the husband must prove (1) the pronouncement, (2) a reasonable cause, and (3) a prior attempt at reconciliation by two arbiters; a plea of divorce in pleadings/affidavits is no evidence. Position after Shamim Ara (as synthesised by Masroor Ahmed): pronouncement must be proved; reasonable cause shown; reconciliation attempted; and communication made — applying to ahsan, hasan and bidaat alike.
⑥ Masroor Ahmed v. State (NCT of Delhi) Triple talaq + communication + rape FIR quashed
2008 (103) DRJ 137 (Del) · Delhi High Court · Badar Durrez Ahmed J. · s. 482 CrPC quashing · (course pack pp. 242–254)
❖ Facts — "the case is unusual because of the facts which led to the registration of the FIR"

Aisha Anjum married Masroor Ahmed on 2.4.2004; a daughter (Sara @ Ushna) was born on 22.10.2005. She left the matrimonial home on 8.4.2005 (she said, ejected over unmet dowry demands; he said, of her own will). In late October 2005, after his brother-in-law and sister failed to persuade her to return, the husband — "very sad and extremely angry", "in this mental condition", in the presence of his brother-in-law and another man — uttered the words of talaq approximately three times or even more. He "forgot about this incident"; the talaq was never communicated to the wife. On 23.3.2006 he filed a suit for restitution of conjugal rights stating she was "still the wife of the petitioner" (the talaq unmentioned); on 13.4.2006 she stated in court she was ready to join him, and she returned home with him from the court itself. Reminded by his brother-in-law that he had pronounced triple talaq in October 2005, and "not want[ing] any illegitimacy in his marital status", he consulted a mufti on 16.4.2006; the fatwa (17.4.2006) said three talaqs in one sitting = one talaq-e-rajai, revocable within the 3-month iddat; that period having elapsed, a fresh nikah was needed. A second nikah was performed on 19.4.2006 (her brother Shahid Naeem witnessing). Discord again; he pronounced talaq (again) on 28.8.2006 and left on 30.8.2006. At the CAW cell on 3.10.2006 he disclosed the October 2005 talaq; on 12.12.2006 she filed a written complaint leading to an FIR u/ss. 376/34 IPC — that the intercourse between 13.4.2006 and 19.4.2006 was rape, her consent having been procured by the fraud that he was still her husband. The ASJ dismissed his bail holding triple talaq = irrevocable talaq-ul-biddat; the second nikah void absent an intervening halala marriage; her consent "tainted". Masroor moved the High Court to quash the FIR.

⚖ The five questions framed
  1. What is the legality and effect of a triple talaq?
  2. Does a talaq given in anger result in dissolution of marriage?
  3. What is the effect of non-communication of the talaq to the wife?
  4. Was the purported talaq of October 2005 valid?
  5. What is the effect of the second nikah of 19.4.2006?

Preliminary concepts set out by the Court: usul al-fiqh (Quran, hadis/sunna, ijma, qiyas); ijtihad and the schools; fatwa (advisory, by a mufti) vs qaza (binding judgment, by a qazi) — both must rest on the shariat; s. 2 Shariat Act 1937; DMMA 1939 and the Maliki principle; khula/mubaraat/lian; and Zohara Khatoon on the unilateral talaq being peculiar to Muslim law.

❖ Arguments of the parties

Petitioner (husband): (1) the alleged October 2005 triple talaq did not dissolve the marriage — it was invalid and uncommunicated (Riaz Fatima v. Mohd Sharif, 135 (2006) DLT 205; Dagdu Chotu Pathan v. Rahimbi Dagdu Pathan, 2002(3) MhLJ 602 FB; Dilshad Begum (Bom, 17.1.2007); Shamim Ara); (2) the complainant continued to be his wife — a wife is expressly excepted from s. 375 IPC, so no rape; (3) in any event triple talaq in a single sitting is, at best, one talaq, making the second nikah permissible; (4) consent before the 19.4.2006 nikah may be presumed (State of A.P. v. P Narasimha, 1994 SCC (Cri) 1180).

State/Complainant: the prosecution rested on the talaq of October 2005 having dissolved the marriage — so the husband had deceived her into intercourse; the ASJ's bail order treated biddat as irrevocable and the second nikah as religiously impermissible without halala.

✓ Decision — the five answers
  1. Legality of triple talaq: ahsan and hasan have "legal recognition under all fiqh schools"; but talaq-e-bidaat is bidaat — an innovation, sinful, deprecated by the Prophet, not approved by any school, and not a valid divorce in Shia law; even among Sunnis many hold three pronouncements in one sitting to be one rajai talaq. "Its harsh abruptness has brought about extreme misery to the divorced women and even to the men..." — "if it is rooted out such a move would not be contrary to any basic tenet of Islam or the Quran or any ruling of the Prophet Muhammad." Held: a triple talaq, even for Sunni Muslims, shall be regarded as ONE revocable talaq — giving the husband time to think, the family time to reconcile, and (after iddat) the option of a fresh nikah on fresh mahr terms.
  2. Talaq in anger: "If a talaq is pronounced in extreme anger where the husband has lost control of himself it would not be effective or valid."
  3. Communication: Pronouncement "materially alters the status of the wife" — her iddat rights of residence and maintenance, deferred dower, custody, right to pledge the husband's credit, and the very possibility of reconciliation all presuppose knowledge. "Communication is an essential element of pronouncement." Where the wife is present, pronouncement and communication are simultaneous; where absent, "the talaq would be effective from the date the pronouncement is communicated to the wife"; if not communicated at all within a reasonable time, "a vital ingredient of pronouncement would be missing and such a talaq would not take effect."
  4. The October 2005 talaq — invalid: (i) given, if at all, in extreme anger; (ii) never communicated at least till 13.4/19.4.2006; (iii) no attempt at reconciliation in the Quranic manner, before or after. Hence the marital tie subsisted through 13.4–19.4.2006 → no offence of rape; the wife remained his wife (s. 375 exception).
  5. Second nikah: "not necessary" — the marriage never having dissolved, the second nikah was of no effect; had the talaq been valid it would (per the Court's view) have operated as one revocable talaq and the remarriage would have been permissible (with presumption of consent just prior). FIR quashed — the Court also noted the parties' genuine settlement and their daughter.
Principle evolved (1) Triple talaq in one sitting = one revocable talaq (a fortiori after Shamim Ara's procedural discipline); (2) talaq in extreme anger is invalid; (3) communication is an essential element — no communication, no talaq; (4) reconciliation (arbitration) is essential — before an irrevocable talaq, and for a revocable talaq either before pronouncement or during iddat; (5) a fatwa is merely advisory and cannot alter legal status. Note: Masroor Ahmed's liberal construction was later overtaken by Shayara Bano (2017), which voided talaq-e-biddat altogether.
⑦ Shayara Bano v. Union of India The Triple Talaq Judgment
(2017) 9 SCC 1 · Supreme Court · 5-Judge Constitution Bench · decided 22 August 2017 · Writ Petition (C) No. 118 of 2016
❖ Facts

Shayara Bano, married in 2002, was in April 2016 given an instant talaq-e-biddat (triple talaq) by her husband at her parents' home in Uttarakhand. She moved the Supreme Court under Art. 32 pleading that talaq-e-biddat, nikah halala and polygamy practised by Muslims violated Arts. 14, 15, 21 and 25 of the Constitution. She was joined by intervening women petitioners (Aafreen Rehman, Gulshan Parveen, Ishrat Jahan, Atiya Sabri); respondents included the Union of India, the All India Muslim Personal Law Board and Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind. A 5-judge Constitution Bench (CJI J.S. Khehar, Kurian Joseph, R.F. Nariman, U.U. Lalit, S. Abdul Nazeer JJ.) confined itself to one question: whether talaq-e-biddat has legal sanction (leaving halala and polygamy open).

⚖ Issues
  1. Is talaq-e-biddat (instant triple talaq) an integral/essential part of Islamic practice protected by Art. 25?
  2. Is the talaq-e-biddat form of divorce "law in force" under Art. 13(1) (via s. 2 of the Shariat Act, 1937) amenable to fundamental-rights scrutiny?
  3. Does instant triple talaq violate Arts. 14 (equality/non-arbitrariness), 15 and 21?
  4. If invalid, what is the remedy — declaration, injunction and legislative space?
❖ Arguments of the parties

Petitioners: talaq-e-biddat is a "sinful innovation" of the Omayyad era, abhorred by the Quran's own scheme (3 monthly pronouncements across tuhrs with arbitration, per Surahs II:228-229, IV:35, XXXIII:49); it is not an essential religious practice; s. 2 of the Shariat Act made talaq "law", hence subject to Part III; it is manifestly arbitrary — an uncanalised, unguided power dissolving marriage at whim, unequally burdening only Muslim wives; several Muslim-majority countries (Pakistan, Bangladesh, and others) had already legislated against it.

Respondents (AIMPLB/Jamiat): talaq-e-biddat is a matter of personal law/religious practice since 1400 years; uncodified personal law is not "law in force" under Art. 13, hence immune from fundamental-rights review; the Court should not direct legislation; at best, the community would reform itself (the AIMPLB even offered guidelines and a suggestion of a stipulation against instant talaq in nikahnamas). The Union of India supported the petitioners.

✓ Decision — 3 : 2, talaq-e-biddat set aside
Fig. 9 — The bench and the three opinions

Operative order: by majority, "the practice of talaq-e-biddat — triple talaq (in any one of its three forms)" is set aside as unconstitutional/void; the Union was asked to consider legislation. The decision thus produced the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019.

Principles evolved (1) Personal law as statutory law is subject to Part III — s. 2 of the Shariat Act, 1937, by making shariat the rule of decision, brings talaq within Art. 13(1); (2) manifest arbitrariness is an independent ground to invalidate "law" under Art. 14 (later applied in Joseph Shine, Swiss Ribbons); (3) essential religious practices — a practice sinful and disapproved within the religion itself is not essential, hence unprotected; (4) what is "bad in theology is bad in law". Capstone of the arc: Rashid Ahmad (1932, PC — bidaat valid) → Shamim Ara (2002 — must be proved + cause + arbitration) → Masroor Ahmed (2008 — one revocable talaq; communication essential) → Shayara Bano (2017 — no instant triple talaq at all) → 2019 Act (criminalised).

PART 6

Effects of Divorce & the Post-Shayara Bano Position

6.1 Effects of divorce (Mulla, para 336)

① Remarriage

Consummated marriage: the wife may marry another husband after iddat; if not consummated, she is free to marry immediately. A husband who had four wives (incl. the divorced one) may marry another after her iddat.

② Dower

On divorce of a consummated marriage, the whole unpaid dower — prompt and deferred — becomes immediately payable; if not consummated: half the specified dower, or (if unspecified) three articles of dress. s. 5 DMMA saves her dower rights.

③ Inheritance

Mutual rights of inheritance cease the moment the divorce becomes irrevocable (exception: talaq during the husband's death-illness — wife inherits until iddat expires).

During iddat she also keeps the right of residence and maintenance (Masroor Ahmed, para 35), and her right to pledge the husband's credit for subsistence ends only on knowledge of the divorce. Post-iddat maintenance of a divorced Muslim woman is governed by the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986 as read in Danial Latifi v. UOI (2001) 7 SCC 740 — a Unit IX topic (fair and reasonable provision beyond iddat, payable by relatives/Wakf Board/state); recall it here only as a cross-reference.

6.2 The current law of talaq — Shayara Bano + the 2019 Act

After 22 August 2017, talaq-e-biddat has no legal sanction; after 1 August 2019, it is also a statutory wrong:

Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019Provision
s. 3 — DeclarationAny pronouncement of talaq-e-biddat by a Muslim husband upon his wife, in any manner — spoken, written, electronic (SMS, email, WhatsApp etc.) — is void and illegal.
s. 4 — PunishmentWhoever pronounces talaq-e-biddat: imprisonment up to 3 years + fine; offence cognizable (arrest without warrant, but only on information by the married Muslim woman upon whom talaq is pronounced), non-bailable, triable by Magistrate First Class.
s. 5 — Allowance & custodyThe married Muslim woman upon whom talaq-e-biddat is pronounced is entitled to an allowance for herself and her dependent children (determined by the Magistrate) from the husband, and custody of her minor children, "in such manner as may be determined by the Magistrate".
s. 7 — CompoundabilityThe offence is compoundable at the instance of the married Muslim woman on settlement; a Magistrate may also grant bail to the accused, and the offence is non-cognizable in the stated sense.
Exam nuance: The 2019 Act makes instant talaq void and criminal — so the marriage continues; the correct modes now are talaq (ahsan/hasan), khula, mubarat, tafweez, or a DMMA decree. Critics (incl. the dissent's logic) argue criminalisation converts a civil wrong into a crime; supporters point to Shayara Bano's direction to legislate.

20 marks · asked 2017 (Q6), 2018 (Q7), 2019 (Q8a), 2020 (Q5), 2021 (Q5), 2022 (Q7) — the paper's favourite
"Discuss the validity of Talaq under Muslim law with reference to statutory provisions and case law. / Critically examine the law relating to Triple Talaq (Talaq-e-Biddat) in India."
Model answer — framework: Introduction → Modes & forms → Statutory frame → Judicial evolution (4 cases) → Constitution Bench → 2019 Act → Conclusion
I. Introduction

Under Muslim law marriage (nikah) is a civil contract; its dissolution is permitted but discouraged — divorce is "the most disliked of lawful things" in the Prophet's words. The husband's unilateral power of talaq is "especially peculiar to Mahomedan law" (Zohara Khatoon v. Mohd. Ibrahim, (1981) 2 SCC 509). The Indian legal journey has moved from the Privy Council's form-over-substance approach (Rashid Ahmad, 1932) to constitutional invalidation of instant triple talaq (Shayara Bano, 2017) and its criminalisation (Act 18 of 2019).

II. Statutory framework

(i) S. 2, Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937: dissolution of marriage "including talaq, ila, zihar, lian, khula and mubaraat" is to be decided by the Shariat notwithstanding custom. (ii) S. 2, DMMA 1939 gives the wife judicial grounds (see MA-2). (iii) Post-2019: ss. 3–5, Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019.

III. Requisites of a valid talaq (Hanafi law)
  • Capacity: Muslim, sound mind, attained puberty (presumed at 15).
  • Form: oral or written (talaqnama); no particular form of words; express words need no proof of intent; ambiguous words do. Need not be in the wife's presence, but she must be named (Calcutta view).
  • Contingent talaq is valid (Hanafi; not Shia).
  • Classical indulgences: talaq valid even under compulsion, in jest or voluntary intoxication (Mulla 315; Rashid Ahmad v. Anisa Khatun, AIR 1932 PC 25 — "A talak actually pronounced under compulsion or in jest is valid and effective"). The modern Court has qualified the anger indulgence: talaq in extreme anger with loss of self-control is invalid (Masroor Ahmed).
IV. Forms and their validity

(a) Talaq-us-sunnat — approved: Ahsan (single pronouncement in tuhr + abstinence through iddat; revocable till iddat ends) and Hasan (three pronouncements in three successive tuhrs; first two revocable, third irrevocable) — valid in all schools.

(b) Talaq-ul-bidaat — disapproved: three pronouncements in one sitting/tuhr, or a single irrevocable declaration. Introduced by the Omayyads; "good in law though bad in theology" (Mulla 311); not recognised by Shia law; the most prevalent form in India. Classical PC view: immediately effective irrespective of iddat, communication, or the husband's real intention (Rashid Ahmad). Modern transformation: see V below.

V. Judicial evolution — the four milestones
  • Jiauddin Ahmed v. Anwara Begum (1981) 1 GLR 358 & Rukia Khatun v. Abdul Khalique Laskar (1981) 1 GLR 375 (Gauhati HC, Baharul Islam J.): the "correct law of talaq as ordained by the Holy Quran" is that (i) talaq must be for a reasonable cause and (ii) preceded by an attempt at reconciliation by two arbiters, one from each family. The "good in law, bad in theology" doctrine treats women as chattel, which the Quran "does not brook".
  • Shamim Ara v. State of U.P., (2002) 7 SCC 518: talaq "has to be pronounced"; a bald plea of previous talaq in a written statement (or an affidavit in non-inter-partes proceedings) is not proof; the husband must lead evidence of (1) pronouncement, (2) reasonable cause, (3) reconciliation attempt. Adopted the Gauhati formulation and Krishna Iyer J.'s Yousuf Rawther critique of "unbridled authority to liquidate the marriage".
  • Masroor Ahmed v. State (NCT of Delhi), 2008 (103) DRJ 137: triple talaq, even among Sunnis, should be regarded as one revocable talaq; talaq in extreme anger is invalid; and communication is an essential element of pronouncement — an uncommunicated talaq does not take effect.
  • Shayara Bano v. Union of India, (2017) 9 SCC 1 (22.08.2017, 5 judges, 3:2): talaq-e-biddat is void. Nariman & Lalit JJ.: it is "law in force" through s. 2 of the Shariat Act and manifestly arbitrary under Art. 14 (instant, capricious, without attempt at reconciliation). Kurian Joseph J.: it is not an essential religious practice — "what is bad in theology is bad in law". Dissent (Khehar CJI & Nazeer J.): uncodified personal law is outside Art. 13; protected under Art. 25; should be met by legislative injunction, not invalidation.
VI. The 2019 Act — the present position

Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019: s. 3 — talaq-e-biddat (spoken, written or electronic) is void and illegal; s. 4 — punishable up to 3 years + fine (cognizable on the wife's information, non-bailable); s. 5 — Magistrate-determined allowance and custody of minor children for the wife; s. 7 — compoundable on settlement. Valid divorce now only through ahsan/hasan talaq, khula, mubarat, tafweez or a DMMA decree.

VII. Conclusion

The law today validates only the Prophet-approved forms of talaq, disciplines every pronouncement by cause, arbitration and communication (Shamim Ara–Masroor line), and abolishes the instant form altogether (Shayara Bano; 2019 Act). The trajectory — from Rashid Ahmad's formalism to constitutional review of personal law — is the classic illustration of personal law being brought into conformity with the Constitution's promise of gender equality (Arts. 14, 15(3), 21, 25 read with 13).

10 marks · asked 2023 (Q8b)
"Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939 — state the grounds on which a Muslim wife may obtain a decree for dissolution of her marriage."
Model answer

Object: Act 8 of 1939 "consolidates and clarifies" Muslim law on the wife's suit for dissolution and removes doubts on apostasy. Before it, a Hanafi wife had virtually no judicial remedy; the Statement of Objects invoked the Hanafi jurists' own rule that in hardship the Maliki/Shafii/Hanbali law may be applied — the Act extends such beneficial rules to all Muslim women, of every school.

Section 2 — the nine grounds: a woman married under Muslim law may obtain a decree on any one or more of: (i) husband's whereabouts unknown for 4 years (decree suspended 6 months; set aside if he appears and undertakes conjugal duties — proviso (b); s. 3 requires notice to his would-be heirs, incl. paternal uncle/brother); (ii) neglect/failure to provide maintenance for 2 years — A. Yousuf Rawther v. Sowramma, AIR 1971 Ker 261: mere fact of non-maintenance suffices; (iii) imprisonment for 7 years or upwards (decree only after sentence final); (iv) failure without reasonable cause to perform marital obligations for 3 years; (v) impotency at marriage and continuing (1-year cure window); (vi) insanity for 2 years or virulent venereal disease (leprosy omitted, 2019); (vii) option of puberty — married by father/guardian before 15, repudiated before 18, marriage not consummated (Mt. Ghulam Sakina v. Falak Sher, AIR 1950 Lah 45 — consummation must be proved as fact; minority cohabitation does not destroy the option); (viii) cruelty in six forms — habitual assault/making life miserable, evil repute, forcing immoral life, disposal of her property, obstruction of religion, and inequitable treatment of multiple wives (cf. Itwari v. Asghari, AIR 1960 All 684); (ix) any other ground valid under Muslim law — covering lian (false charge of adultery), kafat, and even irretrievable breakdown (Amma Khatoon v. Kashim Ansari, AIR 2001 Jhar 28).

Ancillary sections: s. 4 — a married Muslim woman's apostasy/conversion no longer dissolves the marriage by itself; she may still sue under s. 2; proviso — a convert to Islam who re-embraces her former faith stands outside s. 4 (marriage dissolves). s. 5 — dower rights unaffected. Conclusion: the DMMA is the judicial counterpart of talaq: it converted the wife's fragmented rights into a statutory charter, applying "beneficial provisions of one school to adherents of other schools" (Masroor Ahmed) — a "very salutary principle".

10 marks · asked (Q3a)
"Explain the Option of Puberty under Hindu and Muslim law."
Model answer

Muslim law: A marriage contracted on behalf of a minor by a guardian is not void; the minor, on attaining puberty (presumed at 15 unless earlier proved), may ratify or repudiate it — the khyar-ul-bulugh or option of puberty. Statutory form: s. 2(vii) DMMA 1939 — where she was given in marriage by her father or other guardian before 15, she may repudiate before 18, provided the marriage has not been consummated. Rules from Mt. Ghulam Sakina v. Falak Sher (AIR 1950 Lah 45): (1) repudiation is by suit; (2) anything done during minority (even cohabitation) does not extinguish the right accruing after puberty; (3) her assent must come after puberty, express or implied (cohabitation after awareness); (4) consummation to bar the option must be with her consent after majority; (5) refusal of medical examination raises no presumption of consummation (following the PC in Atkia Begum); (6) before 1939 no repudiation lay against the father's/father's father's marriage — the Act put all guardians on par. Contrast Rabia Khatoon v. Mohd. Mukhtar Ahmad (AIR 1966 All 548) (restrictive) as noted in Yousuf Rawther.

Hindu law: Under the pre-1956 shastric law an infant marriage was validated on the option doctrine in reverse — refusal to marry on attaining majority annulled it. Under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, a child marriage is not void; by the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006, s. 3: a child marriage is voidable at the option of the contracting child — the party who was a child may petition within 2 years of attaining majority; where the male is above 18 and the female a minor, only she gets the option. Decree consequences include maintenance and s. 5 PCMA powers (orders for residence, maintenance until remarriage). Additionally, under s. 13(2)(iv) HMA, a wife married before 15 and who has repudiated the marriage before 18 may seek divorce. Conclusion: both systems protect the minor's free consent — Muslim law through the 15/18 DMMA option, Hindu law through PCMA's voidability with the 18+2 formulation.

20 marks · recurring (DMMA/ Divorce theme; pairs with Q8b-2023 & Yousuf Rawther)
"The words 'has failed to provide' in s. 2(ii) of the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939 imply a duty to provide." Critically examine this statement with case law.
Model answer
I. The provision and the problem

S. 2(ii) DMMA: the wife may sue where "the husband has neglected or has failed to provide for her maintenance for a period of two years." Clause (iv), dealing with failure of marital obligations, expressly says "without reasonable cause"; clause (ii) omits those words. Two questions follow: (1) is failure to maintain actionable regardless of cause (poverty, illness, imprisonment)? (2) is it actionable even where the wife's own conduct — refusal to live with the husband, unchastity — caused the failure?

II. The duty view (restrictive)

Early courts held the Act did not abrogate the general Muslim law: a husband "cannot be said to have neglected or failed to provide maintenance for his wife unless under the general Mahomedan Law he was under an obligation to maintain her" (Chief Court of Sind, 1942). Zafar Hussain v. Akbari Begum (AIR 1944 Lah 336): "neglect implies wilful failure"; the words "has failed to provide" imply "an omission of duty". The Nagpur HC (AIR 1951 Nag 375, Mudholkar J.): the Act "crystallises a portion of the Muslim law" and must be read with the whole law — a wife who for no reason stays away from the house where she "ought properly to be" cannot claim maintenance there, so no "failure". The Rajasthan DB (AIR 1956 Raj 102): failure must be "without any justification"; Peshawar and Allahabad (Allsop Ag. C.J., AIR 1947 All 3) similar: the husband "is not bound to follow his wife wherever she may go and force money or food or clothes upon her". On this view a disloyal or disobedient wife gets no decree.

III. The fact view (beneficent/plain-grammatical)

Manak Khan v. Mulkhan (AIR 1941 Lah 167, Abdul Rashid J.): since cl. (iv) expressly contains "without reasonable cause" and cl. (ii) does not, "whatever the cause may be the wife is entitled to a decree... even though the wife may have contributed towards the failure." Noor Bibi v. Pir Bux (AIR 1950 Sind 8, Tyabji C.J., reaffirming Hajra's case): whether there was failure to maintain is "a pure question of fact" not depending on the wife's right to enforce maintenance; a man may "fail to maintain his wife" even where she could not in law claim maintenance, just as he may fail to pay "debts of honour". The principles of maintenance durante marriage and of dissolution are "entirely different": dissolution is granted "when a cessation of the state of marriage has in reality taken place, or the continuance of the marriage has become injurious to the wife" — the Quran abhors suspension of marriage and enjoins either kind treatment or release (II:229, 231; IV:129; the Jameela–Thabit ibn Qais khula). Mulla's note: failure need not be wilful — poverty, failing health, loss of work, imprisonment all entitle the wife; even a rich wife needs no independent means.

IV. The Kerala resolution — A. Yousuf Rawther v. Sowramma

Krishna Iyer J. adopted the Sind view: beneficent legislation for a weaker section must be read with "social perspective and purpose"; s. 2(ii) "does not speak of the wife's right of maintenance but only of the fact of her being provided with maintenance"; the fear that women may divorce "by their own delinquency" is misplaced (Bertrand Russell: "The easiest divorce laws by no means always produce the greatest number of divorces"). Holding: "a Muslim woman, under s. 2(ii) of the Act, can sue for dissolution on the score that she has not as a fact been maintained even if there is good cause for it." s. 2(ix) independently preserves prior Islamic rights (khula).

V. Assessment

The fact view better fits (a) the legislative contrast between cls. (ii) and (iv); (b) the Act's ameliorative object (SOR: "unspeakable misery to innumerable Muslim women"); (c) the Islamic conception that dissolution responds to the reality of a dead marriage, not to the husband's fault; and (d) gender parity — the husband needs no cause for talaq, so the wife's 2-year non-maintenance ground needs no proof of his breach. The duty view survives only at the margins (e.g., where her conduct wholly disentitles her to any maintenance, some courts still deny the decree); Mulla's editor too would read in a conduct-limiter. Conclusion: the statement is unsustainable as an absolute proposition; the better view, per Yousuf Rawther and the Sind line, is that s. 2(ii) looks to the fact of non-maintenance — the wife's own conduct is irrelevant to the ground, though it may colour relief and maintenance claims.

10 marks · asked (Q1d)
"What is a fasid (irregular) marriage? State its legal implications."
Model answer

Concept (Mulla 264): a marriage which is not valid may be void (batil — unlawful in itself, perpetual and absolute prohibition: consanguinity, affinity, fosterage) or irregular (fasid — unlawful "for something else": temporary or relative prohibition, or accidental defect). The five classical irregular marriages: (a) without witnesses; (b) fifth wife; (c) with a woman undergoing iddat; (d) prohibited by difference of religion; (e) unlawful conjunction (two sisters, aunt & niece — jama bain-al-mahramain).

Legal implications (Mulla 267): (1) either party may terminate it, before or after consummation, by words showing intention to separate; (2) it has no legal effect before consummation; (3) if consummated — wife entitled to dower (proper or specified, whichever is less); she must observe iddat (three courses, for divorce or death); issue is legitimate; but no mutual rights of inheritance between spouses. (Shia law treats many Sunni-irregular marriages as void outright.)

Judicial gloss — Chand Patel v. Bismillah Begum, 1 (2008) DMC 588 (SC): whether unlawful conjunction (marriage with the sister of an existing wife) is void or irregular divided the courts — Aizunnissa v. Karimunissa (Cal, 1895: void; children illegitimate) versus Tajbi Abalal Desai v. Mowla Alikhan Desai (Bom, 1917: irregular — the prohibition is temporary, removable by death/divorce of the first wife; supported by Fatawa-i-Alamgiri, Baillie, Ameer Ali, Tyabji, Abdur Rahim), followed by Oudh, Lahore and Madras. The SC adopted the Bombay view: "the bar of unlawful conjunction renders a marriage irregular and not void"; an irregular marriage subsists till terminated in accordance with law; hence both wives are entitled to maintenance under s. 125 CrPC. Conclusion: the fasid category shows Muslim law's middle path — the defect is curable; women and children of such marriages are protected rather than cast out.

10 marks · asked (Q1c, Q8a)
"Write a note on Muta marriage and its legal implications (Nikah vs Muta)."
Model answer

Nature: Muta (literally "enjoyment") is a temporary marriage for a fixed period and specified dower, recognised only by the Ithna Ashari (Shia) school; Sunni law does not recognise it. Essentials: fixation of the term (hours, days, years) and of dower; no witnesses required; no divorce needed — it ends by expiry of the term or by hiba-i-muddat (the husband's gift of the unexpired term, which releases the wife for the balance).

Legal implications: (1) children are legitimate and inherit from both parents (mutual inheritance between spouses only if the contract so provides/custom permits); (2) the wife is entitled to the whole specified dower even if the term expires without consummation (contrast nikah: half if unconsummated); (3) no mutual inheritance between the spouses by default; (4) the wife is not entitled to maintenance as of right; (5) she need not observe iddat except in certain cases (and must observe it after expiry if consummated — 2 courses; if the marriage was with a minor/unmarried woman, longer); (6) any number of muta marriages may be contracted (polygamy limits of nikah do not apply); (7) it is a contract of enjoyment, not a continuing status — hence no presumption of conjugal rights or RCR. Position in India: recognised only where the parties follow Ithna Ashari law (Bombay, Madras & Sind choices-of-law historically); Atika Bibi v. Abu Mohammed type disputes turned on proof of the term. Contrast with nikah: permanence vs term; dower default rules; maintenance; inheritance; divorce machinery (talaq/khula) vs expiry/hiba. Conclusion: muta remains a school-specific institution; for Sunnis such a union is either void or treated as nikah if the parties intended permanence.

10–20 marks · asked (Q8a, Q6) — Unit VIII/IX bridge
"Discuss the claim of a Muslim wife to maintenance under s. 125 CrPC and the effect of a plea of talaq on it."
Model answer

The claim: s. 125 CrPC gives a divorced wife (not able to maintain herself) a summary right of maintenance against a husband with sufficient means who neglects or refuses to maintain her — designed to prevent vagrancy; Arts. 15(3), 38, 39 inform its beneficent construction (Bai Tahira v. Ali Hussain, AIR 1979 SC 362). After Shah Bano and the 1986 Act, the Muslim divorced woman's post-iddat entitlement runs through the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986, read in Danial Latifi v. UOI (2001) 7 SCC 740: the husband must make a fair and reasonable provision beyond iddat within iddat; the relatives/Wakf Board/state scheme is the fallback — hence the Act survives constitutionally. (Unit IX elaboration.)

The talaq-plea gambit — Shamim Ara v. State of U.P. (2002): husbands routinely plead a past talaq to cut off maintenance. The Supreme Court closed the door: (1) talaq must be pronounced and proved by evidence — "a mere plea taken in the written statement... cannot by itself be treated as effecting talaq"; (2) the plea must show reasonable cause and a prior attempt at reconciliation by two arbiters; (3) self-serving affidavits in non-inter-partes proceedings are worthless. Until the husband discharges this burden, the woman remains a "wife" for s. 125. Masroor Ahmed adds the communication requirement; Chand Patel shows that even a fasid (irregular) marriage subsists till annulled, entitling its wife to maintenance. Itwari v. Asghari shows the converse weapon: a mala fide RCR suit filed to defeat maintenance will be dismissed as inequitable. Conclusion: proof-crippled pleas of talaq no longer defeat the summary remedy; the burden squarely lies on the husband.

20 marks · asked (Q7a & b; Q1) — Unit VII bridge (needed for divorce answers)
"State the essentials of a valid nikah under Sunni and Shia law."
Model answer (condensed)

Common essentials: (1) parties of sound mind and past puberty (15 presumption; minors may be contracted by guardians); (2) free consent — proposal (ijab) and acceptance (qubul) at one meeting, in terms signifying marriage, without conditions defeating it; (3) absence of (i) absolute prohibitions — consanguinity, affinity, fosterage (void), and (ii) relative prohibitions — unlawful conjunction, fifth wife, iddat, difference of religion, absence of witnesses (irregular/fasid); (4) dower (consideration); (5) no polyandry; (6) not in violation of the doctrine of permanent scarcity? — (mainly) the above suffice.

Sunni (Hanafi) specifics: proposal & acceptance in the presence of two adult witnesses (their absence makes it irregular, not void); no wali (guardian) indispensable for an adult; writing unnecessary; no specific formula. Shia specifics: witnesses not required for validity but recitation of the formula is; guardian's (wali's) participation valued; a marriage in defiance of a specific prohibition (e.g., ninth wife in aggregate; marriage during pilgrimage ihram) is void; temporary marriage (muta) recognised. Legal effects of valid nikah: legality of intercourse; children's legitimacy and mutual inheritance; wife's dower and maintenance; wife's observance of iddat; prohibition of a fifth wife simultaneously; lawful consortium — enforced, if need be, by a suit for restitution of conjugal rights, which equity may refuse where the husband's second wife has made cohabitation unjust (Itwari v. Asghari, AIR 1960 All 684).

10 marks · asked (Q1b)
"Compare prohibited relationships under Hindu and Muslim law."
Model answer

Hindu law (s. 5(iv)-(v) HMA): marriage within the prohibited degrees (lineal ascent, spouse of lineal ascendant/descendant, sister/aunt etc. as enumerated) or between sapindas (offering pinda — generally 3 generations ascending through the mother and 5 through the father) is void, unless custom permits (s. 5 proviso). Sapinda extends equally to both sides; half/uterine blood rules apply.

Muslim law: prohibitions divide into (1) absolute/perpetual — consanguinity (mother, daughter, sister, aunt, niece...), affinity (mother-in-law, step-daughter raised by her mother, son's wife's mother...), fosterage (wet-nurse within forbidden degrees) — marriage void (batil), children illegitimate, no dower; and (2) relative/temporary — unlawful conjunction (two wives so related they could not have intermarried, e.g., two sisters; a fifth wife; a woman in iddat; difference of religion) — marriage merely irregular (fasid), curable (e.g., by divorcing the first sister), with the consequences stated in Chand Patel v. Bismillah Begum (subsistence till annulled; s. 125 maintenance). Contrast: Hindu law knows no "irregular" middle category and bars both sides symmetrically; Muslim law grades defects and preserves the status/legitimacy of issue under fasid marriages; both allow custom a role (HMA expressly; Muslim law subject to the Shariat Act's override of contrary custom).
