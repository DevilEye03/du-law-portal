PART 1

Unit VI — Sources of Muslim Law

Framing. Family law regulates marriage, divorce, inheritance, succession, adoption, minority and guardianship; in India these matters are governed by the different personal laws — Hindu law (including Jains, Buddhists and Sikhs), Muslim law, Christian law, Parsi law, and the secular Special Marriage Act, 1954. Who is a Muslim? A person whose faith is in Islam — Islam means "submission to the will of God" (and, derivatively, establishment of peace): one who believes that Allah is one and Muhammad is His messenger. For legal purposes a Muslim is one who believes in the one God and the prophethood of Muhammad, and who is governed by Muslim personal law (a person can also become Muslim by a bona fide profession of faith; conversion is dealt with under DMMA s. 4 — see Unit VIII notes).

Regulatory anchor — the Shariat Act, 1937: application of Muslim personal law (as against contrary custom) is regulated by the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937. S. 2: where both parties are Muslim, the rule of decision shall be Muslim personal law (notwithstanding any custom or usage to the contrary) in questions of: (a) intestate succession; (b) special property of females; (c) marriage; (d) dissolution of marriage — including talaq, ila, zihar, lian, khula and mubaraat; (e) maintenance; (f) dower; (g) guardianship; (h) gifts; (i) trusts and trust properties; (j) wakfs (other than charities and charitable institutions and charitable and religious endowments). (Save questions relating to agricultural land. Per your class notes, the Act does not extend to adoption, wills and legacies — s. 2 originally left these to custom; s. 5 on the District Judge's power in dissolution suits was repealed by the DMMA scheme, and s. 5 of the Act itself was repealed by the 1939 Act's s. 6, itself later repealed.)

1.1 Historical foundation (as set out in the class notes)

Fig. 1 — From the Prophet to the Schools: timeline (based on DU class notes)

The Sunni–Shia split was political before it was religious: Sunnis (view urged by Ayesha, the Prophet's youngest wife) advocated election — Abu Bakr was elected first Caliph; Shias (view urged by Fatima, the Prophet's daughter) held that spiritual leadership passes by nobility of blood — succession, not election — and installed Ali as first Imam. The murders of Ali, Hasan and Hussain (Karbala, 680 AD, mourned on Muharram) widened the split into legal divergence. The Motazila sect — defectors from the Shia fold, 9th century AD, associated with Ata-al-Ghazzal — associated with neither mainstream.

1.2 The sources — primary (formal) and secondary (informal)

Fig. 2 — Sources of Muslim Law

The two conceptions of Muslim law (class notes)

① Shariat — the revealed law

Wider, broader orbit covering all human actions with various grades of approval (obligatory, recommended, permitted, disapproved, forbidden); God and the Prophet "laid down the path of shariat".

② Fiqh — the science of jurisprudence

Narrower — the classification of acts as legal/illegal; the whole structure is erected by human agency: the ulema's systematic inquiry (ijtihad) into the four roots (usul al-fiqh).

Related terms from the notes: Tafsir (discussion/discourse on the texts) → yields Qiyas (analytical deduction) and Ijtihad (personal thought of the learned) → Fiqh (jurisprudence) → crystallised through Ijma (consensus) → SHARIAT (Islamic law). The doctrine of ijtihad closed among Sunnis with the four schools; Shia tradition vests interpretive authority in the Imam.

The division is political in origin (succession to the Prophet) but produced legal divergence: differences in reliance on different hadith, different ijma, and different attitudes to qiyas. Shias reject all traditions/words handed down by anyone other than Ali and his line — hence Shia-t-i-Ali, "the faction of Ali". Sunnis accept the traditions and decisions of the Imams and the general body of juristic learning supplementing the Quran.

PART 3

Unit VII — Nikah: Nature, Objects & Solemnisation

3.1 Meaning

Nikah literally means the conjunction of the sexes — "the marriage of the sexes"; some derive it from a root meaning "to tie together". Legally, Mulla: marriage (nikah) is "a legal contract which has for its object the procreation and legalisation of children" — a civil contract which legalises intercourse and legitimises children.

3.2 Nature — contract, sacrament, or both?

The contractual view

Justice Mahmood (Allahabad HC): Muslim marriage "is not a sacrament but purely a civil contract". Support: consent through offer–acceptance; consideration (mahr); capacity; absence of mandatory ceremony or priesthood; dissolution allowed. Abdul Kadir v. Salima (1886, Mahmood J.): marriage is a contract and the analogy Muslim jurists use is that of sale (hence dower compared to the price); dower as "consideration" is nevertheless not Contract-Act consideration — it is an obligation imposed as a mark of respect to the wife (see Part 6).

The religious-duty view

Sulaiman C.J., Anis Begum v. Muhammad Istafa (1933) 55 All 743: Islamic law regards marriage partly as ibadat (devotional acts) and partly as muamlat (worldly dealings/contract). Abdul Rahim likewise terms marriage of the nature of ibadat and muamlat. The Prophet: "Marriage is my sunna; whosoever keeps away from it is not of me." Celibacy is not approved; even the clergy are expected to marry (contrast the Roman Catholic church).

Social dimension: nikah is a well-established social institution which confers a dignified status upon a woman — it converts her from an object of male possession (the pre-Islamic position) into a legal person with contractual rights (dower, maintenance, inheritance). As Krishna Iyer J. observed in Yousuf Rawther (quoted in your Unit VIII material), marriage under Islam is a civil contract yet rights and responsibilities attached to it are "of such importance to the welfare of humanity that a high degree of sanctity is attached to it."

3.3 Objects of marriage

① Legalisation of intercourse

The lawful regulation of the sexual relationship between husband and wife — intercourse outside marriage is prohibited (zina).

② Legalisation of generations

Legitimisation of children — the child of a valid marriage is legitimate and inherits from both parents (in a fasid marriage too, if consummated; never in a batil marriage).

③ Social & religious aims

Promotion of love, mutual support and creation of families — the essential units of society; performance of a religious duty (sunnah of the Prophet); protection of the dignity and identity of women.

PART 4

Unit VII — Conditions for a Valid Nikah (Elements & Components)

The five conditions of validity are: (a) capacity of the parties; (b) free consent (of the parties or their guardians); (c) proposal and acceptance; (d) absence of prohibitions/impediments; (e) consideration (dower) — plus, for Sunnis, the witness requirement. Defects produce the tripartite classification of Part 5.

4.1 Capacity to contract marriage

Puberty (bulugh)

Any Muslim of sound mind who has attained puberty can marry. Privy Council, Mt. Atika Begum v. Mohammad Ibrahim: a girl becomes major on happening of either (i) completion of the 15th year, or (ii) actual attainment of puberty at an earlier age. Presumption of puberty at 15 (rebuttable — burden on the person pleading earlier puberty, per Ghulam Sakina). The rule applies to boys equally. Minors may be married by guardians (jabr) — the minor's later option of puberty (15→18, s. 2(vii) DMMA; Ghulam Sakina) protects against a forced marriage.

Sound mind; guardian's role

Persons of unsound mind cannot marry; a lunatic may marry during a lucid interval. Where parties are minors (or insane), the guardian's consent operates: father, and failing him paternal grandfather, paternal uncle, brother and other paternal relations (Shia: the guardianship of the father/grandfather is primary). Failing any, the Qazi/government may act. A father may bind a minor son by a dower contract (Mulla 288). Modern overlay: POCSO/PCMA (see MA-8).

4.2 Free consent

  • Consent must be free — vitiated by force, fraud or undue influence; consent obtained by fraud/force renders the marriage voidable/void depending on school and degree (class notes: marriage under compulsion is void in all schools except Hanafi, where it is valid).
  • Sane adult parties: their own consent is required; minors: guardian's consent (jabr).
  • Consent must relate to the marriage itself, in unequivocal terms; a proposal and acceptance "without ambiguities" — words must not be capable of innocent interpretation.
  • An assurance to marry in future is not a proposal — a betrothal is not a nikah.

4.3 Proposal & acceptance (ijab–qubul) — the rules

Unit VII — Classification and Types of Muslim Marriages

5.1 MUTA marriage — the temporary union (Ithna Ashari only)

Muta literally means "enjoyment" or use: a temporary union of a man and woman for a specified period on payment of a specified consideration (dower). Recognised only by the Ithna Ashari Shia school; obsolete/unrecognised in Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi, Hanbali and (practically) in India. Hyderabad view: a muta for an unspecified period = muta for life = a permanent nikah; it is the specification of the term that makes it temporary.

Essentials (8)
  • Parties past puberty, sound mind; free consent
  • Formalities of declaration & acceptance (no witnesses needed)
  • Parties not within prohibited relationships
  • Religion bar: man may contract muta with a Muslim, Christian, Jewish woman or even a fire-worshipper, but no other; a Shia woman cannot contract muta with a non-Muslim; affinity bars apply
  • A man may contract any NUMBER of muta marriages
  • Term fixed (day, month, year, term of years)
  • Dower specified
  • Term fixed + dower unspecified → void; dower specified + term unspecified → void as muta but may operate as permanent nikah
Legal effects
  • Cohabitation lawful; children legitimate and inherit from both parents
  • No mutual inheritance between the spouses (unless custom provides)
  • No maintenance as of right under Shia law — though the muta wife may claim under s. 125 CrPC as a neglected wife
  • Ends ipso facto on expiry of term; husband may end earlier by hiba-i-muddat (gift of the unexpired term) without the wife's consent; no divorce machinery
  • Dower: full if consummated; half if not; if the wife leaves before term, proportionate deduction
  • Iddat: if consummated — two monthly courses; on death of husband — 4 months 10 days

PART 6

Unit VII — Dower (Mahr)

6.1 Definitions — the three you must quote

Mulla (para 285)

"Mahr or dower is a sum of money or other property which the wife is entitled to receive from the husband in consideration of the marriage."

Wilson

"Dower is a consideration for the surrender of person by the wife."

Ameer Ali

"Dower is a consideration which belongs absolutely to the wife." (Mulla: a sum of money or property the wife is entitled to receive from the husband in consideration of marriage.)

Justice Mahmood, Abdul Kadir v. Salima (1886): dower was compared to the price in a contract of sale because marriage is a civil contract and sale is the typical contract to which Muslim jurists refer by analogy — but "consideration" is not used in the Contract Act sense: dower is an obligation imposed upon the husband as a mark of respect to the wife. It is an essential incident of the status of marriage (Privy Council): if unspecified at the time of marriage, "the law declares that it must be adjudged on definite principles" (proper dower). Not to be confused with dowry under the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 — amounts paid by a Muslim in connection with a daughter's marriage for purchase of property in the joint names of the couple were held NOT "dowry" (Mulla note on para 285).

6.3 Prompt vs deferred — the presumptions and the rules

SituationRule
Contract silent on prompt/deferredShia law: the whole is presumed prompt. Sunni law: part prompt, part deferred — proportion fixed by custom, failing which by status of parties and amount. Madras HC (all schools): presume prompt unless payment expressly postponed. Case constants: Eidan v. Mazhar Husain (1/5th of Rs 5,000 prompt); Taufik-un-nissa v. Ghulam Kambar & Fatma Bibi v. Sadruddin (1/3rd); Nasiruddin Shah v. Amatul Mughni Begum (presumption: half-and-half absent custom).
Deferred described as "payable on demand"Postponement until demand makes it prompt in law; but merely demanding deferred dower does not make it prompt (Mulla 290).
When does dower "become confirmed" (payable in law)?(a) Consummation; (b) valid retirement (khilwat-e-sahiha — Hanafi/Maliki: whole dower due; Prophet's hadith: "who removes the veil... the whole dower becomes due"); (c) death of either spouse. Shia law: only by consummation or death — not by valid retirement (Mulla 289A).
Right to withhold conjugal societyThe wife "can refuse to live with her husband and admit him to sexual intercourse so long as the prompt dower is not paid". If the husband sues for RCR before consummation — non-payment is a complete defence (suit dismissed); after consummation with her free consent — decree for RCR only conditional on payment of prompt dower (Mulla 293). Right precedes cohabitation.
On divorce/dissolutionWhole unpaid dower (prompt + deferred) immediately payable if the marriage was consummated; if not consummated — half the specified dower; if nothing specified — three articles of dress. s. 5 DMMA 1939: dissolution does not affect her dower rights (Unit VIII link).

6.4 Remission, recovery, widow's rights

Remission by the wife (Mulla 291)
  • She may remit dower or any part in favour of the husband or his heirs — valid even without consideration.
  • Must be with free consent — remission under "great mental distress owing to her husband's death" is not binding.
  • Majority debate: Madras/Patna — remission by a wife under 18 (Indian Majority Act) invalid; Allahabad (better view) — a girl who has attained puberty may relinquish dower though below 18 (IMA s. 2 excludes "marriage or dower").
  • A bar on remission without the consent of her relations is a valid stipulation.
Recovery & limitation (Mulla 292)
  • Wife (and after her death her heirs) may sue. Prompt dower: 3 years from demand & refusal (or dissolution if no demand); deferred: 3 years from dissolution (old Arts. 103–104).
  • Limitation Act 1963 contains no dower articles; s. 29(3) argument — suits under marriage/divorce law not barred.
  • Where divorce is by writing, time runs from communication to the wife (ties to Masroor Ahmed!).
  • Payments by the husband not allocated to dower cannot be presumed gifts — "debitor non praesumitur donare" does not apply as between husband and wife.
Dower as a debt; heirs (Mulla 294–295)
  • Dower ranks as a debt against the husband's estate — but an unsecured one: the widow stands with other creditors; no charge on specific property (unless created by agreement or a final decree, which courts avoid).
  • Heirs are not personally liable — each liable only to the extent of his/her share of the estate (worked example in Mulla 294: widow's 1/8 × debt, son's 7/16 × debt, etc.).
  • An heir may alienate his share before payment; alienation subject to the widow's right of retention if she holds possession.
Widow's right of retention (Mulla 296–299)
  • Where the widow has "lawfully and without force or fraud" obtained possession of her husband's property in lieu of her dower, she may retain possession against the heirs and creditors until the dower is satisfied — to account for rents/profits beyond the debt.
  • Right extinguished on payment; not a "lien" strictly, not analogous to a mortgage, and gives no title; no right of retention during the continuance of marriage.
  • Conflict: Madras & Bombay — no consent of heirs needed; Calcutta & Patna — consent required; Allahabad wavering. Where she lived in the husband's house in his lifetime and continues, the presumption is of lawful possession in lieu of dower.
Exam hook: dower questions fold neatly into maintenance/RCR answers: the prompt-dower defence (Mulla 293) + Itwari equity (Unit VIII) + s. 5 DMMA + Chand Patel's s. 125 holding = a complete "rights of a Muslim wife" arc.

① Ms. Ghulam Kubra Bibi v. Mohd. Shafi Mohd. Din
AIR 1940 Pesh 2 · Peshawar High Court · Mir Ahmad J. · First appeal + cross-objection · (course pack pp. 223–224)
❖ Facts

Mohammad Shafi sued Mt. Ghulam Kubra for restitution of conjugal rights, also impleading her parents and seeking an injunction to restrain them from interfering with his marital relations. The defence: she was never married to him; and a question arose whether she was of age at the time of the alleged marriage. The Mullah who appeared deposed that he read the nikah at the instance of the girl's grandfather and categorically denied that anyone was sent to the girl to enquire from her whether she agreed to the marriage. One Mistri Abdul Karim "vaguely deposed that there were two witnesses of the nikah" without names; two witnesses (Mohammad Ramzan and Mohammad Din) alleged they were witnesses of the nikah but were "laconic" — they stopped at that, gave no detail; one was the plaintiff's neighbour, the other his workmate of 8–9 years. The trial Judge held the girl was of age at the marriage, the marriage proved, and granted the decree as prayed (including the injunction). The District Court maintained the decree but set aside the injunction against the parents. Both sides came up: the girl against the RCR decree; the husband for restoration of the injunction.

⚖ Issues
  1. Was there a valid nikah — in particular, did the essentials of consent and witnesses stand proved?
  2. Was the girl a major under Muslim law at the marriage (and thus personally required to consent), given she was 17 — the parties labelling her minor because they assumed majority at 18?
  3. Could an injunction issue against the wife's parents restraining interference?
❖ Arguments of the parties

Husband (plaintiff): the marriage was performed by the Mullah; two witnesses were produced who deposed to being witnesses of the nikah; the girl's grandfather as guardian contracted the marriage on her behalf — lawful under Muslim law; therefore cohabitation should be decreed and the parents injuncted.

Wife (defendant): no marriage at all — nobody ever sought her consent; the Mullah denies anyone was sent to her; the "witnesses" never said what they witnessed or that they heard her consent; and even as a minor-marriage it fails because she was in fact of age (17 > puberty presumed at 15), so only her own free consent could create a nikah, which was never obtained.

✓ Decision

Both courts below reversed in substance: no valid marriage; the husband's suit dismissed with costs throughout; the girl's appeal allowed; the husband's appeal for the injunction dismissed. Mir Ahmad J.'s reasoning: (1) Under Mahomedan law, the man or someone on his behalf and the woman or someone on her behalf must agree to the marriage at one meeting, and the agreement must be witnessed by two adult witnesses; the customary procedure (relation sent inside with two witnesses, dower explained, her authorisation obtained, then declaration before the Mullah) was described at length to show that "the vague allegation that there were two witnesses of the nikah has no value" — it must be proved that the whole procedure was gone through. (2) The girl was 17 at marriage; the parties evidently laboured under the impression that majority came at 18 "as is the general law", and therefore the grandfather gave her away without consulting her; but under Mahomedan law a girl becomes major for purposes of marriage on reaching puberty, presumed at 15 — she was thus an adult, and for an adult, personal consent was indispensable; a minor's marriage by father/grandfather/paternal relations is permissible, but that route was closed here. (3) Even a nikah contracted by a guardian "requires two adult witnesses" — and the two produced might as well have been witnesses of the giving away by the grandfather, not of the contract itself; the evidence could not exclude that. Hence no valid marriage and no right to consortium — or to an injunction against her parents.

Principle evolved Proof of nikah = proof of the whole solemnisation process: agreement at one meeting (by the parties if adults; by duly authorised guardians for minors) + two adult witnesses who can vouch for the contract itself, not merely the ceremony. Age of majority for marriage = puberty, presumed at 15 — not 18. A girl aged 17 given away by her grandfather without her consent has no marriage. The case is the classic authority for the consent + witnesses essentials (used constantly in "essentials of nikah" answers).
② Chand Patel v. Bismillah Begum Unlawful conjunction — fasid or batil?
1 (2008) DMC 588 (SC) · Supreme Court of India · Altamas Kabir J. · Criminal Appeal — s. 125 CrPC · (course pack pp. 225–232)
❖ Facts

Bismillah Begum (respondent 1) applied under s. 125 CrPC in the Court of the Judicial Magistrate, First Class, Chincholi (Criminal Misc. 6 of 2001) for maintenance for herself and her minor daughter Taheman Bano @ Rs 1,000 p.m. each against Chand Patel. Her case: she was the legally wedded wife, married about eight years earlier; the marriage was consummated; a daughter was born two years later. She candidly admitted that Chand Patel was married to her elder sister, Mashaq Bee, and that with the first wife's consent he had married her as well; the nikahnama was executed but misplaced; he lived with both wives under one roof and had accepted and brought up the child. With time the relationship deteriorated; he neglected the respondents, who had no means. The Magistrate disbelieved his denial of the marriage, held neglect proved, and awarded Rs 1,000 p.m. to each. The Sessions/District Court (Gulbarga) dismissed both revisions: personal law cannot obstruct maintenance under s. 125 (Nanak Chand v. Chandra Kishore Aggarwal, AIR 1970 SC 446 — s. 488/125 applies to all religions irrespective of personal law; Re Hussain Saheb, 1985 Cri LJ 1505 (AP)); a Magistrate cannot try the validity of marriage; the husband "must maintain the wife and children till the marriage between them declares null and void by the competent court". The High Court dismissed his s. 482 petition. On appeal (delay condoned) the question reached the Supreme Court.

⚖ Issue

"This appeal raises an interesting question of law as to whether a marriage performed by a person professing the Muslim faith with his wife's sister, while his earlier marriage with the other sister was still subsisting, would be void in law or merely irregular or voidable even though the subsequent marriage may have been consummated" — with the consequence of entitlement (or not) to s. 125 maintenance.

❖ Arguments of the parties

Appellant (Chand Patel — Mr. Raja Venkatappa Naik): Muslim law specifically prohibits "unlawful conjunction" — marriage with the wife's sister during the first wife's lifetime; the marriage, if performed, was void from inception and conferred no rights on respondent 1 (or, in paternity-disputing submission, on the child); support drawn from Rameshchandra Daga v. Rameshwari Daga (2005) 2 SCC 33 (HMA ss. 11/12 — a subsisting prior marriage made the later Hindu marriage a nullity, though maintenance was upheld) and Savitaben Somabhai Bhatiya v. State of Gujarat (2005) 3 SCC 636 (s. 125 covers an illegitimate child but not a woman not lawfully married — no artificial enlargement of "wife"). Hence the courts below erred in granting maintenance.

Respondents (Mrs. K. Sarada Devi): in a s. 125 proceeding the court is not required to adjudicate the validity of a marriage — a prima facie view suffices; the marriage was performed with full knowledge of all parties and at the instance of the appellant himself, who now took "recourse to technicality" to escape maintenance; and till a competent court declares the marriage void, it continues to subsist with all its rights for the wife and daughter. (Reliance on the Magistrate/revisional line: Nanak Chand; Re Hussain Saheb.)

✓ Decision

Appeal dismissed. The marriage was held IRREGULAR (fasid), not void; the wife and daughter remain entitled to maintenance under s. 125 CrPC. The Court (Altamas Kabir J.): (1) The answer turns on the legal status of the union: if void — no maintenance; if irregular — "the marriage will subsist for all purposes, unless declared to be void by a competent court", and till such declaration both respondents get maintenance. (2) Analogy with HMA ss. 11–12: s. 11 marriages are void ipso jure (Yamunabai Anantrao Adhav); s. 12 marriages are voidable and subsist until annulled. (3) Muslim law's parallel tripartite scheme (Mulla paras 260–267): para 263 — unlawful conjunction (jama bain-al-mahramain): "a man may not have at the same time two wives who are so related to each other by consanguinity, affinity or fosterage that if either of them had been a male, they could not have lawfully intermarried, as for instance two sisters, or aunt and niece. The bar of unlawful conjunction renders a marriage irregular, not void." (4) Judicial history: Aizunnissa v. Karimunissa (Cal, 1895 — void; children illegitimate) versus Tajbi Abalal Desai v. Mowla Alikhan Desai (Bom, 1917 — irregular, on the Fatawa-i-Alamgiri: marriage with a permanently prohibited woman is void, but with a temporarily prohibited one (whose bar death/divorce can remove) if consummated may have legal consequences), followed by Oudh (Kaniza v. Hasan Ahmad Khan, 1925), Lahore (Taliamand v. Muhammad Din, 1930) and Madras (Rahiman Bibi Saheba v. Mahboob Bibi Saheba, 1937) — all against Aizunnissa; the Bombay reasoning harmonises with Baillie, Ameer Ali, Tyabji and Abdur Rahim. (5) Holding: "since a marriage, which is temporarily prohibited may be rendered lawful once the prohibition is removed, such a marriage is in our view irregular (fasid) and not void (batil)… the bar of unlawful conjunction renders a marriage irregular and not void"; an irregular marriage "continues to subsist till terminated in accordance with law", so respondent 1 and her daughter are entitled to maintenance under s. 125. Arrears within six months; current maintenance from March 2008.

Principle evolved (1) Unlawful conjunction → fasid, not batil: the prohibition is relative/temporary (curable by divorcing the first sister), so the marriage subsists until annulled; (2) an irregular marriage carries civil consequences — dower (proper/specified, whichever less), iddat of three courses, legitimacy of children — though no mutual inheritance; (3) for s. 125 CrPC, the Magistrate need not — and cannot — try the validity of marriage; the "wife" and children are maintained unless and until a competent court declares the marriage void (Nanak Chand; distinguished from Savitaben, which turned on the Hindu wife's statutory monogamy). Links: Unit VII (classification) and Unit VIII/IX (maintenance).
③ Saiyid Rashid Ahmad v. Mt. Anisa Khatun Triple talaq — form over intention
AIR 1932 PC 25 · Privy Council · Lord Thankerton · Appeal from Allahabad HC · (course pack pp. 233–236)
❖ Facts

Succession dispute to the estate of Ghiyas Uddin (d. 4.4.1920). The appellants — his brother and sister — denied that respondents 1–6 were his lawful widow and children. Four matrimonial stages of Anisa Fatima: (1) marriage to Manzur Husain (1901); (2) divorce by Manzur (early 1905); (3) marriage to Ghiyas Uddin (28.8.1905); (4) divorce by Ghiyas Uddin (13.9.1905) — a triple talaq pronounced before witnesses but in the wife's absence, Rs 1,000 paid as dower the same day, and a talaqnama of 17.9.1905 stating the three divorces were given "in the abominate form" (bidaat). Concurrent findings: the triple talaq was pronounced and the deed genuine. The Subordinate Judge held the divorce effective — she was not his widow at his death, and the six children, all born after the divorce, were illegitimate. The High Court reversed: the divorce was "fictitious… a mock ceremony performed by Ghiyas Uddin to satisfy his father, without any intention on his part that it should be real or effective."

⚖ Issues
  1. Was the 1905 triple talaq valid and effective — in the bidaat form or the ahsan form?
  2. Could the husband's secret mental intention (a ceremony to pacify his father) negate an otherwise formally pronounced talaq?
  3. Could the children be legitimised by his long recognition of them (presumption of an intervening halala marriage)?
❖ Arguments of the parties

Appellants (collateral heirs): the concurrent findings establish a real triple talaq in the bidaat form — immediately irrevocable irrespective of iddat; under the settled law a talaq "actually pronounced under compulsion or in jest is valid and effective", so the alleged intention is irrelevant; 15 years of subsequent cohabitation and acknowledgment cannot undo a completed dissolution or raise a presumption of remarriage because the legal bar (halala) stands in the way.

Respondents (widow & children): the ceremony was a sham with no animus divorcendi; in any event the Court should infer from the husband's lifelong treatment of Anisa Fatima as wife and the children as legitimate, plus the acknowledgment doctrine in Habibur Rahman Chowdhury v. Altaf Ali Chowdhury (AIR 1922 PC 159), that an intervening marriage-divorce had occurred; the burden lay on the appellants to displace the presumption.

✓ Decision

Appeal allowed; High Court reversed; Subordinate Judge's decree restored. Lord Thankerton adopted Wilson's classification of talaq forms: ahsan (once + abstinence through iddat), hasan (thrice in successive periods of purity), and the third/fourth methods — thrice at shorter intervals or even in immediate succession, or once with words of immediate irrevocability — which are bidaat: "sinful, but… nevertheless regarded by Sunni lawyers as legally valid". The words here — "I divorce Anisa Khatun for ever and render her haram for me", thrice repeated, plus the talaqnama — showed the bidaat form, under which "the divorce at once becomes irrevocable, irrespective of the iddat". The wife's presence was unnecessary; her alimony could continue until she was informed. Intention is immaterial: the High Court's "mock ceremony" view was "contrary to all authority — a talak actually pronounced under compulsion or in jest is valid and effective." The presumption of legitimacy fails at the threshold: the legal bar to remarriage (remarriage only after her intervening marriage and its dissolution + iddat) must itself be proved removed before any acknowledgment presumption arises — which the respondents never did. The children, born after the irrevocable divorce, were illegitimate and excluded from inheritance.

Principle evolved In classical Hanafi law, form prevails over intention: a triple talaq in the bidaat form operates immediately and irrevocably; neither compulsion, jest, nor secret mental reservation avails the husband; legitimacy of post-divorce children cannot be presumed across the halala bar. The case's afterlife: it states the doctrine that Shamim Ara (2002) gradually unwrote (pronouncement must be proved, with cause and arbitration) and Shayara Bano (2017) abolished (bidaat void) — the starting point of the triple-talaq arc your examiner expects you to narrate.

20 marks · asked twice (Q7a & b; Q1)
"Essentials of Nikah under Sunni and Shia law."
Model answer
I. Meaning & nature

Nikah — the conjunction of the sexes; a civil contract (Justice Mahmood) whose object is the legalisation of intercourse and the legalisation of generations; partly ibadat and partly muamlat (Sulaiman C.J., Anis Begum); the sunna of the Prophet. Because it is contractual, no ceremony or priesthood is essential — only the contractual conditions.

II. The essentials (common to both schools)
  1. Capacity: sound mind + puberty (PC, Atika Begum: 15 years or earlier actual puberty); minors married by guardians (jabr), protected later by the option of puberty (s. 2(vii) DMMA).
  2. Free consent: of the parties (if adult/sane) or their guardians; vitiated by force, fraud, undue influence; compulsion-marriage valid under Hanafi classical law but void under Shia law.
  3. Ijab and qubul: proposal and acceptance at one meeting, oral or written, without ambiguity, by the parties or their agents; a promise to marry in future is not a proposal.
  4. Absence of impediments: absolute (polyandry, consanguinity, affinity, fosterage — void) and relative (unlawful conjunction, fifth wife, difference of religion, absence of witnesses, iddat — irregular in Sunni law; Shia law has no middle category).
  5. Dower (consideration): obligation imposed as a mark of respect; if unspecified — proper (mahr-i-misl) dower.
  6. Witnesses (Sunni only): two males or one male and two females; absence → fasid, not void. Shia law requires no witnesses — the marriage is valid without them.
III. Sunni–Shia contrasts to close

Witnesses (required/optional); consent under compulsion (valid/void); fifth wife & iddat & non-Kitabia wife (irregular/void); muta (unrecognised/recognised); proof-of-nikah standard — Ghulam Kubra Bibi: the whole process (one-meeting agreement + two adult witnesses) must be proved; vague witness evidence worthless.

IV. Conclusion

Whether valid (sahih), irregular (fasid) or void (batil) turns on which condition is defective; cite Ghulam Kubra Bibi (consent + witnesses), Atika Begum (puberty), Chand Patel (fasid marriage's civil consequences) — and note the modern overlay of PCMA 2006/POCSO.

20 marks · asked twice (Q6; Q8b)
"Essentials of Muslim marriage and restitution of conjugal rights."
Model answer
Part A — Essentials

(State the five essentials + witnesses as in MA-1, with Fig. 4/5 of these notes: capacity, free consent, ijab-qubul, no impediment, dower; classification follows the defect — sahih/fasid/batil.)

Part B — Restitution of conjugal rights under Muslim law
  • Basis: marriage being a civil contract, either spouse may sue for the return of conjugal society — the suit enforces "the right to consortium"; disobedience is enforceable by attachment of property or imprisonment (Moonshee Buzloor Ruheem v. Shumsoonissa Begum, PC; Abdul Kadir v. Salima, FB — courts act "as mixed Courts of equity").
  • Wife's defences: (i) non-payment of prompt dower — a complete defence before consummation; after consummation, decree only conditional on payment (Mulla 293); (ii) cruelty — to a degree rendering it unsafe for her to return to his dominion (PC in Shamsunnissa Begum); (iii) gross failure of marital obligations (Moonshee Buzloor Ruheem); (iv) equity of the second marriage — Itwari v. Asghari (AIR 1960 All 684): where the husband has taken a second wife, in the absence of weighty explanation the court presumes cruelty to the first and refuses the "equitable relief" of restitution; (v) conditional decrees — Anis Begum v. Muhammad Istafa (Sulaiman C.J.): decree for the husband on conditions (no mistress in the house, separate house, two servants of her choice); (vi) minority/pregnancy/absence of guardian consent bars as applicable.
  • Structure the answer: essentials (A) → RCR as contractual remedy (B) → defences with Itwari as the centrepiece → conclusion (the Muslim husband's RCR remedy is hedged by equity and the dower-linked right of the wife to withhold society).
10 marks · asked twice (Q1c; Q8a)
"Muta marriage and its legal implications (Nikah vs Muta)."
Model answer

Definition: temporary union for a fixed term on specified dower — Ithna Ashari Shia only ("muta" = enjoyment); Sunnis do not recognise it. Essentials: puberty + sound mind + free consent; declaration & acceptance; no prohibited relationship; religion rule (man: Muslim/Christian/Jewish/fire-worshipper woman, no other; woman: Muslim man only); term fixed; dower specified; any number permissible. Term fixed without dower → void; dower without term → void as muta but may operate as permanent nikah (Hyderabad view: unspecified term = life = permanent). Implications: no mutual inheritance (children legitimate, inherit both parents); no maintenance as of right (s. 125 CrPC possible); dower — full on consummation, half if not; wife leaving early → proportionate deduction; dissolution — expiry of term ipso facto, hiba-i-muddat, death; no divorce; iddat — 2 courses (consummation) or 4 months 10 days (death). Comparison table (duration, witnesses, number of wives, dower, inheritance, maintenance, divorce, recognition) as in Part 5 of these notes. Conclusion: an institution of the Shia school, practically obsolete in India; a permanent nikah may not be dressed as muta — the specified term is decisive.

10 marks · asked (Q1b)
"Prohibited relationships under Hindu and Muslim law."
Model answer

Hindu law (s. 5(iv)–(v) HMA): marriage within the prohibited degrees (lineal ascent; spouse of certain lineals; sisters/aunts etc.) or between sapindas (3 generations up through the mother, 5 through the father) is void — s. 11; custom may save (s. 5 proviso). No "irregular" middle class.

Muslim law: two tiers — absolute (consanguinity — mother/daughter/sister/niece/aunt lines; affinity — wife's mother, wife's daughter (if consummated), father's wife, son's wife; fosterage — milk relations within the same bars; polyandry; wife of another) → batil; and relative (unlawful conjunction — e.g., two sisters; fifth wife; woman in iddat; difference of religion; absence of witnesses) → fasid in Sunni law, void in Shia law. Cures: divorce the obstructing wife; wait out iddat; conversion per the inter-religious matrix.

Judicial authority: Chand Patel v. Bismillah Begum — marriage with the wife's sister during the first marriage is irregular (fasid), not void; it subsists till annulled, and both wives claim s. 125 maintenance. Contrast: Hindu law's defects are graded only as void/voidable; Muslim law grades them as void/irregular, protecting women and children of fasid unions (legitimate issue, dower, iddat) — a functional similarity to the Hindu voidable category (Chand Patel expressly analogises HMA s. 12).

10 marks · asked (Q1d)
"Fasid marriage and its legal implications."
Model answer

Meaning: irregular marriage — not unlawful in itself but unlawful "for something else": a temporary/relative prohibition or an accidental defect (Mulla 264): the five classical instances — no witnesses; fifth wife; woman in iddat; difference of religion; unlawful conjunction (with the pregnancy/guardian-consent variants noted). Not recognised in Shia law (valid or void only).

Legal implications (Mulla 267): (1) terminable by either party, before or after consummation, by words showing intention to separate ("I have relinquished you"); (2) no legal effect before consummation; (3) if consummated — wife entitled to dower (proper or specified, whichever is less); bound to observe iddat of three courses (divorce or death alike); issue legitimate; (4) no mutual inheritance between spouses; (5) the marriage subsists till terminated/declared void — hence, per Chand Patel, both wives of an unlawfully-conjoined husband are entitled to maintenance under s. 125 CrPC until a competent court annuls the marriage. Conclusion: the fasid category softens the consequences of curable defects — the law protects the woman and children rather than casting the union into nullity (contrast batil: no rights, illegitimate issue).

20 marks · core syllabus (dower appears inside marriage/maintenance questions; prepare as full essay)
"Define dower. Distinguish prompt and deferred dower and explain the widow's right of retention."
Model answer
I. Definition & nature

Mulla/Wilson/Ameer Ali definitions (Part 6). Nature: not Contract-Act consideration but an obligation imposed on the husband as a mark of respect to the wife (Mahmood J., Abdul Kadir v. Salima); sale analogy acknowledged but rejected; an essential incident of the status of marriage (PC); distinct from dowry. Objects: security/dignity of the wife; a check on capricious divorce (a high dower deters talaq).

II. Kinds

Specified (mahr-i-musamma): any amount the husband pleases, but not less than 10 dirhams (Hanafi; ≈ Rs 3–4); Shia — no minimum, proper dower capped at 500 dirhams; may be fixed before/at/after marriage; father's contract binds the minor son. Proper (mahr-i-misl): where unspecified — even if the marriage stipulated no dower — determined by the family standard (father's sisters), the wife's qualities, husband's means and social standing. Specified dower splits into prompt (on demand; wife may refuse cohabitation till paid; complete defence to RCR before consummation, conditional decree after) and deferred (on death/divorce; unsecured debt against the estate). Presumptions where the contract is silent: Shia — all prompt; Sunni — part/part by custom/status (cases: Eidan 1/5th; Taufik-un-nissa 1/3rd; Nasiruddin Shah — half-half); Madras — prompt unless expressly postponed. Confirmation: consummation, valid retirement (khilwat-e-sahiha; Shia: not), or death.

III. Remission & recovery

Remission valid without consideration but needs free consent (not under death-distress); puberty suffices to remit (Allahabad view). Recovery: suit by wife or her heirs; limitation under the old Arts. 103–104; written divorce → time from communication (link to Masroor Ahmed).

IV. Widow's right of retention

Where she has lawfully, without force or fraud, obtained possession of the husband's property in lieu of dower, she may retain it against heirs and creditors until satisfaction, accounting for surplus rents — a right "sometimes called a lien, but not a lien in the strict sense"; not analogous to a mortgage; gives no title; unavailable during marriage; extinguished on payment. Consent-of-heirs debate (Madras/Bombay vs Calcutta/Patna; Allahabad divided). Presumption where she lived in the husband's house in his lifetime. Heirs' liability: proportionate to their shares (Mulla's worked example); dower an unsecured debt; courts reluctant to create charges by decree.

V. Conclusion

Dower is the Muslim wife's greatest statutory-recognised civil protection — pre-dating and paralleling modern maintenance law; s. 5 DMMA and s. 125 CrPC round it off (Chand Patel; Danial Latifi, Unit IX).

10/20 marks · core Unit VI (no dedicated capsule row — highest-probability "extra")
"Discuss the sources of Muslim law and the differences between the Sunni and Shia schools."
Model answer
I. Sources (Fig. 2)

Primary: (1) Quran — direct word of God via Gabriel over 23 years; ~6,000 verses, ~200 legal; (2) Sunnah/Hadith — qaul, fail, tuqrir; (3) Ijma — consensus of mujtahids where the texts are silent; validated by Quran and Sunnah; (4) Qiyas — analogical deduction from the three; rejected by Shias (law can be widened only by the Imam). Secondary: customs (urf); judicial decisions (e.g., Hamira Bibi v. Zubaida Bibi — interest on dower allowed contrary to pure law); legislation (Shariat Act 1937; DMMA 1939; MWPRDA 1986); justice, equity & good conscience (Abu Hanifa's istihsan). Mention the two conceptions: Shariat (revealed path, wide) vs fiqh (jurists' science, narrow) — and the Shariat Act 1937 s. 2 (matters listed; custom displaced; agricultural land excluded; adoption/wills/legacies outside).

II. Schools (Fig. 3)

Sunni: Hanafi (Abu Hanifa; India's dominant school; Hedaya; Fatawa-i-Alamgiri), Maliki (Malik ibn Anas; Madinan practice; liberal judicial-divorce doctrine — seed of the DMMA), Shafei (Ash-Shafei; systematised usul), Hanbali (Ibn Hanbal; most literal). Shia: Ithna Ashari/Twelvers (Akhbari & Usuli sub-sects), Ismailia (Khojas — East; Bohras — West; Daudi & Sulaimani), Zaidi; plus the Motazila defectors (9th c.). Differences table (Part 2): authority of traditions; qiyas; witnesses for marriage/talaq; compulsion; iddat/fifth-wife/non-Kitabia bars (fasid vs void); bidaat talaq; muta; dower minima and prompt/deferred presumption; khula revocability. Origin: the succession dispute — election (Abu Bakr) vs hereditary imamate (Ali); Karbala 680; caliphate abolished 1924.

III. Conclusion

The sources explain the schools: divergent hadith, ijma and qiyas practices produced divergent doctrine — visible across marriage (witnesses), divorce (bidaat), and dower (minima) — before modern legislation and the Constitution overlay them.

10 marks · asked twice (Q8a; Q8b) — bridge
"Validity of child marriage under the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006."
Model answer

Traditional Muslim-law position: a minor may be given in marriage by a guardian (jabr); the marriage is valid but voidable — the option of puberty: repudiation after 15 and before 18, marriage not consummated (s. 2(vii) DMMA; Ghulam Sakina: consummation must be proved as a fact; minority cohabitation does not destroy the option; Atika Begum PC — majority at 15 or earlier puberty). Restraint statute: the Child Marriage Restraint Act, 1929 (s. 6 guardianship proviso noted in your materials) → PCMA 2006.

PCMA 2006 scheme: child = male under 21 / female under 18 (s. 2); child marriages are voidable at the option of the contracting child who was a minor — petition within 2 years of attaining majority (s. 3); the option belongs to the female alone where the male was over 18 at marriage; decree consequences — maintenance and residence orders until remarriage; s. 3(g) — children born are legitimate regardless; offences — s. 9 (adult male marrying a child: rigorous imprisonment up to 2 years + fine up to Rs 1 lakh), s. 10 (solemnisation), s. 11 (parents/guardians); courts may pass interim orders (s. 13); marriages mass-produced on Akha Teej etc. can be restrained (s. 13).

Convergence: under both systems the child marriage is not void but voidable at the minor's instance — Muslim law through the DMMA 15/18 option, Hindu/PCMA through the majority+2 formulation; POCSO/FCRA overlays criminal liability irrespective of personal-law validity (Khaledur Rahman v. State of Kerala, 2023 — POCSO prevails where a party is a minor). Conclusion: personal-law validity and child-protection statutes now run on parallel tracks; the minor's consent — always the core of nikah (Ghulam Kubra Bibi) — is statutorily deferred to majority.
