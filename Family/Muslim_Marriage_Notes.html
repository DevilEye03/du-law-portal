UNIT VI — SOURCES AND SCHOOLS OF MUSLIM LAW

Before studying the rules of Nikah, one must understand where Muslim law comes from and which school governs the parties — because the validity, classification and incidents of a Muslim marriage differ sharply between the Sunni (Hanafi) and Shia (Ithna Ashari) schools.

1. Who is a Muslim? & the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937

A Muslim is a person whose faith is in Islam. Islam literally means “submission to the will of God” (and also signifies the establishment of peace). In Islam, Allah is One and Muhammad is His Messenger. Thus, any person who believes in (i) the unity of God (Tauheed) and (ii) the prophethood of Muhammad (Risalat) is a Muslim. Conversion requires no elaborate ceremony — a profession of faith before competent witnesses suffices.

Shariat Act, 1937 — s. 2
Application of Muslim personal law is regulated by the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937. Section 2 provides that where both parties are Muslims, the rule of decision shall be Muslim personal law in matters of:
(i) intestate succession (inheritance);
(ii) special property of females;
(iii) marriage;
(iv) dissolution of marriage;
(v) maintenance;
(vi) dower;
(vii) guardianship;
(viii) gifts;
(ix) trust & trust properties;
(x) wakf.

The Act does not apply to adoption, wills and legacies; and it does not cover agricultural land, testamentary succession and charities other than wakf (except as notified by States). It is an “important and far-reaching enactment” applying to the entire Muslim community regardless of school.

Note: the Indian Majority Act, 1875 does not apply to marriage, dower and divorce — for these, Muslim law fixes its own age (puberty, presumed at 15). Relevant to Ghulam Kubra Bibi

2. Historical backdrop — origin of the Shia–Sunni division

  • The Prophet Muhammad was born in 571 A.D. At 40, while meditating in the cave of Hira, he received the first revelation (Wahi). In 622 A.D. he migrated from Mecca to Medina (Hijrah — beginning of the Hijri era); his followers who migrated were the Muhajirun, the Medinan helpers the Ansar. He died in 632 A.D., the last political and religious head.
  • On his death the question of succession arose. The Prophet left no male heir. Two groups formed — essentially on a political basis:
SHIAS
“Shiat-i-Ali” — faction of Ali (cousin, son-in-law). Advocated succession/nobility of blood & spiritual leadership (Imamat). Backed by Fatima, the Prophet’s daughter.
↔
SUNNIS
Advocated election of the community’s elder. Backed by Ayesha, the Prophet’s youngest wife. Abu Bakr elected 1st Caliph.

Caliphs: (1) Abu Bakr — (2) Umar (introduced Diwan) — (3) Usman (codified the Quran) — (4) Ali. Ali’s sons Hasan and then Hussain (killed at Karbala, 680 A.D., commemorated in Muharram) deepened the split. The Umayyads (Damascus) and Abbasids (Baghdad) followed; the Caliphate was abolished in 1924 at Ankara.

4. Schools of Muslim Law

Schools emerged from differences in interpreting the texts. Shias reject traditions handed down by anyone other than Ali; Sunnis accept traditions, decisions of Imams and the general body of juristic knowledge supplementing the Quran. (A third defector group, the Motazila, founded by Ata-al-Ghazzal in the 9th century, associates with neither.)

Why the school matters for Nikah
Indian Muslims are predominantly Sunni Hanafi. The Hanafi school recognises a three-fold classification — valid (sahih), void (batil) and irregular (fasid) — and requires two witnesses. The Shia (Ithna Ashari) school recognises only two categories (valid or void), does not require witnesses, recognises Muta, and treats most Sunni-“irregular” marriages as void. A wife does not change her school on marriage (Mulla §32).

UNIT VII — MARRIAGE (NIKAH) UNDER MUSLIM LAW

“Nikah – Solemnisation of Marriage — conditions for validity, classification and types; Dower.”

1. Nature and Object of Muslim Marriage (Nikah)

نکاح

Marriage (Nikah) is defined by Mulla (§250) as “a contract which has for its object the procreation and the legalising of children.” The Hedaya describes Nikah as a contract for the legalisation of intercourse and the procreation of children.

Civil-contract view

Justice Mahmood observed that Muslim marriage is not a sacrament but purely a civil contract. All rights and obligations it creates arise immediately and are not dependent on any condition precedent such as payment of dower. In Abdul Kadir v. Salima he likened it (by analogy) to a contract of sale, with dower as the “price”.

Religious / social view

Abdur Rahim called marriage part Ibadat (devotional act/worship) and part Muamlat (dealings among men). It is the Sunnah of the Prophet — “Marriage is my Sunnah; whosoever keeps away from it is not from me.” It confers dignified status on woman and legalises the sexual relationship and generations.

Modern synthesis
The 20th-edition note in Mulla states the institution comprises both worship (ibadat) and worldly affairs (muamalat): it is a permanent and unconditional civil contract (unlike Hindu marriage, a sacrament) requiring free consent of both parties, yet once accomplished the relationship is determined “in combination with the religious connotation”. In Pakistan, S.A. Rahman J. in Khurshid Bibi v. Mohd Amin held marriage is “in the nature of a civil contract”, with spiritual and moral overtones.

Object is two-fold: (1) legalisation of sexual relationship between husband and wife; (2) legalisation/procreation of children (legitimate generation). Because it is a contract, no religious ceremony as such is essential — only the conditions of a valid marriage contract must be fulfilled. Sexual intercourse not permitted by Muslim law is Zina (fornication/adultery); its offspring is illegitimate and cannot be legitimated by acknowledgment.

2. Essentials / Conditions for Validity of a Muslim Marriage

Mulla §252 lays down the core rule:

“It is essential to the validity of a marriage that there should be a proposal made by or on behalf of one of the parties, and an acceptance of the proposal by or on behalf of the other, in the presence and hearing of two male or one male and two female witnesses, who must be sane and adult Mahomedans. The proposal and acceptance must both be expressed at one meeting… Neither writing nor any religious ceremony is essential.”

2(a). Capacity to contract marriage

  • Every Muslim of sound mind who has attained puberty may enter into a marriage contract (Mulla §251). A Muslim girl of 15 who has attained puberty is competent to marry without her parents’ consent.
  • Puberty is presumed, in the absence of evidence, on completion of 15 years (Explanation to §251). The Privy Council in Atika Begum v. Mohammed Ibrahim laid down that a girl becomes major on either (i) completion of her 15th year, or (ii) attainment of puberty at an earlier period. (The Hedaya notes the earliest period as 12 for a boy and 9 for a girl.)
  • Minors and lunatics who have not attained puberty may be validly contracted in marriage by their guardians (§251(2); see jabar below).
  • A marriage of a sane, adult Muslim brought about without his/her consent is void (§251(3)).
Force / fraud on consent
Consent obtained by force or fraud makes the marriage invalid unless ratified. Where consent was never obtained, even consummation against the woman’s will does not validate the marriage. Hanafi exception: under all schools a coerced marriage is void except the Hanafi school, where (as to a contract entered by the person themselves) it is treated as valid/ratifiable — but where consent is wholly absent, consummation cannot validate.

2(b). Consent of the parties

  • Consent must be free. If parties are sane and adult, their own consent is required; if minors, the guardian’s consent is required (jabar).
  • Under Hanafi law a woman may give consent with or without a wali (guardian). Under the Shafei & Maliki schools an adult virgin’s consent must be communicated through a wali; the wali’s authority emanates from the woman herself.

2(c). Proposal and Acceptance — Ijab-wa-Qabul & witnesses

  • There must be a proposal (ijab) and acceptance (qabul), oral or in writing (Nikahnama/Kabinnama). Usual form: proposal “I have married myself to you”; acceptance “I have consented”.
  • Both must be expressed at one and the same meeting (majlis). A proposal at one meeting and acceptance at another = no valid marriage.
  • Words must be unambiguous and made by the parties or their agents. A mere promise/assurance to marry in future is not a proposal.
  • Witnesses (Sunni/Hanafi): the declaration and acceptance must be heard by two male, or one male and two female, sane, adult Muslim witnesses. A marriage without witnesses is irregular (fasid), not void (§254).
  • Shia law: presence of witnesses is not necessary — a marriage without witnesses is valid.
  • Neither writing nor any religious ceremony is essential; registration is not a requisite of validity (though the Kazi’s Act, 1880 and local Acts regulate Kazis; registration by Jamath may harden into custom but cannot be made mandatory).
The purdah procedure (as described in Ghulam Kubra Bibi)
Where women observe pardah, a relation of the woman, accompanied by two witnesses, goes inside and asks the girl within the witnesses’ hearing whether she authorises him to agree to the marriage on her behalf for the dower offered, explaining the dower. On her “yes”, the three come out; the Mullah asks the bridegroom and then the girl’s agent whether each agrees; on both “yes” the Mullah reads the scriptures and the marriage is complete. Vague proof that “there were two witnesses” has no value — the whole procedure must be shown to have been gone through.

5. Effects / Incidents of a Valid (Sahih) Marriage

A valid marriage (Mulla §265) confers the following rights and obligations:

  1. Sexual intercourse becomes lawful; children born of the union are legitimate.
  2. The wife becomes entitled to her dower (Mahr).
  3. The wife becomes entitled to maintenance and residence in the husband’s house.
  4. Mutual rights of inheritance are established between the spouses.
  5. Prohibitions arising from affinity come into operation; and the bar of unlawful conjunction and of a 5th wife applies.
  6. The wife may not remarry after the husband’s death or divorce without observing iddat.
  7. The woman does not change her status/school on marriage; neither spouse acquires any interest in the other’s property merely by marriage. The husband imposes on the wife obligations of fidelity, obedience to reasonable orders, and admission to intercourse.
“Valid retirement” — Khilwat-us-Sahiha
When husband and wife are alone together with no legal, moral or physical impediment to intercourse, it is valid retirement. In Sunni law it has the same effect as actual consummation as regards dower, paternity, iddat, the wife’s-sister bar and the fifth-wife bar — but not as to the bar on the wife’s daughter (affinity) or remarriage between divorcees (which require actual consummation). Shia law does not equate valid retirement with consummation.

6. Muta (Temporary) Marriage

Muta means “enjoyment”. It is a temporary union of a man and a woman for a fixed period in consideration of dower. It is recognised only by the Shia (Ithna Ashari) school; the Sunni law does not recognise Muta at all. It is almost obsolete in India.

Shia law & modern cases
Under Shia law only the father and paternal grandfather are recognised guardians. The Delhi HC in Mohd. Nihal v. State held a minor’s marriage without the wali’s consent (during the father’s lifetime, a brother-in-law acting) is completely void; once a girl attains majority she has the absolute right to contract marriage, which no one (even the father) may exercise. A minor given in marriage by father/grandfather has no option to repudiate unless the guardian acted fraudulently, negligently or to her manifest disadvantage (§272).

Option of puberty — Khyar-ul-Bulugh (§274)

Where a minor is contracted by any guardian other than father/father’s father, the minor has the option to repudiate the marriage on attaining puberty. A female loses the right if, after attaining puberty and being informed of the marriage and her right, she does not repudiate without unreasonable delay; a male loses it on express/implied ratification (payment of dower, cohabitation). The mere exercise of the option does not itself dissolve the marriage — repudiation must be confirmed by the Court (§275), though later/Lahore (Pakistan) views hold the option ends the marriage without a decree.

Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939 — s.2(vii)
The Act abolished restrictions on the option of puberty even where the marriage was arranged by father/grandfather. A wife is entitled to dissolution if she proves: (1) the marriage was not consummated; (2) it took place before she attained 15 years; and (3) she repudiated before attaining 18 years. (Provisions for lunatics are analogous, the option being exercised on recovery of reason — §276.)

8. Presumption of Marriage (Mulla §268)

In the absence of direct proof, marriage is presumed from:

  • (a) prolonged and continual cohabitation as husband and wife;
  • (b) the man’s acknowledgment of paternity of a child born to the woman (subject to §344 conditions); or
  • (c) the man’s acknowledgment of the woman as his wife.

The presumption is rebutted if conduct is inconsistent with the marital relation, or if the woman was admittedly a prostitute before being brought to the house (but merely not living behind purdah is insufficient). This presumption of fact (not juris et de jure) is central to Saiyid Rashid Ahmad v. Anisa Khatun — where a legal bar (triple talaq without intervening marriage) prevents remarriage, the acknowledgment cannot raise the presumption.

9. DOWER (MAHR / MEHAR)

مَہر

Mulla §285: “Mahr or dower is a sum of money or other property which the wife is entitled to receive from the husband in consideration of the marriage.” It is an essential feature of a valid Nikah and an obligation imposed upon the husband as a mark of respect to the wife. The word “consideration” is not used in the Contract Act sense — Mahmood J. in Abdul Kadir v. Salima explained that jurists analogise dower to the “price” in a sale only because sale is the typical contract; it is not bride-price (a post-nuptial agreement to pay dower is valid for want of Contract-Act consideration would otherwise be void). The wife may relinquish her dower voluntarily, but such relinquishment must be free from duress, fraud, misrepresentation, influence or mistake.

9(a). Specified dower — Mahr-i-musamma (§286)

  • The husband may settle any amount he likes, even beyond his means or leaving nothing for heirs; but he cannot settle less than 10 dirhams (≈ Rs. 3–4) under Sunni (Hanafi) law. Shia law: no fixed minimum.
  • Courts award the entire contracted sum (unless a statute says otherwise, e.g. Oudh Laws Act 1876 s.5 allows a “reasonable” sum with reference to husband’s means and wife’s status).
  • Excessiveness is no defence — high dower is deliberately used to restrain arbitrary divorce. Where two amounts exist (a private real one and an inflated public one for show), the private amount is allowed (doctrine of as-sunnat).
  • Specified dower is an incident of a valid marriage only. In an irregular marriage after consummation, specified or proper dower, whichever is less, is due — proper dower arises from cohabitation, not the contract itself.

9(b). Proper dower — Mahr-i-misl (§289)

If no dower is fixed, the wife is entitled to “proper” dower — even if the marriage was contracted on an express condition that she would claim none. Regard is had to the dower settled on other female members of her father’s family (e.g. father’s sisters), her family’s social position, the husband’s wealth/status, her personal qualifications, and the conditions of society. The Privy Council called dower “an essential incident under the Mussalman law to the status of marriage… when unspecified, the law declares it must be adjudged on definite principles.” Under Shia law proper dower must not exceed 500 dirhams.

9(c). Time of fixation & increase (§287–288)

Dower may be fixed before, at, or after marriage, and may be increased after marriage. A father may contract dower on behalf of his minor son and it binds the son; among Sunnis the father is not personally liable, but among Shias he is liable where the minor son has no means. A surety who guarantees dower is liable as such.

9(d). Confirmation of dower (§289A)

Dower becomes confirmed/vested by: (a) consummation; (b) valid retirement (khilwat-e-sahiha); or (c) death of either party. The whole dower is not payable merely on the contract; after valid retirement the whole becomes payable (Hanafi/ Maliki). Under Shia law dower is established by consummation or death, not by valid retirement.

9(f). Remission, suit & limitation (§291–292)

  • The wife may remit dower (or her heir, if she dies) — but remission must be by free consent. The maxim debitor non praesumitur donare (a debtor is not presumed to give) has no application between spouses; yet sums paid by husband to wife without proof of allocation to dower are not treated as satisfaction.
  • Limitation: a suit for prompt dower — 3 years from demand and refusal (both must be unambiguous) or from dissolution; for deferred dower — 3 years from dissolution by death/divorce. Where divorce is by writing, time runs from communication to the wife. Under the Limitation Act, 1963 s.29(3), suits under marriage/divorce law are not barred by the Limitation Act.

9(g). Liability of heirs; dower as debt; widow’s lien (§294–302)

  • Heirs are not personally liable. Each heir is liable for the dower debt only to the extent of his/her share of the estate (proportionate liability). Example: widow (1/8) liable Rs.400 of Rs.3,200; son (7/16) Rs.1,400; each daughter (7/32) Rs.700.
  • Dower ranks as an unsecured debt payable out of the husband’s estate on death, along with other creditors; the wife has no automatic charge, though a charge may be created by agreement or by a court decree (courts ordinarily pass a simple money decree).
  • Widow’s right of retention / “lien” for dower (§296): if the widow, lawfully and without force or fraud, with the express/implied consent of the husband or his heirs, obtains possession of the whole or part of his estate in satisfaction/security of dower, she may retain possession until her dower is satisfied. This is the only creditor’s lien of Muslim law recognised by British Indian courts and the Privy Council (Hamira Bibi v. Zubaida Bibi; Maina Bibi v. Chaudhri Vakil Ahmad).
Feature of widow’s retentionRule
Analogous to mortgage?No. Possession/right to retain is not conferred by agreement of the husband; it is conferred by law once possession is peaceably/lawfully acquired (Maina Bibi).
Gives title?No — she retains possession only; title remains in the heirs (including herself as an heir). If dispossessed she may sue for recovery.
During marriage?The right arises first on the husband’s death, or on divorce — not during the marriage. She cannot resist a purchaser in execution of a decree against the living husband.

Wife’s refusal where dower unpaid (§293)
The right to claim prompt dower precedes cohabitation. The wife may lawfully refuse conjugal society and sexual intercourse until prompt dower is paid; in a husband’s suit for restitution before intercourse, non-payment is a complete defence (suit dismissed); after free-consent intercourse, the proper decree is restitution conditional on payment of prompt dower.

CASE LAWS — FULL ANALYSIS (DU CASE MATERIAL, LB-105)

The three cases prescribed for Unit VII (pp. 223–236 of the DU Cases Selected and Edited, 2023). Each is analysed below on facts, issues, arguments of both sides, decision, and the principle evolved.

Case 1 · Solemnisation / Essentials (offer, acceptance, witnesses)

Ms. (Mt.) Ghulam Kubra Bibi v. Mohd. Shafi Mohd. Din

AIR 1940 Peshawar 2  ·  Mir Ahmad, J. (Court of the Judicial Commissioner, Peshawar)
Facts

Mohammad Shafi (husband/plaintiff) sued Mt. Ghulam Kubra (the woman/defendant) for restitution of conjugal rights. He also impleaded her parents and sought an injunction restraining them from interfering in his marital relations with his alleged wife.

Defence of Ghulam Kubra: she was never married to Mohammad Shafi. There was also a question whether the woman was of age (an adult) at the time of the alleged marriage. Evidence was led by both sides.

  • The Mullah who was said to have read the nikah appeared and stated that he read the nikah at the instance of the girl’s grandfather. He categorically denied that anyone was sent to the girl to enquire whether she agreed to the marriage.
  • One Mistri Abdul Karim vaguely deposed that “there were two witnesses of the nikah” but gave no names.
  • Two alleged witnesses — Mohammad Ramzan (admittedly the plaintiff’s neighbour) and Mohammad Din (who did not deny that the plaintiff had been working with him for 8–9 years) — merely said they were “witnesses of the nikah” and gave no detail of what they actually did.

The trial Judge held the girl was of age and the marriage was proved; he granted the decree for restitution and the injunction against all defendants. On appeal to the District Court, both parties admitted the girl was of age when married. The Additional District Judge maintained the restitution decree but set aside the injunction against the parents. Ghulam Kubra appealed further to the Peshawar Court against the restitution decree; Shafi cross-appealed to restore the injunction.

Issues
  1. Whether a valid Nikah was in fact solemnised between the parties — i.e., whether the essential formalities (proposal/acceptance at one meeting before two competent witnesses, with the adult bride’s own consent) were proved.
  2. Whether the vague evidence of witnesses (“we were the nikah witnesses”) suffices to prove the marriage, particularly where the person who performed the nikah swears no one consulted the bride.
  3. Whether the plaintiff was entitled to a decree for restitution of conjugal rights (and the ancillary injunction) if no valid marriage is proved.
Arguments of both sides
For the husband (Shafi)
  • A nikah was in fact performed; witnesses (Ramzan, Din) were produced to depose to it.
  • The girl was of age (admitted before the Additional District Judge); a valid marriage subsists and he is entitled to restitution of conjugal rights and to an injunction restraining the parents from interfering.
  • The trial court had accepted the marriage as proved, and the District Court maintained the restitution decree.
For the wife (Ghulam Kubra)
  • She was never married to Shafi at all.
  • The Mullah himself admitted he performed the nikah at the grandfather’s instance and that no one was sent to obtain the girl’s consent — the core requirement for an adult bride was absent.
  • The alleged witnesses were interested/vague (a neighbour and a long-time employer/associate of the plaintiff) and gave no particulars; bare statements that “there were two witnesses” have no evidentiary value and do not prove the prescribed procedure.
Decision & reasoning (Mir Ahmad, J.)

The Judge set out the standard Hanafi procedure for a nikah in a pardah society (set out in the note box below) and stressed that the law “absolutely” requires that the man (or someone on his behalf) and the woman (or someone on her behalf) agree to the marriage at one meeting, witnessed by two adult witnesses.

The valid pardah nikah procedure described by the court
A female relation, accompanied by two witnesses, goes inside to the bride and, within the witnesses’ hearing, asks whether she authorises him to agree to the marriage on her behalf for the dower offered, explaining the dower details. On her “yes” (or other sign of consent), the three come out. Before the Mullah, the bridegroom is asked whether he offers to marry her on the specified dower (“yes”); the girl’s agent then declares he is her duly authorised agent and accepts (“yes”); the witnesses are present so the Mullah can, if doubtful, question them whether the agent was really authorised. On both “yes” the Mullah reads the scriptures and the marriage is complete.

The court held the vague allegation that “there were two witnesses of the nikah” has no value; the whole procedure must be proved — especially when the man who read the nikah is positive that no one was sent to the girl to ask if she was a willing party.

On age: the record showed the girl was 17 when the marriage was solemnised. The parties mistakenly thought majority (for marriage) was 18 (the general law); under Mahomedan law a girl becomes major for marriage on attaining puberty, presumed at 15. Because they assumed she was still a minor, the grandfather gave her away without personally consulting her. The court clarified: where a girl is a minor, her father, grandfather or other paternal relation may give her away and the marriage is a valid nikah — but even such a nikah requires two adult witnesses, and the witnesses here may merely have witnessed the “giving away” by the grandfather rather than a valid contract. In any event, she was in fact adult (17), and her own consent/authorisation was essential and was not proved.

Result: “For the reasons given above I hold that no valid marriage has taken place in this case, and that the plaintiff has therefore no right to sue for restitution of conjugal rights.” The appeal of Mt. Ghulam Kubra was accepted, Shafi’s suit dismissed with costs throughout, and Shafi’s cross-appeal (to restore the injunction) was dismissed.

Principle evolved / proposition
  • Essentials of a Sunni nikah must be strictly proved: offer (ijab) and acceptance (qabul) at one meeting, by the parties or their duly authorised agents, with the bride’s real consent, in the presence and hearing of two sane, adult Muslim witnesses. Mere formal performance/readings by a Mullah, or a bare assertion that “two witnesses were present,” is insufficient — the substance of authorisation and witnessing must be established.
  • Majority for marriage = puberty (presumed 15), not 18; the Indian Majority Act, 1875 does not govern marriage. An adult woman must herself consent (or duly authorise an agent); she cannot be “given away” without consultation.
  • A minor’s marriage by a father/grandfather/guardian is valid — but still requires two adult witnesses; and interested/perfunctory witnesses do not discharge the burden.
  • No valid marriage → no right to restitution of conjugal rights and no injunction against the woman’s relations.
Case 2 · Classification — void (batil) vs. irregular (fasid); unlawful conjunction; s.125 CrPC

Chand Patel v. Bismillah Begum

1 (2008) DMC 588 (SC) · AIR 2008 SC 1915 · (2008) 4 SCC 774  ·  Altamas Kabir, J. (Supreme Court of India)
Facts

Bismillah Begum (respondent 1) filed an application under Section 125 CrPC for maintenance for herself and her minor daughter Taheman Bano (respondent 2) against Chand Patel (appellant/husband) in the Court of the Judicial Magistrate, First Class, Chincholi (Crl. Misc. No. 6 of 2001). She claimed she was the legally wedded wife, married about eight years earlier; the marriage was consummated and a daughter was born two years later (Taheman Bano, in the mother’s care).

Crucial admission: Bismillah categorically admitted that Chand Patel was already married to her elder sister, Mashaq Bee, and that with the consent of the first wife he married her (the younger sister); a Nikahnama was executed but had been misplaced. She also admitted he lived with both women under one roof and had accepted and brought up Taheman Bano as his daughter.

Relations later deteriorated; he began to neglect them. She sought maintenance @ Rs.1,000 p.m. for each. Chand Patel denied the marriage, sexual relations, and paternity. The Magistrate (prima facie accepting the marriage and paternity and finding neglect) ordered Rs.1,000 p.m. to the wife and to the daughter till adulthood. The Revisional Court (IV Additional District Judge, Gulbarga) dismissed both revisions, holding personal law cannot bar a Muslim from maintenance under s.125, the Magistrate cannot go into validity of marriage in s.125 proceedings, and the husband must maintain wife and child until the marriage is declared null and void by a competent court (relying on Nanak Chand v. Chandra Kishore Aggarwal, AIR 1970 SC 446, and Re Hussain Saheb). The Karnataka High Court (under s.482) found no merit and dismissed Chand Patel’s petition. He appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issue

“An interesting question of law”: whether a marriage performed by a Muslim with his wife’s sister while his earlier marriage (with the other sister) was still subsisting is void in law or merely irregular (or voidable) — even though the subsequent marriage may have been consummated — and, consequently, whether the wife and daughter are entitled to maintenance under s.125 CrPC until the marriage is declared void.

Arguments of both sides
For the husband (Chand Patel) — Mr. Raja Venkatappa Naik
  • Muslim law prohibits “unlawful conjunction” — a man may not marry his wife’s sister during the wife’s lifetime.
  • He denied the marriage and sexual relations and disputed paternity; such a union, even if performed, is void (batil) from its very inception and confers no rights on the woman or child.
  • Reliance on Rameshchandra Rampratapji Daga v. Rameshwari Daga (2005) 2 SCC 33 and Savitaben Somabhai Bhatiya v. State of Gujarat (2005) 3 SCC 636 — arguing that where the marriage itself is void ab initio, s.125 maintenance cannot be granted to a woman “not lawfully married.”
For the wife (Bismillah Begum) — Mrs. K. Sarada Devi
  • In a s.125 proceeding the court is not required to adjudicate the validity of the marriage; on a prima facie view it can order maintenance of both wife and daughter.
  • The marriage was solemnised with the facts known to both parties and with the first wife’s consent; the husband lived with both, acknowledged the child, and now resorts to a technicality to avoid his statutory duty.
  • Until the marriage is declared void by a competent court, it continues to subsist and all rights of a valid marriage (including maintenance for the wife and minor daughter) remain available.
Decision & reasoning (Altamas Kabir, J.)

Delay condoned. The answer turned on the legal status of the union: if void, no s.125 maintenance; if irregular, the marriage subsists for all purposes until declared void, and the wife and child are entitled to maintenance. The Court drew the analogy with ss.11 (void) and 12 (voidable) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 and Yamunabai v. Anantrao (AIR 1988 SC 644): s.11 marriages are void ipso jure from inception, whereas voidable marriages subsist until annulled by a decree.

Muslim law position (Mulla §§260–267): paragraphs 260–262 (consanguinity, affinity, fosterage) are complete/perpetual prohibitions → void (batil). But §263 “Unlawful conjunction” expressly provides: “A man may not have at the same time two wives who are so related… as for instance two sisters, or aunt and niece. The bar of unlawful conjunction renders a marriage irregular, not void.”

The Court then traced the case-law conflict:

  • Calcutta HC — Aizunnissa v. Karimunissa (1895) 23 Cal 130: marriage with a wife’s sister during the wife’s lifetime is void; children illegitimate, no inheritance.
  • Bombay HC — Tajbi Abalal Desai v. Mowla Alikhan Desai (1917) 39 IC: differed, and relying on the Fatawa-i-Alamgiri held such marriage is irregular (fasid), not void. A permanently prohibited woman’s marriage is void and has no legal consequence; but a temporarily prohibited woman’s marriage, if consummated, may have legal consequences — the bar can be removed by the death of the first wife or by divorce, making the second sister lawful. The Court noted the Fatawa-i-Alamgiri statement does not conflict with the Quran, harmonises with the law’s development, and has the support of Baillie, Ameer Ali, Tyabji and Abdur Rahim; only the single Calcutta judgment stood against it.
  • The Oudh Chief Court (Kaniza v. Hasan Ahmad Khan, 1925), Lahore HC (Taliamand v. Muhammad Din, 1930) and Madras HC (Rahiman Bibi v. Mahboob Bibi, 1937) all followed the Bombay view and held the Calcutta decision incorrect.

The Court quoted Mulla §264 (void vs. irregular — the five irregular categories including “a woman so related to the wife that if one had been male they could not intermarry”) and §267 (effects of fasid marriage: terminable by either party; no effect before consummation; after consummation — (i) wife gets dower, proper or specified whichever is less; (ii) iddat of three courses; (iii) issue is legitimate; but no mutual inheritance between spouses).

Held: “Since a marriage which is temporarily prohibited may be rendered lawful once the prohibition is removed, such a marriage is… irregular (fasid) and not void (batil).” The bar of unlawful conjunction (jama bain-al-mahramain) renders a marriage irregular and not void. Under Hanafi law (Indian Muslims), an irregular marriage continues to subsist till terminated in accordance with law, and the wife and children are entitled to maintenance under s.125 CrPC. As the marriage had not been declared void by any competent forum, it continued to subsist.

Result: appeal dismissed; the Karnataka High Court and Magistrate’s orders affirmed; interim stay vacated; Chand Patel directed to pay all arrears within six months and current maintenance from March 2008.

Principle evolved / significance
  • Supreme Court settled the law for India: marriage with a wife’s sister (or within the bar of unlawful conjunction) during the subsistence of the first marriage is irregular (fasid), not void (batil), under Hanafi Muslim law — approving the Bombay/Fatawa-i-Alamgiri view and rejecting the Calcutta Aizunnissa view.
  • The test: a perpetual/absolute prohibition (consanguinity, affinity, fosterage, polyandry) → void; a temporary/relative/accidental bar that can be removed (death/divorce of the obstacle wife, expiry of iddat, conversion, divorce of a fifth wife, re-attestation before witnesses) → irregular.
  • An irregular (consummated) marriage produces legal effects — legitimate children, dower (lesser amount), iddat — though no mutual inheritance between spouses.
  • Social-welfare dimension: a marriage merely irregular or voidable subsists until set aside/declared void by a competent court; hence the wife and children remain entitled to s.125 CrPC maintenance. A Magistrate in s.125 proceedings does not finally adjudicate marital validity, and personal law cannot override the statutory obligation of maintenance.
Case 3 · Talaq (forms) — validity of triple talaq given in jest/without intention; acknowledgment & presumption of marriage

Saiyid Rashid Ahmad v. Mt. Anisa Khatun (Anis Fatima)

AIR 1932 PC 25 · (1932) 59 IA 21 · 54 All 46  ·  Lord Thankerton (Privy Council)
Facts

Appeal from a decree of the Allahabad High Court (1 Feb 1927) which reversed a decree of the Subordinate Judge of Bijnor at Moradabad (15 Dec 1923). The dispute concerned succession to the estate of Ghiyas Uddin, a Mahomedan who died on 4 April 1920 leaving considerable moveable and immoveable property.

The appellants (plaintiffs) were a brother and sister of Ghiyas Uddin who, with pro forma defendants (his other relations), would be his heirs under Mahomedan law unless respondents 1–6 established their claim to be his widow and legitimate children. The controversy turned on four stages in the matrimonial history of Anis Fatima (respondent 1):

  1. Her marriage to Manzur Husain in 1901;
  2. Her alleged divorce by Manzur Husain in early 1905;
  3. Her marriage to Ghiyas Uddin on 28 Aug 1905; and
  4. Her alleged divorce by Ghiyas Uddin on/about 13 Sept 1905.
  • Stage 1–2: The 1901 marriage was admitted; respondents argued it was invalid because both parties were minors, but the Subordinate Judge (and High Court) held it valid — Anis Fatima was then adult, and Manzur’s marriage was contracted through his mother/guardian. As to Manzur’s alleged 1905 divorce: Manzur was the sole witness; the Subordinate Judge rejected his evidence and held he had not reached the age of discretion; the High Court accepted the divorce and found he was of age.
  • Stage 3: The Subordinate Judge held the Ghiyas Uddin marriage not proved; the High Court reversed (finding it proved), and the appellants acquiesced in that finding — merely contending the marriage was invalid if she were still Manzur’s undivorced wife.
  • Stage 4 (decisive): The appellants’ case was that on 13 Sept 1905 Ghiyas Uddin pronounced the triple talaq in the presence of witnesses (though in the wife’s absence); she received Rs.1,000 towards her dower the same day (a registered receipt was produced), and a talaknama (deed of divorce) dated 17 Sept 1905 narrated the divorce. Respondents denied the divorce and challenged its validity, contending the Rs.1,000 was prompt dower and the deed was not genuine/written by him. Both lower courts concurrently found Ghiyas Uddin did pronounce the triple talaq and the deed was genuine.

The Subordinate Judge held Ghiyas Uddin irrevocably divorced Anis Fatima — she was not his wife at his death in 1920, and the children (admittedly born after the divorce date) were not legitimate. The High Court reversed, holding the divorce was fictitious and inoperative — a mock ceremony performed to satisfy Ghiyas Uddin’s father, without any real intention that it take effect.

Issues
  1. Whether the triple talaq pronounced by Ghiyas Uddin in September 1905 constituted an immediately effective, irrevocable divorce (despite being pronounced in the wife’s absence and alleged to be a mere mock/without genuine intention).
  2. Whether a talaq pronounced in jest or under compulsion, or without mental intention to make it real, is valid under Sunni (Hanafi) law.
  3. Whether, after triple talaq, the husband’s subsequent cohabitation/acknowledgment of the children as legitimate for ~15 years could revive the marriage or confer legitimacy — and whether the presumption of marriage from acknowledgment could be raised despite the legal bar (that after triple talaq the parties cannot remarry until she marries another who then divorces her/dies — halala).
Arguments of both sides
For the appellants (husband’s brother & sister)
  • The triple talaq was in fact pronounced on 13 Sept 1905 (concurrent findings), Rs.1,000 dower was paid against a registered receipt, and a genuine talaknama was executed.
  • A triple talaq in the bidaat form operates as an immediate and irrevocable dissolution irrespective of iddat, intention or the wife’s presence.
  • Once the divorce is proved, later cohabitation/acknowledgment cannot undo it; children born after the divorce are illegitimate; the widow and children take no share, so the estate devolves on the husband’s siblings.
For the respondents (claimed widow & children)
  • The divorce was a fictitious mock ceremony performed only to satisfy Ghiyas Uddin’s father, without intention that it be real — hence inoperative (the High Court accepted this).
  • For about 15 years after the alleged divorce, Ghiyas Uddin treated Anis Fatima as his wife and the children as legitimate; the husband’s brothers also admitted their status.
  • The acknowledgment of legitimacy raised a presumption of marriage (relying on the Board’s earlier decision in Habibur Rahman Chowdhury v. Altaf Ali Chowdhury, AIR 1922 PC 159) — i.e., that she must in the interval have married another who died/divorced her and then remarried Ghiyas Uddin — and the burden lay on the appellants to displace that presumption.
Decision & reasoning (Lord Thankerton)

Counsel were asked to argue Stage 4 first, since it could dispose of the appeal. Their Lordships held the Subordinate Judge was right and dealt with that stage alone.

The parties were Sunni Hanafi. Applying Sir R.K. Wilson’s Digest of Anglo-Mahomedan Law (5th edn., p.136), talaq may be irrevocable (bain) or revocable (raja). A talaq bain effects an immediate and complete dissolution and may be pronounced by clear words addressed to the wife: (a) once followed by abstinence during iddat — ahsan (best); (b) three times during successive intervals of purity — hasan (good); (c) three times at shorter intervals or in immediate succession; or (d) once in words clearly intending immediate irrevocability — (c) and (d) are bidaat (sinful) but legally valid among Sunni lawyers.

The words used
Ghiyas Uddin repeated three times (even though the wife was absent): “I divorce Anisa Khatun for ever and render her haram for me.” These words clearly showed an intention to dissolve the marriage — the fourth (bidaat) form. The talaknama confirmed the three divorces were given “in the abominable form” (bidaat). The High Court had erred in treating it as ahsan rather than bidaat. The case was distinguished from Farzund Hossein v. Janu Bibee (1878) 4 Cal 588 because here the talaq was addressed to the wife by name.

Legal effect: In the bidaat form the divorce becomes irrevocable at once, irrespective of iddat (Baillie’s Digest, 2nd edn., p.206). It is not necessary that the wife be present when talaq is pronounced (her right to alimony may continue only until she is informed). Crucially:

“A talak actually pronounced under compulsion or in jest is valid and effective.” The validity and effectiveness of the divorce would not be affected by Ghiyas Uddin’s mental intention that it should not be a genuine divorce, as such a view is contrary to all authority.

On the presumption from acknowledgment: the Board found it difficult to treat the contention as serious, because the Habibur Rahman dicta directly negative it. Acknowledgment of a son as legitimate raises a presumption of marriage only if it is not impossible on its face — e.g., not where the mother is the wife of another or within prohibited degrees, which would make the issue the offspring of adultery/incest. Being a presumption of fact (not juris et de jure), it can be rebutted by contrary proof. Here the legal bar to remarriage created by the triple talaq (she could not lawfully remarry Ghiyas Uddin until she had married another and that husband had died or divorced her) equally prevented the presumption from being raised. Respondents would have had to prove the removal of that bar — an intervening marriage to another and its termination before the children’s birth and acknowledgment — which they did not. Later cohabitation and treatment for 15 years could not undo an established divorce or confer the claimed status.

Result: the appeal was allowed; the High Court’s decree reversed and the Subordinate Judge’s decree restored — i.e., the triple talaq was valid and effective; Anis Fatima was not Ghiyas Uddin’s widow and the children born after the divorce were not his legitimate heirs; the estate devolved on his siblings.

Principle evolved / significance
  • Forms of talaq recognised under Hanafi law: ahsan and hasan (approved) and the triple/immediate bidaat forms — the latter are theologically sinful but legally valid and operate as immediate, irrevocable dissolution, independent of iddat and of the wife’s presence.
  • Subjective intention is irrelevant to a talaq once formally pronounced: a talaq uttered under compulsion, in jest, or in anger — or as a mere “mock” ceremony — is valid and effective if the words clearly import divorce. (This rule is consistent with the Kerala HC observations in Mohammed Haneefa v. Pathummal Beevi and Pathayi v. Moideen, but was later constitutionally re-examined in Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017), where the Supreme Court struck down instant triple talaq (talaq-ul-biddat) as unconstitutional, now codified in the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019.)
  • Presumption of marriage/legitimacy from acknowledgment has limits: it is a rebuttable presumption of fact and cannot be raised where a legal bar (such as the triple-talaq bar to immediate remarriage, or prohibited degrees) makes the alleged marriage impossible; the party relying on it must plead and prove the removal of the bar (an intervening valid marriage).
  • After an irrevocable triple talaq, subsequent cohabitation and acknowledgment cannot revive the dissolved marriage or legitimate children born after the divorce absent a lawful halala marriage.

Cross-reference (DU Unit VIII): the strict common-law view that a bald plea of divorce in pleadings/affidavit could itself operate as divorce (Mulla §310; A.P. HC in (1975) 1 APLJ 20) was later rejected by the Supreme Court in Shamim Ara v. State of U.P., 2002 Cr LJ 4726 (SC), which required reasonable cause and proof of a valid talaq and its communication; the present case (Rashid Ahmad) remains authority for the proposition that where a talaq is in fact proved and the words clearly express divorce, the husband’s secret lack of intention does not save the marriage.

Q.1 — Long Answer (20 marks)
“Muslim marriage is a civil contract rather than a sacrament.” Discuss the nature of Nikah and explain in detail the essential conditions of a valid Muslim marriage.
Answer plan: Intro (definition & nature — contract vs. ibadat/muamlat) → essentials: (1) capacity/puberty, (2) free consent, (3) ijab-wa-qabul + witnesses, (4) no impediments, (5) dower → no ceremony/registration required → case law: Ghulam Kubra Bibi → conclusion.
Introduction & nature

Marriage (Nikah) is defined by Mulla (§250) as “a contract which has for its object the procreation and the legalising of children.” Justice Mahmood observed it is not a sacrament but a civil contract whose rights and obligations arise immediately and do not depend on payment of dower. Yet Abdur Rahim and the modern view treat it as a blend of ibadat (worship) and muamlat (worldly dealings) — it is the Sunnah of the Prophet and also a contract requiring free consent. Its objects are to legalise (i) the sexual relationship and (ii) procreation/legitimisation of children; intercourse outside marriage is zina.

Essential conditions of validity
  1. Capacity (Mulla §251): every Muslim of sound mind who has attained puberty may contract marriage. Puberty is presumed at 15 (Atika Begum v. Mohammed Ibrahim); the Indian Majority Act, 1875 does not apply to marriage, dower and divorce. Minors/lunatics may be married by guardians (jabar).
  2. Free consent: consent must not be induced by force, fraud or compulsion; a sane adult’s marriage without consent is void, and consummation against the woman’s will does not validate it. (The Hanafi school treats a coerced contract as ratifiable, but absent real consent it is not binding; Shafei/Maliki require the adult bride’s consent through a wali.)
  3. Proposal and acceptance (ijab-wa-qabul) — §252: a proposal by/on behalf of one party and acceptance by/on behalf of the other, both at one meeting (one majlis), in clear, unambiguous words (oral or written). A future promise to marry is not a proposal. In Sunni law it must be in the presence and hearing of two male, or one male and two female, sane, adult Muslim witnesses; absence of witnesses makes the marriage irregular (fasid), not void. Under Shia law witnesses are not required.
  4. No legal impediment: no absolute bar (consanguinity, affinity, fosterage, polyandry → void) or uncured relative bar (unlawful conjunction, fifth wife, iddat, religion, no witnesses → irregular).
  5. Dower (Mahr): an obligation on the husband as a mark of respect to the wife; it is not a Contract-Act “consideration” and the marriage is valid even if dower is fixed later or not at all (proper dower then applies).

Neither writing nor any religious ceremony is essential, and registration is not a condition of validity (though it may be proved by Nikahnama, cohabitation or acknowledgment).

Case law — Ms. Ghulam Kubra Bibi v. Mohd. Shafi Mohd. Din, AIR 1940 Pesh. 2

The husband sued for restitution of conjugal rights. The Mullah admitted he read the nikah at the grandfather’s instance and that no one was sent to consult the bride; the alleged witnesses merely said they “were the nikah witnesses” without particulars and were interested (a neighbour and a long-time associate). Mir Ahmad J. described the proper pardah procedure (an agent + two witnesses obtaining the adult bride’s authorisation within the witnesses’ hearing, then offer/acceptance before the Mullah) and held the vague assertion of “two witnesses” had no value. Although the girl was 17 (an adult — majority for marriage being puberty/presumed 15), her own consent/authorisation was never proved, and even a guardian-given minor’s nikah requires two adult witnesses. No valid marriage was proved; the restitution suit was dismissed with costs. The decision shows that the essentials must be strictly proved — formal performance by a Qazi is not enough.

Conclusion

Nikah is thus a consensual civil contract with religious and social overtones. Where capacity, free consent, offer/acceptance in one meeting with competent witnesses (Sunni), absence of impediments and dower concur, the marriage is sahih and produces full legal effects; a defect going to an absolute bar makes it batil, while a curable formal defect makes it fasid.

Q.2 — Long Answer (20 marks) / Problem
Distinguish between a void (batil) and an irregular (fasid) marriage under Muslim law. “A man may not marry two sisters at the same time.” Explain with the help of case law whether such a marriage is void or irregular.
Answer plan: Three-fold classification (sahih/batil/fasid; Shia has only two) → definition of batil (perpetual bar: consanguinity, affinity, fosterage, polyandry) → definition of fasid (temporary/curable bar: no witnesses, fifth wife, iddat, religion, unlawful conjunction) → effects table → Chand Patel (wife’s sister = irregular) → conflict of High Courts resolved by SC → conclusion.
Classification (Mulla §253, §264)

A Muslim marriage is valid (sahih), void (batil), or irregular (fasid). A void marriage is “unlawful in itself, the prohibition being perpetual and absolute” — e.g. consanguinity (§260), affinity (§261), fosterage (§262), and polyandry (§256). It is no marriage at all, creates no civil rights, and the offspring are illegitimate (§266). An irregular marriage is “not unlawful in itself, but unlawful for something else”, where the prohibition is temporary or relative or the irregularity is accidental — (a) without witnesses; (b) fifth wife; (c) during iddat; (d) difference of religion; (e) unlawful conjunction. It is curable and, once consummated, yields limited legal effects (§267): the wife gets dower (proper or specified, whichever is less), iddat of three courses, and the children are legitimate; but no mutual inheritance arises between spouses. Either party may terminate it by words showing intention to separate.

Unlawful conjunction & two sisters — Chand Patel v. Bismillah Begum, (2008) 4 SCC 774

§263 (Unlawful conjunction): a man may not simultaneously have two wives so related by consanguinity/affinity/fosterage that if one were male they could not intermarry — e.g. two sisters, or aunt and niece. “The bar of unlawful conjunction renders a marriage irregular, not void.” In Chand Patel, a Muslim married his wife’s younger sister during the subsistence of the first marriage (with the first wife’s consent), lived with both, and had a daughter. When he denied maintenance, the Supreme Court (Altamas Kabir J.) resolved the conflict: the Calcutta HC in Aizunnissa v. Karimunissa (1895) had held such marriage void and the issue illegitimate; but the Bombay HC in Tajbi Abalal Desai (1917), relying on the Fatawa-i-Alamgiri and supported by Baillie, Ameer Ali, Tyabji and Abdur Rahim, held it irregular (fasid) — the bar is temporary because it can be removed by the death or divorce of the first wife. The Oudh, Lahore and Madras High Courts agreed with Bombay.

Held: “Since a marriage which is temporarily prohibited may be rendered lawful once the prohibition is removed, such a marriage is irregular (fasid) and not void (batil).” An irregular marriage continues to subsist until declared void by a competent court; hence the wife and daughter were entitled to maintenance under s.125 CrPC.

Conclusion

The decisive test is whether the prohibition is perpetual/incurable (void) or temporary/curable (irregular). Marriage with two sisters at once is within the bar of unlawful conjunction and is, for Hanafi Muslims in India, irregular — not void; it becomes lawful if the obstacle wife dies or is divorced, and until set aside it supports maintenance and legitimacy of children.

Q.3 — Long Answer (20 marks)
Define Dower (Mahr). Discuss its different kinds and explain the widow’s right to retain possession of her husband’s estate in lieu of dower.
Answer plan: Definition (§285) — not Contract-Act consideration → kinds: specified vs proper; prompt vs deferred; (confirmation §289A) → amount (10 dirhams; no Shia minimum) → prompt dower as defence to restitution (§293) → dower as unsecured debt; heirs’ proportionate liability → widow’s right of retention/“lien” (§296–302; Hamira Bibi; Maina Bibi) → conclusion.
Definition

Mulla §285: “Mahr or dower is a sum of money or other property which the wife is entitled to receive from the husband in consideration of the marriage.” It is an obligation imposed on the husband as a mark of respect; “consideration” is not used in the Contract Act sense (Mahmood J., Abdul Kadir v. Salima). It is not bride-price — a post-nuptial dower agreement is valid — and the wife may voluntarily relinquish it with free consent. High dower often restrains arbitrary divorce; excessiveness is no defence.

Kinds of dower
  • Specified dower (Mahr-i-musamma, §286): the amount settled by the parties (before/at/after marriage, §287); may be any sum but not less than 10 dirhams (Sunni; no minimum in Shia). The court awards the whole contracted sum. A father may contract it for his minor son (binding on the son; §288).
  • Proper/customary dower (Mahr-i-misl, §289): payable if none is fixed — even on an express stipulation not to claim — fixed by reference to the dower of the female relations of the wife’s father’s family, her status, the husband’s means, etc. (Shia cap: 500 dirhams).
  • Prompt dower (Mu’ajjal, §290): payable on demand, before or after consummation. The wife may refuse cohabitation/conjugal society until it is paid; in a suit for restitution before intercourse non-payment is a complete defence, and after free-consent intercourse the decree is restitution conditional on payment (§293).
  • Deferred dower (Mu’wajjal): payable on dissolution by death or divorce. If the allocation is unspecified, Shia law treats the whole as prompt; Sunni law divides it (custom/status), while the Madras view presumes the whole prompt.

Confirmation (§289A): dower vests on consummation, valid retirement (khilwat-e-sahiha), or death of either party (Shia: consummation or death, not valid retirement).

Widow’s right of retention / “lien” (§296–302)

Dower ranks as an unsecured debt payable out of the husband’s estate; the wife has no automatic charge (though one may be created by agreement/decree), and heirs are liable only rateably to the extent of their shares (§294–295). However, if the widow lawfully and without force or fraud, with the express/implied consent of the husband or his heirs, obtains possession of his estate to satisfy her dower, she may retain possession until the dower is paid. In Hamira Bibi v. Zubaida Bibi the Privy Council recognised this as the only creditor’s lien of Muslim law. Characteristics: (i) it is not analogous to a mortgage — possession is not conferred by agreement but by law once peaceably acquired (Maina Bibi v. Chaudhri Vakil Ahmad); (ii) it gives no title and no right to alienate (an alienation binds only her own share); (iii) it arises only on death or divorce, not during the marriage; (iv) she must account for rents/profits but is allowed interest as compensation; and (v) if she gives up possession she loses the right to retain.

Conclusion

Dower is the wife’s secured-in-equity financial safeguard — a debt she can claim promptly or on dissolution, and which (with lawful possession) underpins a unique right to retain the husband’s estate until satisfied.

Q.4 — Short Note (10 marks)
Write a short note on Muta marriage.

Muta (literally “enjoyment”) is a temporary marriage for a fixed period in consideration of dower, recognised only by the Shia (Ithna Ashari) school; Sunni law does not recognise it (Mulla §269). It is virtually obsolete in India.

Essentials: (1) parties must have attained puberty and be of sound mind; (2) free consent; (3) the ordinary marriage formalities; (4) parties not within prohibited degrees; (5) declaration and acceptance; (6) a fixed term (a day to years) and (7) a specified dower — both are mandatory; a fixed term with no dower is void, while specified dower with no fixed term operates as a permanent marriage. A Shia male may contract Muta with a Muslim, Christian, Jewess or even a fire-worshipper (but no other faith), and with any number; a Shia female may not contract Muta with a non-Muslim.

Incidents: it is dissolved ipso facto on expiry of the term; there is no divorce, though the husband may end it early by hiba-i-muddat (“gift of the term”) without the wife’s consent. There are no mutual inheritance rights between the spouses, but children conceived during the term are legitimate and inherit from both parents (cohabitation beyond the term raises an inference of extension). Consummated Muta gives full dower; unconsummated gives half; the wife leaving early suffers proportionate deduction. Iddat is two courses (four months and ten days on the husband’s death). A Muta wife gets no maintenance under Shia personal law but may claim under s.125 CrPC.

Q.5 — Short Note / Problem (10 marks)
Write a note on guardianship in marriage (jabar) and the “option of puberty” (khyar-ul-bulugh). How is a minor’s marriage affected under the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939?

A minor (one who has not attained puberty) cannot contract marriage personally but may be contracted by a guardian (wali). The order of guardianship (Mulla §271) is: father → paternal grandfather (however high) → brother and other male paternal relations (in order of residuaries) → mother, maternal uncle/aunt and other maternal relations → the ruling authority/Qazi. Shia law recognises only the father and paternal grandfather.

A marriage contracted by the father or paternal grandfather is valid and binding and cannot be repudiated on puberty unless the guardian acted fraudulently, negligently or to the minor’s manifest disadvantage (§272). Where the marriage is brought about by any other guardian, the minor has the option of puberty (khyar-ul-bulugh) to repudiate on attaining puberty (§274); a female loses it by unreasonable delay after being informed, a male by express/implied ratification (dower payment, cohabitation). Repudiation must ordinarily be confirmed by a court (§275), and consummation does not validate a void marriage.

DMMA, 1939, s.2(vii): the Act removed restrictions on the option even where the father/grandfather arranged the marriage. A wife may obtain dissolution on proving (1) the marriage was not consummated; (2) it took place before she attained 15 years; and (3) she repudiated it before attaining 18 years. Lunatics’ marriages are analogous, the option arising on recovery of reason (§276).

Q.6 — Problem Questions (with model solutions)
Attempt the following problems, citing the DU-prescribed cases.
Problem (a)

A, a Muslim male, sues B for restitution of conjugal rights. The Qazi admits he performed the nikah at the instance of B’s grandfather and that no one was sent to obtain B’s consent; the two “witnesses” merely state they attended, give no details, and are A’s neighbour and employee. B was 17 at the time. Decide.

Solution: Following Ghulam Kubra Bibi v. Mohd. Shafi (AIR 1940 Pesh 2), no valid marriage is proved. B, being 17, had attained majority for marriage (puberty, presumed 15; Majority Act inapplicable), so her own consent/authorisation was essential; the Qazi’s admission that no one consulted her is fatal, and the vague, interested witness evidence does not prove the required offer/acceptance before two competent witnesses at one meeting. Even a guardian-given minor’s nikah requires two adult witnesses. The restitution suit must be dismissed.

Problem (b)

A Muslim male, already married to X, marries X’s younger sister Y with X’s consent, lives with both and acknowledges a daughter by Y. On neglect, Y claims maintenance under s.125 CrPC. The husband argues the marriage is void. Decide.

Solution: This is the bar of unlawful conjunction (Mulla §263). Per Chand Patel v. Bismillah Begum (2008), marriage with a wife’s sister during the first wife’s lifetime is irregular (fasid), not void (batil), because the bar is temporary (it ends on X’s death or divorce) and is curable. The marriage was consummated and the child acknowledged; an irregular marriage subsists until declared void by a competent court. Y and the daughter are entitled to s.125 CrPC maintenance; the Magistrate does not finally adjudicate validity in maintenance proceedings.

Problem (c)

H pronounces triple talaq on his wife W in the presence of witnesses but without informing her, later saying it was only a jest to pacify his father, and continues to live with W and treats children born thereafter as legitimate for 15 years. On H’s death, W and the children claim inheritance. Decide.

Solution: On the classical Hanafi law applied in Saiyid Rashid Ahmad v. Mt. Anisa Khatun (AIR 1932 PC 25), a triple talaq in the bidaat form — words such as “I divorce you for ever and render you haram” — is an immediate, irrevocable divorce, effective even in the wife’s absence and even if pronounced in jest, under compulsion, or without genuine intention. Subsequent cohabitation and acknowledgment cannot revive the marriage, and the presumption of marriage from acknowledgment cannot arise because of the legal bar (after triple talaq she cannot remarry him without an intervening halala marriage, which was not proved). Children born after the divorce are not legitimate heirs. [Note: the position is now materially affected by Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017) and the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019, which make instant triple talaq illegal and void; and Shamim Ara (2002) requires proof of a valid talaq for its communication to be effective. Students should contrast the classical rule (Rashid Ahmad) with the current law.]

Q.7 — Short-answer / viva bank
Quick model points for frequent short questions.
Sources of Muslim law

Primary: Quran (revealed word of God; ~200 legal verses), Sunnah/Hadith (Prophet’s sayings/doings/tacit approvals), Ijma (consensus of jurists), Qiyas (analogical deduction — not recognised by Shias). Secondary: custom (urf), judicial precedent, legislation (Shariat Act 1937; DMMA 1939; MWPRDA 2019), and justice, equity & good conscience (Abu Hanifa’s istihsan). The Shariat Act 1937 s.2 applies Muslim personal law to Muslims in matters of marriage, divorce, maintenance, dower, guardianship, gifts, wakf, inheritance, etc., but not adoption, wills/legacies or agricultural land.

Sunni vs. Shia (marriage points)

Hanafi Sunni: two witnesses required (absence = irregular); recognises fasid; marriage with Kitabia valid, with idolatress irregular; fifth-wife/iddat/unlawful-conjunction marriages irregular; no Muta. Shia (Ithna Ashari): no witnesses needed; only valid or void (no fasid); Muslim–non-Muslim marriage void (but valid Muta with Kitabia/fire-worshipper); recognises Muta; only father/grandfather are marriage guardians; valid retirement ≠ consummation.

Iddat

Period of seclusion after divorce/death to ascertain pregnancy and avoid confusion of paternity. On divorce: three menstrual courses (or three lunar months if non-menstruating; until delivery if pregnant) — only if consummated. On death: four months and ten days (or until delivery if longer), whether or not consummated. Marriage during iddat is irregular (Sunni) / void (Shia).

Presumption of marriage

Arises (in absence of direct proof) from prolonged cohabitation as spouses, acknowledgment of paternity, or acknowledgment of the woman as wife (Mulla §268) — rebuttable and unavailable where a legal bar/impossibility appears (Rashid Ahmad).
