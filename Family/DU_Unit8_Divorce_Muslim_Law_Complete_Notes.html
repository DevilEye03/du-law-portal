01Conceptual Foundations — Divorce in Muslim Law

1.1 Marriage is a civil contract — so divorce is its natural corollary

Classical Muslim law treats marriage (nikah) as a civil contract — not a sacrament (unlike Hindu marriage) and not an indissoluble union. Since the contract can be made, it can also, under certain conditions, be dissolved. This foundational idea runs through every DU-prescribed judgment. The Supreme Court said in Zohara Khatoon v. Mohd. Ibrahim (1981) 2 SCC 509 (quoted in the DU casebook in Masroor Ahmed):

“…under the Mahomedan law the commonest form of divorce is a unilateral declaration or pronouncement of divorce of the wife by the husband according to the various forms recognised by the law. A divorce given unilaterally by the husband is especially peculiar to Mahomedan law. In no other law has the husband got a unilateral right to divorce his wife by a simple declaration because other laws viz. the Hindu law or the Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936, contemplate only a dissolution of marriage on certain grounds brought about by one of the spouses in a Court of law.”Zohara Khatoon v. Mohd. Ibrahim, (1981) 2 SCC 509, as extracted in Masroor Ahmed (DU Casebook p. 248)

1.2 The Quranic (Islamic) ethos of divorce

The DU case material repeatedly stresses (quoting V. R. Krishna Iyer, J. in A. Yousuf Rawther v. Sowramma, itself quoted with approval in Shamim Ara) that:

  • The Prophet declared divorce to be “the most disliked of lawful things in the sight of God”; Islam discourages divorce in principle and permits it only in extreme emergency when the parties cannot “live within the limits of Allah” (i.e., fulfil mutual marital obligations).
  • Quran IV: 34 – “If they (namely, women) obey you, then do not seek a way against them.” The Quran expressly forbids a man to seek pretexts for divorcing a faithful, obedient wife.
  • “It is a popular fallacy that a Muslim male enjoys, under the Quranic law, unbridled authority to liquidate the marriage.” (Krishna Iyer, J., para 7, Yousuf Rawther; reiterated in Shamim Ara).
  • Divorce is permissible only when all efforts at reconciliation have failed; the ideal process is arbitration: at the time of divorce the husband must pay off the settlement (dower) debt; at the time of khula the wife surrenders dower or abandons some rights “as compensation” (Dr. Galwash, quoted in Yousuf Rawther).
  • The first khula in Islam — Jameela (wife of Thabit-ibn-Quais) — reported in Bukhari: she told the Prophet she did not complain of his morals or religion but feared she could not observe the bounds set by God; the Prophet asked her to return the garden (her settlement) and told Thabit to “take your garden and divorce her at once.” This tradition is quoted at length in Yousuf Rawther (paras 8–9 of the DU text).

1.3 Statutory recognition of the modes of dissolution — MPL(S) Act, 1937

The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937 settled that Muslims would be governed by their personal law (Shariat) notwithstanding contrary custom or usage. Section 2 of the 1937 Act — reproduced in extenso inside the DU casebook (in Masroor Ahmed, para 16) — reads:

“2. Application of Personal Law to Muslims. — Notwithstanding any customs or usage to the contrary, in all questions (save questions relating to agricultural land) regarding intestate succession, special property of females, including personal property inherited or obtained under contract or gift or any other provision of Personal Law, marriage, dissolution of marriage, including talaq, ila, zihar, lian, khula and mubaraat, maintenance, dower, guardianship, gifts, trusts and trust properties, and wakfs (other than charities and charitable institutions and charitable and religious endowments) the rule of decision in cases where the parties are Muslims shall be the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat).” The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937, s. 2 — quoted in Masroor Ahmed (DU Casebook p. 246)

The key learning from the DU text: the words “notwithstanding any customs or usage to the contrary” and “the rule of decision … shall be the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat)” are an injunction upon the Court; and the expression “dissolution of marriage, including talaq, ila, zihar, lian, khula and mubaraat” gives statutory recognition to the fact that dissolution can be brought about by several modes, of which talaq is only one. Islam considers divorce odious yet pragmatically permissible at the core of which lies the concept of an irretrievably broken marriage (DU text, Masroor para 16).

02Talaq — Divorce by the Husband (Extra-judicial)

2.1 Meaning & capacity

Talaq (literally “release”) is the unilateral repudiation of the marriage by the husband — a mode unique to Muslim law. Per Mulla (§ 308): “Any Mahomedan of sound mind, who has attained puberty, may divorce his wife whenever he desires without assigning any cause.” The conditions of capacity are therefore: (i) puberty (majority under Muslim law), and (ii) soundness of mind. A talaq by a minor or by a lunatic is invalid. It may be pronounced orally or by a written talaknama (§ 310).

Words of divorce: if express (saheeh) — e.g., “I have divorced thee”, “thou art divorced” — no proof of intention is needed; if ambiguous (kinayat) — e.g., “I give up all relations with you” — the intention must be proved. (Mulla § 310; the DU judgment in Masroor also discusses pronouncement.).

2.4 When does talaq take effect? — Revocable (rajai) vs irrevocable (bain); and iddat

  • Rajai (revocable) talaq: dissolution does not occur at pronouncement; the tie is in suspense through the iddat; revocation (express or implied by resumption of cohabitation) keeps the marriage alive; on iddat’s expiry dissolution completes automatically. A hasan first-two and an ahsan talaq are rajai; a biddat (triple) talaq was classically bain instantly.
  • Bain (irrevocable) talaq: the moment of pronouncement puts an end to the marriage (subject to iddat for purposes of remarriage, mahr, maintenance). The parties cannot cohabit as husband and wife; remarriage between them requires a fresh nikah with fresh mahr — unless it is the third divorce, when halala is the only route.
  • Halala: the wife must marry another man; that marriage must be consummated and then end by that husband’s death or divorce; after her iddat she may lawfully re-marry the first husband. The DU casebook stresses halala “cannot be employed as a device to re-marry the same spouse but must happen in the natural course of events. It is, in effect, a near impossibility and, for all intents and purposes, the third talaq brings about a final parting” (Masroor, para 21).
  • Iddat is the woman’s waiting period: for divorce — three menstrual courses (or, if no menses, three lunar months; if pregnant, until delivery); for widowhood — four months and ten days. During iddat of a revocable divorce she is entitled to residence and maintenance; she cannot marry another man during iddat.

2.5 Delegated divorce — Talaq-i-tafwiz

An important “extra-judicial” right of the wife arises where the husband delegates to her the power of pronouncing talaq (often stipulated in the nikah-nama itself — e.g., “if my husband marries a second wife, I may divorce myself”). If the stipulated condition occurs, the wife may pronounce talaq upon herself without court intervention. The DU casebook recognises this expressly: “She can also put an end to the marital tie by pronouncing talaq upon herself in the case of talaq-e-tafwiz where the husband delegates the power of pronouncing talaq to his wife” (Masroor para 18). In Itwari v. Asghari the Allahabad HC relied on such stipulations (Badu Mia v. Badrannessa, AIR 1919 Cal 511; Sheikh Mohd. v. Badrunnissa Bibee) and on Ayatunnessa Beebee v. Karam Ali (ILR 36 Cal 23) — which held that a second marriage is “not a single but a continuing wrong to the first wife”, so her option is not lost by delay.

Mulla on delegation: where divorce takes effect on a contingency, the husband can delegate his right of pronouncing divorce to the wife; a contingent talaq is recognised in Sunni (Hanafi) law but not in Shia law (Mulla § 309).

2.6 The judicial journey of triple talaq — from “valid in law” to void (1832 → 2019)

The classical position: Under Hanafi law as administered by the Privy Council and Indian courts for over a century, talaq-e-biddat was “bad in theology but valid in law” — it effected an immediate, irrevocable divorce. This position is visible in the DU casebook itself in the Unit-VII Privy Council case Saiyid Rashid Ahmad v. Mt. Anisa Khatun, AIR 1932 PC 25 (pp. 233–236 of the casebook) — “their Lordships are of opinion that the pronouncement of the triple talak by Ghiyas Uddin constituted an immediately effective divorce … A talak actually pronounced under compulsion or in jest is valid and effective.”

The reform position (post-1981): Gauhati HC in Jiauddin Ahmed v. Anwara Begum ((1981) 1 GLR 358) and Rukia Khatun v. Abdul Khalique Laskar ((1981) 1 GLR 375), speaking through Baharul Islam, J., held that the correct law of talaq as ordained by the Quran is: (i) talaq must be for a reasonable cause; and (ii) it must be preceded by an attempt at reconciliation between husband and wife by two arbiters — one chosen from the wife’s family, one from the husband’s; only if those attempts fail may talaq be effected. These decisions expressly recorded dissent from the Calcutta and Bombay view (ILR 59 Cal 833; ILR 30 Bom 537). The Supreme Court in Shamim Ara (2002) declared itself in “respectful agreement” with these observations.

Masroor’s refinement (DU casebook, paras 32 & 37): where the talaq is irrevocable, reconciliation must precede pronouncement; where it is revocable (ahsan, hasan’s first two, and — after Masroor — a triple talaq treated as one revocable talaq), reconciliation may take place after pronouncement but before the iddat ends. Pronouncement of talaq does not ipso facto dissolve the marriage; during iddat of a revocable talaq the wife’s status is akin to divorce a mensa et thoro; full dissolution (like a vinculo) occurs only on completion of iddat without revocation.

2.7 Pronouncement and communication — the modern requirements

The DU casebook (Masroor, paras 33–36) distils the modern law as follows:

  • Pronouncement is essential: “A talaq to be effective has to be pronounced” (Shamim Ara). “Pronounce” = to proclaim, utter formally, declare, articulate.
  • Communication is an essential element of pronouncement: while a talaq need not be pronounced in the presence of the wife, it must be made known (communicated) to her at the earliest — otherwise she would be deprived of her post-talaq, pre-dissolution rights (iddat, maintenance, residence, mahr, custody, and the right not to pledge the husband’s credit). Where the wife is present, pronouncement includes communication; where absent, “the talaq would be effective from the date the pronouncement is communicated to the wife; if not communicated at all, even after a reasonable length of time, a vital ingredient of pronouncement is missing and such a talaq would not take effect.”
  • Reasonable cause + reconciliation: after Shamim Ara, wherever talaq is contested, the husband must prove: (a) the factum of pronouncement (pleadings are not proof), (b) reasonable cause, and (c) an attempt at reconciliation. This applies to ahsan, hasan and biddat alike.
  • Talaq in extreme anger is invalid: where the husband has lost self-control, the pronouncement is not a free act of will and does not dissolve the marriage (Masroor, Q.2).
“The position of the law relating to talaq, where it is contested by either spouse, is that, if it has to take effect, first of all the pronouncement of talaq must be proved (it is not sufficient to merely state in court in a written statement or in some other pleading that talaq was given at some earlier point of time), then reasonable cause must be shown as also the attempt at reconciliation must be demonstrated to have taken place.”Masroor Ahmed (Delhi HC), as put by the Supreme Court in Shamim Ara — DU Casebook p. 251

2.9 Effects of a valid talaq (summary)

  • Matrimonial tie: dissolves at once (bain) or on expiry of iddat without revocation (rajai).
  • Iddat: wife must observe iddat (3 courses / 3 months / till delivery if pregnant); cannot remarry during it.
  • Dower (mahr): if deferred dower or its balance exists, it becomes payable on dissolution; a prompt dower already received is not returned (save in khula, where dower is typically the consideration).
  • Maintenance: during iddat of a revocable talaq the wife continues to be maintained; post-dissolution rights arise under s.125 CrPC / Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986 — studied in Unit IX (Danial Latifi; Noor Saba Khatoon).
  • Remarriage: wife may remarry after iddat; husband may remarry at once (except after 4 wives); the same couple cannot remarry after the third/final divorce except through halala occurring naturally.
  • Children: legitimacy unaffected; custody (hizanat) rules apply; father remains bound to maintain children.
  • Succession: divorced spouses do not inherit from each other (unless death occurs during revocable iddat, in some views).

03Khula, Mubaraat & the Other Modes (Ila, Zihar, Lian, Apostasy)

3.1 Khula — divorce at the instance of the wife

Meaning: “Khula” (literally “to put off, as one puts off a garment”) is a divorce with the consent and at the instance of the wife, in which she gives (or agrees to give) a consideration to the husband for her release from the marriage tie (Mulla § 319; Baillie).

  • The wife proposes dissolution; she generally offers to give up her claim to mahr (dower) or part of it, or any other agreed benefit.
  • The husband cannot unreasonably refuse: per the DU casebook, khula “is a divorce which proceeds from the wife which the husband cannot refuse subject only to reasonable negotiation with regard to what the wife has offered to give him in return” (Masroor, para 16).
  • Once the offer is accepted, it operates as a single irrevocable divorce (talaq-i-bain); operation is not postponed to execution of the khulanama; failure of the wife to pay the consideration does not invalidate the divorce — the husband may only sue for it (Mulla).
  • No reason or fault needs to be alleged — no-fault/no-blame basis (Masroor).
  • Unless otherwise agreed, khula operates as a release of the wife’s dower, but the husband still must maintain her during iddat and maintain the children (Mulla § 320). The wife must observe iddat.
  • Judicial khula: where the husband unreasonably withholds consent, the wife may sue under s. 2(ix), DMMA 1939 (any other ground recognised by Muslim law) read with the khula doctrine — recognised in A. Yousuf Rawther (Krishna Iyer, J. called khula “the last gateway for a Muslim woman out of an irreparably embittered co-existence”).

3.2 Mubaraat — divorce by mutual consent

Meaning: “Mubaraat” means mutual release. It is a dissolution of marriage by agreement where the aversion is on both sides and both desire separation.

  • The offer may proceed from either spouse; once accepted, dissolution is complete and operates as talaq-i-bain (irrevocable), like khula (Mulla § 319).
  • Because it is truly mutual, no consideration needs to be given by the wife: “Since this is divorce by mutual consent there is no necessity for the wife to give up or offer anything to the husband” (Masroor, para 16).
  • No reason need be specified — no-fault divorce.
  • Iddat must be observed by the wife; subject to contrary contract, mahr does not become forfeit (unlike khula) but effect follows the terms; maintenance during iddat of the wife and of children remains the husband’s liability.
  • Resort to khula (and to a lesser degree mubaraat) is “quite common in India” (Masroor).

3.4 Ila

Ila is a species of constructive divorce by a vow: the husband swears to abstain from sexual intercourse with his wife for a period of not less than four months. If he persists beyond four months, divorce takes effect (Hanafi); under Shafei law the expiry itself does not dissolve the marriage but gives the wife a right to demand judicial divorce (Mulla § 317). Per the DU casebook (Masroor para 17), ila and zihar are “virtually non-existent in India”, yet both are statutorily recognised in s. 2 of the Shariat Act 1937.

3.5 Zihar

Zihar is an inchoate divorce: the husband compares his wife to his mother or another female within the prohibited degrees (e.g., “you are like my mother’s back to me”). The wife then has a right to refuse herself to him until he performs penance (kaffara); in default of expiation, the wife may seek a judicial divorce. Cases of zihar are unknown in India (Mulla § 318), but the law of zihar received statutory recognition in s. 2 of the Shariat Act, 1937.

3.6 Lian — divorce on false accusation of adultery (zina)

  • If a husband accuses his wife of adultery (zina) but cannot prove it, the wife may approach the qazi for dissolution of marriage; in India a regular suit is filed.
  • Once the suit is filed, the husband may retract the charge — then the suit fails. If he persists, he must make four oaths in support of the charge; the wife makes four oaths of her innocence; thereafter the court declares the marriage dissolved (DU casebook, Masroor para 17).
  • Lian thus lies at the border of extra-judicial and judicial dissolution — it is a recognised mode under s.2 Shariat Act 1937 and is enforced through court; it is also covered by the residuary clause s. 2(ix) of the DMMA 1939 (Masroor para 18).

3.7 Apostasy (renunciation of Islam) and divorce

  • Before the DMMA 1939: apostasy from Islam by either party operated as complete and immediate dissolution of the marriage.
  • s. 4, DMMA 1939 (verified text): “The renunciation of Islam by a married Muslim woman or her conversion to faith other than Islam shall not by itself operate to dissolve her marriage: Provided that after such renunciation, or conversion, the woman shall be entitled to obtain a decree for the dissolution of her marriage on any of the grounds mentioned in section 2: Provided further that the provisions of this section shall not apply to a woman converted to Islam from some other faith who re-embraces her former faith.”
  • Husband’s apostasy still operates as immediate dissolution (Mulla § 321): he cannot take advantage of his own faithless act to keep the marriage alive; e.g., a convert-to-Islam husband becoming Christian dissolves the marriage, so the wife’s remarriage after iddat is not bigamy.
  • The DMMA was enacted inter alia “to remove doubts as to the effect of the renunciation of Islam by a married Muslim woman on her marriage tie”; Mulla discusses whether s.4 operates retrospectively — the better view is that it does not (Sarwar Yar Khan v. Jawahar Devi, AP HC).

04Judicial Divorce — The Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939

4.1 Background and need for the Act

  • Hanafi hardship: Before 1939 a Hanafi wife (India’s dominant school) could obtain a judicial dissolution only on very few grounds — e.g., impotence of the husband, or option of puberty in marriages contracted by guardians other than father/father’s father. There was no provision enabling her to obtain a decree where the husband neglected to maintain her, made her life miserable by desertion or persistent maltreatment, or absconded leaving her unprovided for.
  • Maliki remedy unutilised: Maliki law (and Shafi’i/Hanbali) did allow dissolution in such circumstances, and the Hanafi jurists themselves laid down that “in cases in which the application of Hanafi Law causes hardship, it is permissible to apply the provisions of the Maliki, Shafi‘i or Hambali Law” — but courts in British India hesitated to apply Maliki law.
  • Consequence: “unspeakable misery to innumerable Muslim women in British India” (Statement of Objects and Reasons, quoted by Mulla and in the DU text on Masroor: the 1939 Act “introduced a very salutary principle … the principle of applying beneficial provisions of one school to adherents of other schools as well”).
  • The Act (VIII of 1939, 17 March 1939) is described as one “to consolidate and clarify” the law and “to remove doubts as to the effect of the renunciation of Islam by a married Muslim woman on her marriage tie”. It applies to women of all sects and schools of Muslim law.
Examinable points: (i) the Act is available to the wife alone (a husband cannot sue under s.2); (ii) grounds are independent — proof of any one suffices; (iii) the Act is largely declaratory/consolidating of s.2, but s.4 altered the law on apostasy; (iv) whether retrospective — the better view: not retrospective (Mulla; Sarwar Yar Khan, AP HC; the Lahore cases on Fazal Begum).

The “neglect” or “failure” need not be wilful — poverty, failing health, loss of work or imprisonment are no excuse (Mulla). Whether the wife’s own misconduct (leaving the home) bars her: two views — (a) Nagpur/Rajasthan/Allahabad view: no failure unless there was a duty to maintain under general Muslim law; (b) Sind view of Tyabji C.J. (AIR 1950 Sind 8) approved by Krishna Iyer J. in Yousuf Rawther: the plain words “has failed to provide” do not admit the wife’s delinquency; non-maintenance for two years is an index of irreconcilable breach — see full treatment in Case C4. Also covers unequal treatment of co-wives (see s.2(viii)(f)).
(iii)that the husband has been sentenced to imprisonment for a period of seven years or upwards; Requires a final sentence: proviso (a) — “no decree shall be passed on ground (iii) until the sentence has become final.” If the conviction is later set aside, the decree dissolves permanently (marriage once dissolved is not revived).
(iv)that the husband has failed to perform, without reasonable cause, his marital obligations for a period of three years; Note the words “without reasonable cause” appear here but not in clause (ii) — courts (e.g., Abdul Rashid J. in AIR 1941 Lah 167, quoted with approval in the Sind case and in Yousuf Rawther) have used this contrast to hold that under clause (ii) even a wife who contributed to the failure may sue. “Marital obligations” includes consortium, maintenance, equitable treatment.
(v)that the husband was impotent at the time of the marriage and continues to be so; The classical Hanafi ground, now available to all. Proviso (c): before passing a decree on this ground, the Court shall on the husband’s application make an order requiring him to satisfy the Court within one year that he has ceased to be impotent; if he so satisfies the Court, no decree shall be passed. Medical examination and consummation evidence are usual (s.2(v)(c) — Mulla: the husband must not merely be impotent but “continues to be so”).
(vi)that the husband has been insane for a period of two years or is suffering from [leprosy or] a virulent venereal disease; Insanity must have lasted two years; alternatively a virulent venereal disease (e.g., syphilis) entitles the wife to dissolution. Note: the ground of leprosy was omitted by the Personal Laws (Amendment) Act, 2019 (Act 6 of 2019) — the DU casebook (2023) still describes the original list as including leprosy; answer with the present statutory text and add the 2019 amendment as a footnote of awareness.
(vii)that she, having been given in marriage by her father or other guardian before she attained the age of fifteen years, repudiated the marriage before attaining the age of eighteen years: Provided that the marriage has not been consummated; Statutory “option of puberty” for marriages contracted in minority by any guardian — the famous clause applied in Ghulam Sakina v. Falak Sher (Case C3). Three ingredients: (1) marriage before 15 by father or other guardian; (2) repudiation by the girl before she turns 18; (3) the marriage has not been consummated. Since 1939 the father’s contract “stands on no higher footing” than any other guardian’s. Consummation with consent after puberty = ratification; cohabitation during minority does not destroy the option. Repudiation may be by suit or declaration.
(viii)that the husband treats her with cruelty, that is to say, —
(a) habitually assaults her or makes her life miserable by cruelty of conduct even if such conduct does not amount to physical ill-treatment; or
(b) associates with women of evil repute or leads an infamous life; or
(c) attempts to force her to lead an immoral life; or
(d) disposes of her property or prevents her exercising her legal rights over it; or
(e) obstructs her in the observance of her religious profession or practice; or
(f) if he has more wives than one, does not treat her equitably in accordance with the injunctions of the Quran;		 A statutory, gender-friendly definition of cruelty going beyond physical violence: mental cruelty ((a)), infamous life ((b)), immorality ((c)), property interference ((d)), religious obstruction ((e)) and — crucially for polygamy — inequitable treatment among co-wives ((f)). Note the DU-prescribed case Itwari v. Asghari holds that a second marriage itself, in modern conditions, presumptively amounts to cruelty to the first wife for purposes of defending restitution (and feeds into the s.2(viii)(f) and s.2(ii) remedies). Also: the DMMA ground operates cumulatively with the general law — e.g., s. 2(ii) read with s. 2(viii)(f): a husband who fails to maintain the first wife after a second marriage enables divorce on both clauses.
(ix)on any other ground which is recognised as valid for the dissolution of marriages under Muslim law: Residuary clause — preserves the entire pre-existing Islamic law in favour of women: e.g., judicial khula, lian, option of puberty under Shia law, faskh on other classical grounds, desertion-cum-cruelty in Maliki law etc. The DU casebook states: “Act 8 of 1939 does not abrogate the grounds already available to a woman and s.2(ix) is clearly a statutory preservation of prior Islamic rights” (Krishna Iyer J., Yousuf Rawther, para 19 — though he declined to rest the decree on it there).
Preamble & long title: “An Act to consolidate and clarify the provisions of Muslim law relating to suits for dissolution of marriage by women married under Muslim law and to remove doubts as to the effect of the renunciation of Islam by a married Muslim woman on her marriage tie.” — Act VIII of 1939 (assented to and commenced 17 March 1939); extends to whole of India (J&K covered after 2019 amendments); s. 5 preserves the wife’s right to dower on dissolution: “Nothing contained in this Act shall affect any right which a married woman may have under Muslim law to her dower or any part thereof on the dissolution of her marriage.”

4.3 Procedure, provisos and nature of the decree

  • Suit by the wife (or on her behalf) in a civil court / family court; a suit for dissolution cannot be referred to arbitration or decided under the Indian Oaths Act — it is for the Court to decide on evidence (Mulla).
  • Grounds may be combined (“any one or more”); grounds are independent — failure on one does not defeat another.
  • Provisos: (a) no decree on (iii) till sentence final; (b) decree on (i) inoperative for 6 months, liable to be set aside on the husband’s appearance and readiness to perform conjugal duties; (c) one-year potency test on (v).
  • Effect of decree: marriage stands dissolved (faskh — annulment-type dissolution) from the decree; the wife observes iddat of divorce from the decree date; her dower rights are untouched (s.5); children’s legitimacy and custody are unaffected; she is free to remarry after iddat.
  • A decree once passed on the husband’s later acquittal (iii) is not automatically revived — marriage already dissolved.
  • Representation of the husband’s heirs and notice procedure (s.3) apply to clause (i) suits so that property rights are not disturbed unfairly.

Case 01 — Shamim Ara v. State of U.P. & Anr.

Supreme Court of India • 2002 Cri LJ 4726 (SC) = AIR 2002 SC 3551 = (2002) 7 SCC 518 • Decided: 1 October 2002 • Bench: R. C. Lahoti & P. Venkatarama Reddi, JJ. • DU Casebook pp. 237–241
Tags“Validity of talaq” — most repeated 20-mark DU question (2017 Q6; 2018 Q7; 2019 Q8(a); 2020 Q5; 2021 Q5; 2022 Q7)talaq must be pronouncedplea in written statement ≠ talaqcommunication to wifereconciliation
Facts
  • Shamim Ara (appellant) and Abrar Ahmed (respondent) were married in 1968 according to Muslim Shariyat law; four sons were born.
  • On 12.4.1979 the wife, for herself and her two minor sons, filed an application under s. 125 CrPC complaining of desertion and cruelty.
  • On 3.4.1993 the Family Court, Allahabad refused maintenance to the wife — holding she had already been divorced by the husband and hence was not entitled — but allowed ₹150 p.m. for one minor son (the other had attained majority).
  • The husband’s written statement (filed 5.12.1990) contained, as an “additional plea”, only a bald assertion that he had divorced the wife on 11.7.1987, claiming the protection of the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986, and asserting he had given her a house in lieu of mehar (dower). No particulars of the talaq (manner, witnesses, circumstances) were pleaded.
  • In the witness box the husband deposed that he had pronounced a triple talaq on 11.7.1987 at 11 a.m. before Mehboob and 4–5 neighbours — although no such case was stated in the written statement. He admitted paying nothing since 1988 for the wife or sons.
  • The Family Court also relied on an affidavit dated 31.8.1988 filed by the husband in some other civil litigation to which the wife was not a party, stating he had divorced the wife 15 months earlier — and treated this as corroboration.
  • The High Court accepted that the alleged divorce was not pronounced in the wife’s presence and not communicated, but held communication “would stand completed on 5.12.1990” (service of the written statement); the wife was therefore entitled to maintenance only from 1.1.1988 to 5.12.1990 (₹200 p.m.).
  • The wife appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave.
Issues
  1. Can the appellant be said to have been divorced, and the divorce communicated to her, so as to become effective from 5.12.1990 — the date of filing of the written statement? (the singular issue framed by the Court)
  2. Does a mere plea of a past divorce in pleadings — or a self-serving affidavit in non-inter-partes proceedings — amount to a valid pronouncement of talaq?
  3. What are the conditions of a valid talaq under Muslim law as administered in India (pronouncement, reasonable cause, reconciliation, communication)?
Arguments — Wife (appellant)She emphatically denied having ever been divorced. The alleged talaq of 11.7.1987 was never proved: no particulars were pleaded, no evidence was led except the husband’s self-serving oral statement, and it was not communicated to her. The Family Court wrongly relied on an affidavit filed in proceedings to which she was not a party. An unproved plea in a written statement cannot dissolve a marriage or terminate her s.125 maintenance.
Arguments — Husband (respondent)He claimed he divorced the wife on 11.7.1987 by triple talaq before witnesses; that in any case a statement in his written statement/affidavit acknowledging a prior divorce operated as divorce (relying on the line of authorities collected by Mulla in para 310 and by Dr. Tahir Mahmood — e.g., the Andhra Pradesh view in (1975) 1 APLJ 20 that a statement in pleadings filed in answer to a maintenance petition operates as divorce); and that under the 1986 Act he owed no maintenance beyond the iddat since he had given the house in lieu of dower.
Judgment / Decision (R. C. Lahoti, J.)
  1. No scriptural basis for “recital-as-divorce”: none of the holy scriptures or texts of Muslims provides that a recital in any document — pleading or affidavit — stating that the husband had already divorced his wife on an unspecified/specified date, even if never communicated to her, becomes an effective divorce when the wife happens to learn of it from a served copy (para 7).
  2. Review of the old learning: the Court set out the statement in Mulla (para 310) and the decisions it relied upon, and contrasted them with the critique of judges like V. Khalid, J. (Mohammed Haneefa v. Pathummal Beevi, 1972 Ker LT 512 — “Should Muslim wives suffer this tyranny for all times?... My judicial conscience is disturbed at this monstrosity”) and V. R. Krishna Iyer, J. (Yousuf Rawther — “It is a popular fallacy that a Muslim male enjoys, under the Quranic law, unbridled authority to liquidate the marriage… the whimsical and capricious divorce by the husband is good in law, though bad in theology” is a statement “based on the concept that women were chattel belonging to men, which the Holy Quran does not brook”).
  3. Quranic conditions affirmed: with “respectful agreement” the Court adopted the law declared by Baharul Islam, J. of Gauhati HC in Jiauddin Ahmed v. Anwara Begum and Rukia Khatun v. Abdul Khalique Laskar: “talaq must be for a reasonable cause and be preceded by attempts at reconciliation between the husband and the wife by two arbiters — one from the wife’s family and the other from the husband’s; if the attempts fail, talaq may be effected.” The Calcutta and Bombay view was expressly disapproved.
  4. No proof of talaq here: the husband pleaded no particulars, gave no reason justifying talaq, showed no reconciliation attempt, and adduced no evidence beyond himself. The Family Court’s use of the affidavit of 31.8.1988 (filed in a different, non-inter-partes litigation) was wrong — a self-serving statement in those proceedings could not be read as relevant or of any value.
  5. “Pronouncement” is the essence — holding on the written statement: “the talaq to be effective has to be pronounced” (“pronounce” = proclaim, utter formally, articulate). “We are very clear in our mind that a mere plea taken in the written statement of a divorce having been pronounced sometime in the past cannot by itself be treated as effecting talaq on the date of delivery of the copy of the written statement to the wife… A plea of previous divorce taken in the written statement cannot at all be treated as pronouncement of talaq by the husband on wife on the date of the filing of the written statement.” The Court expressly disagreed with the view in the decided cases referred to by Mulla and Tahir Mahmood “wherein a mere plea of previous talaq taken in the written statement, though unsubstantiated, has been accepted as proof of talaq.”
  6. Result: appeal allowed; the marriage did not stand dissolved on 5.12.1990; the husband’s liability to pay maintenance continued until it ends in accordance with law; costs on the husband.
Principle evolved (ratio)
  1. Talaq, to be effective, must be pronounced; a bald, unsubstantiated plea of prior divorce in a written statement (or an affidavit in other proceedings) is not pronouncement and does not dissolve the marriage on the date the pleading is served on the wife.
  2. The onus is on the husband who pleads talaq to prove the factum of pronouncement by evidence, and the Quranic conditions — reasonable cause and an attempt at reconciliation through two arbiters — are part of the valid exercise of the right.
  3. The talaq must be communicated/known to the wife to operate on her (implied approval of the High Court’s and earlier HCs’ insistence on knowledge); the 2002 decision thereby checked the ancient doctrine of instantaneous, secret, or self-serving divorce.
  4. Maintenance liability under s.125 CrPC does not end by an unproved plea of divorce — it continues until the marriage is lawfully dissolved.
Exam angle: Shamim Ara is the turning point of the entire unit. Typical questions — “Discuss the validity of talaq under Muslim law with reference to Shamim Ara”; “Can a husband divorce his wife by a mere statement in a written statement?”; “After Shamim Ara what is the role of reconciliation?” Mention: (i) the Mulla para-310 doctrine it overruled-in-effect; (ii) agreement with Jiauddin/Rukia Khatun; (iii) the follow-up in Masroor (below).

Case 02 — Masroor Ahmed v. State (NCT of Delhi) & Anr.

Delhi High Court • 2008 (103) DRJ 137 (Del.) = ILR (2007) II Del 1329 • Decided: 3 October 2007 • Judge: Badar Durrez Ahmed, J. • DU Casebook pp. 242–254
Tagstriple talaq legalitytalaq in angercommunicationone revocable talaqShariat Act 1937 s.2quashing of FIR u/s 376 IPC
Facts
  • The complainant, Aisha Anjum, married the petitioner Masroor Ahmed on 2.4.2004 according to Muslim rites; a daughter was born. She alleged she was driven out for non-fulfilment of dowry demands (complaint lodged with the Crime Against Women Cell).
  • The petitioner filed a suit for restitution of conjugal rights; on 13.4.2006, from the court itself, the complainant went with her husband to the matrimonial home; they lived together till 19.4.2006, on which date a second nikah was performed between the same parties.
  • On 12.12.2006 the complainant filed a written complaint; the same day FIR No. 817/2006 (PS Preet Vihar) under s. 376 IPC was registered, alleging rape between 13.4.2006 and 19.4.2006. Her case was that she had been divorced by triple talaq in October 2005; hence during 13–19 April 2006 she was no longer the petitioner’s wife, her consent was “tainted” and sex amounted to rape; the second nikah (19.4.2006) was, she claimed, to regularise the union.
  • The Additional Sessions Judge rejected bail (20.12.2006). The petitioner moved the High Court for quashing of the FIR and bail.
  • A settlement was reached between the parties (they decided to part ways; custody of the daughter settled) — this itself would justify quashing, but the court also examined the Muslim-law merits, because the FIR’s foundation was the alleged triple talaq of October 2005.
Five questions framed (para 12)
  1. What is the legality and effect of a triple talaq?
  2. Does a talaq given in anger result in dissolution of marriage?
  3. What is the effect of non-communication of the talaq to the wife?
  4. Was the purported talaq of October 2005 valid?
  5. What is the effect of the second nikah of 19.4.2006?
Arguments — Petitioner (husband) (1) The alleged triple talaq of October 2005 was invalid in law and was never communicated (relying on Riaz Fatima v. Mohd Sharif (135 (2006) DLT 205); Dagdu Chotu Pathan v. Rahimbi Dagdu Pathan (2002 (3) MhLJ 602 (FB)); Dilshad Begum Ahmadkhan Pathan v. Ahmadkhan Hanifkhan Pathan (Bom HC, 17.1.2007); and Shamim Ara). (2) Consequently the wife continued to be his wife, so sex between 13–19.4.2006 could not be rape — a wife is excepted under s.375 IPC itself. (3) In any event, a triple talaq at one sitting could at best be one talaq, so the second nikah of 19.4.2006 was permissible and valid. (4) Therefore consent may be presumed for sexual acts before that nikah (State of A.P. v. P. Narasimha, 1994 SCC (Cri) 1180).
Arguments — Complainant (wife)She stood on the triple talaq of October 2005: it dissolved the marriage; the consent given from 13.4.2006 to 19.4.2006 was “tainted consent” and not free consent; hence sexual intercourse during that period was rape even though a nikah was performed on 19.4.2006. (The court noted these contentions about Muslim law were incorrect because their foundation — a valid October 2005 talaq — was illusory.)
Judgment — the Court’s doctrinal survey (Badar Durrez Ahmed, J.)
  • Sources & schools: fiqh rests on four roots (usul al-fiqh) — Quran, hadis/sunna, ijma, qiyas — developed by ijtihad; Sunni schools: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi‘i, Hanbali; Shia schools: Ithna Ashari, Jafariya, Ismaili. Indian Muslims are predominantly Sunni-Hanafi; Indian Shias largely Ithna Ashari.
  • Fatwa vs qaza: a mufti’s fatwa is merely advisory; a qazi’s qaza is binding — both must be based on the shariat.
  • Shariat Act 1937 (s.2 reproduced in full): dissolution of marriage, including talaq, ila, zihar, lian, khula and mubaraat — statutory recognition that talaq is only one of several modes (see §1.3 above).
  • Khula & mubaraat explained (paras 16–17) — see Section 3 above; ila and zihar are virtually non-existent; lian (process of four oaths each) explained.
  • DMMA 1939: enabled Muslim women of all sects to sue on s.2 grounds; s.2(ix) residuary clause; the Act’s SOR shows the “salutary principle” of applying Maliki/Shafi‘i/Hanbali provisions when Hanafi law causes hardship.
  • Who may divorce how: wife can seek divorce outside court (khula) or through court (1939 Act or lian), and can exercise talaq-i-tafwiz where delegated; husband can dissolve only outside court through talaq; both can agree on mubaraat without court (para 18).
  • Three forms of talaq (ahsan/hasan/biddat) explained with the Quranic verses; halala explained as “a near impossibility” that must occur in the natural course (paras 20–23).
  • Triple talaq is sinful — deprecated by the Prophet, not approved by any school, not a valid divorce for Shias, and even among Sunnis there are views that three pronouncements at one go count as one (paras 24–26). Its “harsh abruptness has brought extreme misery to divorced women and even to the men.”
Answers to the five questions (para 37)
  1. Triple talaq (talaq-e-biddat), even for Sunni Muslims, shall be regarded as one revocable (rajai) talaq — enabling the husband to think and revoke during iddat, family members to attempt reconciliation, and, if iddat expires unrevoked, a fresh nikah on fresh terms (para 27).
  2. Talaq in extreme anger, where the husband has lost control of himself, is not effective or valid.
  3. Communication is an essential element of pronouncement. If communicated, talaq takes effect from the date of communication; if never communicated, it does not take effect at all (paras 33–36).
  4. The October 2005 talaq was invalid — (i) it was given, if at all, in extreme anger; (ii) never communicated to the complainant by the relevant period (13.4.2006 or even 19.4.2006); (iii) no attempt at reconciliation in the Quranic manner before or after. Hence the marital tie subsisted between 13.4.2006 and 19.4.2006, and the offence of rape was not made out even on the complaint’s own allegations (the wife is excepted under s.375 IPC).
  5. The second nikah of 19.4.2006 was of no effect (marriage already subsisting). Had the October 2005 talaq been valid, it would have operated only as a single revocable talaq, so remarriage would have been permissible and the nikah valid.
Result: the FIR and all proceedings emanating from it were quashed; the bail application became infructuous.
Principle evolved
  1. After Shamim Ara, the law where talaq is contested: prove pronouncement; show reasonable cause; demonstrate an attempt at reconciliation — for an irrevocable talaq the attempt must precede pronouncement; for a revocable talaq (including a triple talaq read down as one) it may follow pronouncement but must occur before the iddat ends.
  2. Triple talaq = one revocable talaq; talaq in extreme anger is invalid; communication is a vital ingredient — effective from communication; silence/no communication = no talaq.
  3. Pronouncement does not ipso facto dissolve: during iddat of a revocable talaq the wife remains wife-like (residence + maintenance), like divorce a mensa et thoro; dissolution completes at iddat’s end — divorce a vinculo.
Exam angle: This single judgment supplies almost the entire theory of the Unit — use it for answers on “forms of talaq”, “triple talaq”, “Shariat Act 1937 s.2”, “DMMA 1939 background”, “khula/mubaraat/lian”, “Shamim Ara’s effect”, “talaq in anger” and “communication of talaq”.

Case 03 — Ghulam Sakina v. Falak Sher Allah Bakhsh

Lahore High Court (pre-partition India) • AIR 1950 Lah 45 • Decided: 26 April 1949 • Judge: Muhammad Sharif, J. • DU Casebook pp. 255–258
Tagsoption of puberty (10-mark DU Q — with Hindu law comparison)s.2(vii) DMMA 1939repudiationminority marriage by father
Facts
  • The plaintiff (Ghulam Sakina) sued for dissolution of her marriage. She pleaded: (i) she was never validly given in marriage as claimed; (ii) even if a marriage took place during her infancy, she never approved it and repudiated it; (iii) she was a Sunni girl while the defendant was a Shia and the marriage was not good (the sect issue was not pressed).
  • The defendant husband maintained that the marriage was performed by her father when she was five years old as an exchange (barter) marriage in lieu of the marriage of his own sister with the plaintiff’s uncle; that the parties lived together and the marriage was consummated; and both parties were Sunni.
  • Per the marriage register (Ex. D-2), the nikah was performed by the father on 23 November 1932. Per her birth entry, the plaintiff was born on 13 November 1931 — i.e., she was about one year old at marriage (the nikah-khawan said she was stated to be 3–4 months old, the husband about 5–7 years). Medical estimate put her at ~17 at the time of the suit examination.
  • The suit for dissolution was instituted on 29 August 1945 — in any view before she attained 18 (age ~14 per birth entry; ~17 per medical testimony).
  • Trial court: found the marriage in infancy and no credible proof of consummation; decreed dissolution. District Judge (Mianwali), on appeal, reversed, believing consummation was proved — two witnesses (P.W.2 Muhammad Niwaz, brother of defendant; P.W.7 Allah Bakhsh, his father) had “admitted” consummation, and the plaintiff refused medical examination to prove she was virgo intacta, from which a “very strong presumption” of consummation was drawn.
  • Plaintiff came in second appeal to the Lahore High Court.
Issue

The sole question: whether the plaintiff had repudiated her marriage in accordance with the requirements of s. 2(vii) of the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939 — i.e., was she given in marriage by her father before 15, did she repudiate before 18, and was the marriage not consummated? (The consummation finding and the effect of refusal of medical examination were the contested sub-issues.)

Arguments — PlaintiffThe marriage was contracted when she was an infant (~1 year); she never went to the husband; she always lived in her father’s house (letters of 1944 between the fathers showed dissensions and that the fathers did not consider the marriage in force); the alleged “rapprochement” of December 1944 was a concoction; even if some consummation occurred, it happened (if at all) when she was a child below 15 and could not destroy her option of puberty; refusal of medical examination cannot prove consummation (Privy Council in Atkia Begum v. Muhammad Ibrahim, 36 IC 20 disapproved drawing such an inference).
Arguments — Defendant (husband)The marriage was by her father, was an exchange marriage, and the couple had lived as husband and wife; consummation was established by two “respectable” witnesses (though they were his own brother and father, produced as plaintiff’s witnesses); the girl’s pointed refusal of a lady-doctor’s examination raised a strong presumption of consummation; therefore s.2(vii)’s proviso (“provided the marriage has not been consummated”) barred her suit.
Judgment — the High Court’s exposition of the option of puberty
  • Consummation was not proved: the evidence was “of a very unsatisfactory character”; the defendant never specified when consummation allegedly took place (the words “about one year ago” were scored out from his statement; he evaded the plaintiff’s request to disclose the time); the two witnesses’ evidence had to be read with the letters showing the fathers treated the marriage as no longer in force. The refusal of medical examination could not be treated as proof of consummation — the Privy Council in Atkia Begum had disapproved the very argument; “the refusal of the plaintiff to have her examined by a lady doctor could not, therefore, be taken to be a proof of the consummation of marriage which should have been proved as a fact on the consideration of the entire evidence.”
  • “The real significance of option of puberty” (para 10, quoted in full in most textbooks): marriage under Muhammadan law is a contract requiring the free and unfettered consent of the parties; for minors, guardians may contract. “Before the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, a minor girl given in marriage by the father or the father’s father, had no option to repudiate it on the attainment of her puberty but this has now been changed. The contract of the father or the father’s father stands on no higher footing than that of any other guardian and the minor could repudiate or ratify the contract made on his or her behalf during the minority, after the attainment of puberty.”
  • Puberty under Muhammadan law is presumed, absent evidence, at completion of 15 years; the option is exercisable after 15 (unless earlier puberty is proved — burden on the person so pleading).
  • Minority acts do not destroy the right: “Anything done by the minor during the minority would not destroy the right which could accrue only after puberty. The co-habitation of a minor girl would not thus put an end to the ‘option’ to repudiate the marriage after puberty.” Assent must come after puberty — the minor is incompetent to contract; nor should consummation have taken place without her consent (Baillie I.59; Abdul Karim v. Amina Bai, AIR 1935 Bom 308). Assent may be express or implied (words or conduct such as cohabitation); and the girl must be aware of the marriage before she can be expected to exercise the option.
  • Application: at the time of the alleged consummation the plaintiff was still below 15; assuming consummation to be a fact, it could not destroy her right to repudiate after 15. She had three years within which to proclaim the exercise of the right, and the institution of the suit is one mode of proclaiming it. She repudiated well before 18; the marriage was not (validly) consummated.
Result: second appeal allowed; the decree of the trial judge (dissolution of marriage) was restored with costs throughout.
Principle evolved
  1. Under s.2(vii), DMMA 1939 the option of puberty is a right of the minor girl, not of her guardian; the father’s contract now stands on the same footing as any other guardian’s, and the marriage is repudiable after puberty (15, presumed) and before 18.
  2. Consummation during minority (before 15) does not destroy the option; the bar applies only to consummation with her post-puberty consent. A child cannot consent to or ratify a marriage contract.
  3. Repudiation may be by conduct including the filing of a suit; refusal of medical examination is not evidence of consummation; consummation must be proved as a fact on the totality of evidence.
Exam angle: cite for any answer on the wife’s right to repudiate an infant marriage; on “marriage is a contract requiring free consent”; on guardians’ power of marriage; on s.2(vii) ingredients; and for the PYQ “Option of puberty under Hindu and Muslim law” (compare s.13(2)(iv) HMA). Note the DU compendium also cites this case in Yousuf Rawther’s text for the proposition that even child cohabitation cannot destroy the option.

Case 04 — A. Yousuf Rawther v. Sowramma

Kerala High Court • AIR 1971 Ker 261 • Decided: 24 June 1970 • Judge: V. R. Krishna Iyer, J. (single judge) • DU Casebook pp. 259–268 (the compendium’s running head prints “AIR 1971 Ker 266” — a typographical slip; the standard citation is AIR 1971 Ker 261)
Tagss.2(ii) DMMA — 2-year non-maintenancewife’s delinquency irrelevant (Tyabji view approved)khula / s.2(ix)famous quotes (Shamim Ara adopts them)
Facts
  • Sowramma, a Hanafi girl of about 15 (who had already attained puberty), married Yousuf Rawther, nearly twice her age, in 1962.
  • The couple spent only a few days together: the very next day the husband left for Coimbatore (where he ran a radio business); after about a month’s sojourn in the husband’s house the girl went back to her parents — each blamed the other.
  • The separation lasted over two years, during which the husband admittedly did not maintain the wife. His plea: he was willing and anxious to keep her, but she wrongfully refused to return (allegedly due to her father’s inhibition). The husband even moved the mosque committee through his brother (Ext. D2), which reported that divorce was the only solution (Ext. D4).
  • After lawyer’s notices, the wife sued for dissolution of marriage. Trial court dismissed; the Subordinate Judge granted the decree; the husband appealed to the High Court.
  • By then the husband had taken another wife, and she too had remarried after dissolution was granted in appeal — the court noted reconciliation was impossible and a decision on merits was unavoidable.
Issues
  1. The “seminal issue”: has a wife who is herself allegedly at fault (wrongfully leaving the matrimonial home) the right to judicial dissolution on the mere failure of the husband to maintain her for two years under s.2(ii), DMMA 1939 — or must she show that the failure was wrongful on the husband’s part and that she was entitled to be maintained under the general Muslim law?
  2. Did the wife have a further/alternative claim under the residuary clause s.2(ix) — including dissolution through khula?
Arguments — Appellant husbandThe wife deserted without cause; under Muslim law a wife who refuses to live with her husband is not entitled to maintenance; s.2(ii) must be read with the general Muslim law — the husband “cannot be said to have neglected or failed to provide maintenance for his wife unless under the general Mahomedan law he was under an obligation to maintain her” (Sind, 1942 view; Nagpur HC in AIR 1951 Nag 375: no neglect where the wife voluntarily stays away despite requests; Rajasthan (AIR 1956 Raj 102): failure must be “without any justification”; Allahabad (AIR 1947 All 3): “the Act does not mean that the husband is bound to follow his wife wherever she may go…”). Since the wife was not entitled to maintenance, she could not convert her own wrong into a divorce.
Arguments — Respondent wife (and amicus curiae)The plain grammatical meaning of s.2(ii) — “has neglected or has failed to provide for her maintenance for a period of two years” — requires only the fact of non-maintenance; the clause deliberately omits “without reasonable cause” (contrast s.2(iv)); divorce for non-maintenance is not a penalty on the husband but a recognition that the marriage has ceased to be a reality; classical Muslim texts and the ethos of the Quran favour dissolution when the parties cannot “live within the limits of Allah”; Indian Hanafis have long allowed divorce for simple failure to maintain (ancient texts, traditions, fatwas); Tyabji C.J. in AIR 1950 Sind 8 had so held; fear of women abusing the right is overstated (Bertrand Russell on law and custom in “Marriage and Morals”).
Judgment (V. R. Krishna Iyer, J.) — landmark reasoning
  • “The interpretation of a legislation, obviously intended to protect a weaker section of the community, like women, must be informed by the social perspective and purpose and, within its grammatical flexibility, must further the beneficent object” (para 6); the Court must appreciate “the Islamic ethos and the general sociological background.”
  • Famous Quranic pronouncements (quoted later in Shamim Ara too): “It is a popular fallacy that a Muslim male enjoys, under the Quranic law, unbridled authority to liquidate the marriage”; “The whole Quran expressly forbids a man to seek pretexts for divorcing his wife… ‘if they (namely, women) obey you, then do not seek a way against them’ (Quran IV:34)”; the Prophet declared divorce “the most disliked of lawful things in the sight of God”; divorce is permissible only in extreme emergency after all reconciliation efforts fail; at talaq the husband must pay off the settlement debt, and at khula the wife surrenders dower as compensation (Dr. Galwash).
  • Women’s right to divorce in Islam: the Quran first gave the right of divorce to women in the history of Arabia; the khula of Thabit-ibn-Quais’ wife (Jameela) before the Prophet (Bukhari) shows a wife may obtain release by returning the settlement; “if a woman be prejudiced by a marriage, let it be broken off.”
  • Resolution of the conflict of views on s.2(ii): after surveying the Sind (Tyabji C.J., AIR 1950 Sind 8, following Beckett J. in AIR 1943 Sind 65), Lahore (Abdul Rashid J., AIR 1941 Lah 167 — “whatever the cause may be the wife is entitled to a decree… if the husband fails to maintain her for a period of two years, even though the wife may have contributed towards the failure”), Nagpur, Rajasthan, Peshawar and Allahabad decisions, the learned Judge declared himself “impressed with the reasoning of Tyabji C.J., which … accords with the holy Islamic texts and the ethos of the Muslim community.”
  • Holding on s.2(ii): “I hold that a Muslim woman, under s.2(ii) of the Act, can sue for dissolution on the score that she has not as a fact been maintained even if there is good cause for it — the voice of the law, echoing public policy, is often that of the realist, not of the moralist.” The wife’s delinquency is irrelevant; the two-year failure is an “index of irreconcilable breach.” The rationale (Tyabji): Muslim divorce law is not a punishment for the husband or an enforcement of maintenance — “the failure to maintain when it has continued for a prolonged period … is regarded as an instance where a cessation or suspension of the marriage has occurred”; therefore the wife’s disobedience does not affect the principle.
  • s.2(ix) and khula: “Act 8 of 1939 does not abrogate the grounds already available to a woman and s.2(ix) is clearly a statutory preservation of prior Islamic rights”; khula is “the last gateway for a Muslim woman out of an irreparably embittered co-existence”; but since the decree was affirmed under s.2(ii), the Court did not decide the khula claim under s.2(ix) (that aspect was “supererogatory”).
  • Result: appeal dismissed — the wife’s decree of dissolution was affirmed.
Principle evolved
  1. s.2(ii) DMMA 1939: the wife need only prove the fact of the husband’s failure to maintain her for two years; she need not prove wilful neglect, and her own conduct in living apart does not defeat her right (adopting Tyabji C.J.’s Sind view and disapproving the Nagpur-Rajasthan-Allahabad line). The words of s.2(ii) do not import the phrase “without reasonable cause” found in s.2(iv).
  2. Welfare legislation for women must be interpreted purposively; Muslim law, correctly understood, does not give the husband unbridled power of divorce and recognises the wife’s release through khula.
  3. s.2(ix) preserves all pre-existing Islamic grounds (judicial khula etc.) in favour of the wife.
Exam angle: Yousuf Rawther is the authority for the “plain meaning” of s.2(ii); its Quranic passages and Krishna Iyer dicta were quoted with approval in Shamim Ara (the DU casebook reprints them there); it also supplies the khula doctrine (first khula of Islam) useful in “khula” questions; cite it with Ghulam Sakina (option) and s.2(vii) discussion inside the judgment.

Case 05 — Itwari v. Asghari

Allahabad High Court • AIR 1960 All 684 • Judge: S. S. Dhawan, J. • DU Casebook pp. 269–275
TagsRCR by Muslim husband after second marriagepolygamy tolerated, not encouragedpresumption of cruelty to first wife“continuing wrong” (Ayatunnessa)court of equity
Facts
  • Itwari, a Muslim husband, married Smt. Asghari about 1950 and lived with her for some time; things went wrong; the wife left him and went to her parents; he took no steps to bring her back and married another woman.
  • The first wife filed an application for maintenance under s.488 CrPC (now s.125 CrPC). Thereupon the husband sued her for restitution of conjugal rights (impleading her father and two brothers as co-defendants).
  • The wife contested, alleging she had been turned out by the husband (who had formed an illicit union and later married the woman), had been beaten, deprived of her ornaments, and thus caused physical and mental pain; he had also not paid her dower.
  • Trial Court (Munsif): decreed the husband’s suit — the wife failed to prove ill-treatment; mere second marriage raised no presumption of cruelty (the second wife was not brought into the same house); if aggrieved, the wife should have sued for dissolution — a strange and inconsistent view (the Court of appeal noted: conduct good enough to found a dissolution suit yet no defence to restitution?). It also restrained the wife’s father and brothers from preventing her return.
  • District Judge (Rampur), on appeal: reversed and dismissed the suit with costs — the husband’s suit was only a counter-blast to the wife’s maintenance claim; his long silence showed he never cared for her; a wife deserted for years would not find peace once another woman was installed as his wife. Itwari came in second appeal.
Issues
  1. Is the husband’s conduct in taking a second wife a ground for the first wife to refuse to live with him, or for dismissing his suit for restitution of conjugal rights?
  2. Even without proof of cruelty, may the court, sitting in equity, refuse restitution when it would be inequitable to compel the first wife to share consortium with a husband who has remarried?
  3. Does dismissal of such a suit deny the Muslim husband’s right to take up to four wives?
Arguments — Appellant husband (Mr. N. A. Kazmi) (1) Cruelty defeating a husband’s right to consortium is a mixed question of law and fact, so the High Court in second appeal could re-examine the evidence. (2) The mere fact of a second marriage is no proof of cruelty — every Muslim has the right to four wives (Quran IV:3), and the District Judge erred in law. (3) To defeat restitution there must be cruelty “of such a character as to render it unsafe for the wife to return to her husband’s dominion” (Mulla, 14th ed., p. 246 — borrowed from the Privy Council in the Shamsunnisa Begum case). (4) Dismissal would virtually deny his right to a second marriage.
Arguments — Respondent first wifeShe had been ill-treated and turned out; the suit was filed only to escape maintenance (mala fide); a second wife had been installed; compelling her to return after years of desertion and neglect would be inequitable; the second marriage itself, under modern social conditions, amounts to cruelty to the first wife and a “continuing wrong” (Ayatunnessa Beebee v. Karam Ali, ILR 36 Cal 23); her right to maintenance survived despite a second marriage (2nd proviso to s.488(3) CrPC).
Judgment (S. S. Dhawan, J.) — key holdings
  • Marriage as contract; restitution as equitable relief: a Muslim marriage is a civil contract and a suit for restitution of conjugal rights enforces the right to consortium under it (disobedience enforceable by imprisonment/attachment — Moonshee Buzloor Ruheem v. Shumsoonissa Begum, 11 Moo IA 551; Abdul Kadir v. Salima, ILR 8 All 149 (FB)). But specific performance is equitable and discretionary; courts function as “mixed courts of equity” guided by English equitable principles, including the plaintiff’s own conduct — clean hands, good faith, hardship (para 6).
  • “The question before the Court is not whether the husband had the right to take a second wife but whether this Court, as a court of equity, should lend its assistance to the husband by compelling the first wife … to live with him after he has taken a second wife in the circumstances in which he did.” (para 5)
  • Polygamy tolerated, not encouraged: Quran IV:3 was a restrictive measure — a ceiling of four, qualified by a “better not” advice: marry one if you cannot be equitable; several jurists regard even-handedness as an impossible condition, showing Muslim law discourages polygamy in practice (paras 9–11). Muslim law as enforced in India “has not conferred upon the husband any fundamental right to compel the first wife to share his consortium with another woman in all circumstances.”
  • Muslim law itself arms the first wife: she may stipulate for (or receive by delegation) the power of divorce on the husband’s second marriage (Badu Mia v. Badrannessa, AIR 1919 Cal 511; Sheikh Mohd. v. Badrunnissa Bibee, 7 Beng LR App 5); and in Ayatunnessa (ILR 36 Cal 23) a second marriage was called “not a single but a continuing wrong to the first wife”, so her option is not lost by delay; agreements restraining a second marriage are not void under s.23 Contract Act. Muslim law thus “prefers the breaking up of the first marriage to compelling the first wife to share her husband with the second.”
  • Cruelty — universal standard: on questions of legal cruelty, Mohammedan law “would probably not differ materially from the English law” (Privy Council in Shamsunnisa Begum); cruelty = conduct causing danger to life, limb or health (bodily or mental) or reasonable apprehension thereof (Rayden); there is no separate “Muslim cruelty”; social conditions matter — Muslim society is not static (Abdur Rahim’s Tagore Law Lectures).
  • Second marriage = presumption of cruelty to the first wife (modern conditions): “Today the importing of a second wife into the household ordinarily means a stinging insult to the first… leads to the asking of awkward questions, the raising of unsympathetic eyebrows and the pointing of derisive fingers at the first wife who is automatically degraded by society… Under the prevailing conditions the very act of taking a second wife, in the absence of a weighty and convincing explanation, raises a presumption of cruelty to the first (the Calcutta High Court called it a ‘continuing wrong’). The onus today would be on the husband … to explain his action and prove that his taking a second wife involved no insult or cruelty to the first.”
  • Even without cruelty, equity refuses: in several cases courts refused restitution because circumstances made it inequitable (Hamid Hussain v. Kubra Begum AIR 1918 All 235 — worst of terms and property motive; Nawab Bibi v. Allah Ditta AIR 1924 Lah 188; Khurshid Begum v. Abdul Rashid AIR 1926 Nag 234). The DMMA 1939 itself is proof of the impact of social change — it enabled a wife to sue for dissolution when the husband “has more wives than one” and fails to treat them equitably (s.2(viii)(f)) — hence “it is but a short step … to ask a husband who has taken a second wife … to explain the reasons for this conduct.”
  • Dismissal does not deny the second marriage: the second marriage remains valid and its children legitimate; he simply cannot force the first wife to share his consortium. The lower appellate court’s finding that the suit was mala fide (a counter-blast to the maintenance claim) and its belief in the wife’s evidence of cruelty and desertion for years were accepted.
Result: second appeal dismissed (under O.41 R.11 CPC); the husband’s suit for restitution failed; the wife kept her right to maintenance.
Principle evolved
  1. A Muslim husband has the legal right to four wives, but polygamy is tolerated, never encouraged; and he has no fundamental right to compel the first wife to share his consortium with a second wife.
  2. Restitution of conjugal rights is an equitable remedy; the court may refuse it where the plaintiff’s conduct is mala fide/inequitable; a second marriage in modern conditions raises a rebuttable presumption of cruelty to the first wife (a “continuing wrong”), shifting the onus to the husband to explain.
  3. First wife’s remedies: defence to restitution; maintenance under s.125 CrPC (2nd proviso to s.488(3) CrPC protects her when refusal is due to the second marriage); dissolution under DMMA (cruelty s.2(viii); inequitable treatment s.2(viii)(f); failure of maintenance s.2(ii)); talaq-i-tafwiz or stipulated divorce on second marriage.
Exam angle: Itwari is indispensable for answers on “restitution of conjugal rights and Muslim law”, “polygamy and cruelty to first wife”, “social perspective in administering Muslim law” and it complements s.2(viii)(f). Its reasoning anticipates later Supreme Court concerns about arbitrary polygamy/instant divorce. (Note: s.2(ix)-type equitable checks and the 2017 triple-talaq verdict complete the modern picture.)

Case 06 — Shayara Bano v. Union of India & Ors.

Supreme Court of India (Constitution Bench of five) • Decided: 22 August 2017 • (2017) 9 SCC 1 = AIR 2017 SC 4609 • Listed in DU Unit VIII case-list; text not printed in the 2023 compendium (supplemented from the reported judgment)
Tagstriple talaq — constitutional validity (6 of 7 years in DU papers 2017–2023)Art. 14 manifest arbitrarinessArt. 25 essential religious practice2019 Act
Facts
  • Shayara Bano (a Muslim woman from Uttarakhand) was married in 2002; after years of matrimonial cruelty she approached a magistrate; while proceedings were pending her husband pronounced talaq-e-biddat (triple talaq) over the telephone — instantly and irrevocably ending the marriage.
  • She filed Writ Petition (C) No. 118 of 2016 before the Supreme Court challenging the constitutional validity of triple talaq (talaq-e-biddat), and of the practices of polygamy and nikah-halala, as violative of Articles 14, 15 and 21. (Several other petitions, including the lead petition of the Bharatiya Muslim Mahila Andolan through its member, were tagged.)
  • The Union of India, numerous women’s organisations and individual petitioners pressed for invalidation; the All India Muslim Personal Law Board (AIMPLB) and others defended the practices as part of personal law protected under Article 25. The question of the constitutional validity of personal laws (framed in an earlier reference) was referred to a Constitution Bench.
Issues (as framed for the Constitution Bench)
  1. Are Muslim personal-law practices — in particular talaq-e-biddat (instant triple talaq) — amenable to Part III (fundamental rights) scrutiny?
  2. Is talaq-e-biddat an “essential religious practice” of Islam, hence protected by Article 25(1)?
  3. Is triple talaq violative of Article 14 (equality / non-arbitrariness) and Articles 15 & 21?
  4. Is s.2 of the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937 — which statutorily recognised talaq — ultra vires the Constitution (s.2 itself making triple talaq “law under Article 13”)?
  5. Interim: pending legislation, what directions should operate (status quo)?
Arguments — Petitioners / Union of India / women’s bodiesTriple talaq is not essential to Islam (it is “biddat” — an innovation, condemned by the Prophet); it is discriminatory and arbitrary — a Muslim wife can be divorced instantly for any or no reason, by phone/message, without notice, hearing or reconciliation, while a husband cannot be divorced by the wife unilaterally — violating Art. 14/15/21; personal laws are “laws in force” under Art. 13(3)(a) and must conform to Part III; even if not, state action enforcing them (courts, s.2 of the 1937 Act) brings them within review; the practice has caused extreme misery to women and children.
Arguments — AIMPLB and supporting respondentsTalaq-e-biddat is a part of Muslim personal law based on the Quran, sunna and centuries of practice (including by the Companions) and is protected by Art. 25; personal law is “law” only when enforced by the state — judicial enforcement cannot convert a religious practice into reviewable “law”; the matter is of legislative policy; the courts must not rewrite religion; reform, if any, should come from Parliament; the Muslim personal law board undertook to advise the community against triple talaq.
Judgment — a 3:2 verdict (22 August 2017)
  • Nariman & Lalit, JJ. (majority): (i) s.2 of the Shariat Act 1937 (so far as it refers to talaq) read with Art. 13(1) makes triple talaq a matter of statutory law, hence fully amenable to constitutional review; (ii) personal law enjoys no blanket immunity — the “essential religious practices” test applies; (iii) triple talaq is not an essential part of Islamic religious practice; it is manifestly arbitrary — “arbitrary and unguided” — in that it is a “form of divorce which a man may resort to capriciously and whimsically, without any attempt at reconciliation so as to save the institution of marriage”, and therefore violates Article 14; being void under Art. 13 it must be struck down. (They left polygamy and nikah-halala to the legislature; nikah-halala’s consideration question was also left open in the main petitions.)
  • Kurian Joseph, J. (majority on the result): looked first to whether triple talaq is an essential religious practice under the Quran: it is not — the Quran prescribes reasonable cause and arbitration; “talaq” in the Quran is revocable and spread over time; therefore what remains is only the sinful “talaq-e-biddat” practice, which is not protected by Art. 25 and cannot be enforced; being contrary to the Quran and law, it is void — but he refused to read down s.2 of the 1937 Act or strike down the statute itself (holding that s.2 only applies the uncodified personal law).
  • Khehar, CJI & Nazeer, J. (minority/dissent): held that the practice is protected as personal law under Art. 25 (though “sinful”), that the 1937 Act only declares personal law, and that the Court could not invalidate it; however — noting the plight of Muslim women — they directed a six-month status quo (no triple talaq during that period) to enable Parliament to legislate.
  • Final directions (majority): talaq-e-biddat (triple talaq in one sitting/instant) is declared unconstitutional and void; the majority also restrained husbands from pronouncing triple talaq for six months (until 22 February 2018) — the interim window for legislation.
Aftermath — the statute: Parliament enacted the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019 (No. 20 of 2019; enforced 1 August 2019):
  • s.3: any pronouncement of talaq by a Muslim husband upon his wife by words, spoken or written, or in electronic form or otherwise — shall be void and illegal;
  • s.4: whoever pronounces such talaq — imprisonment up to 3 years and fine;
  • ss. 5–6: the wife is entitled to subsistence allowance (for herself and dependent children) and custody of minor children, as determined by the Magistrate;
  • s.7: the offence is cognizable (on information by the wife or her blood/marriage relations), compoundable at the instance of the wife with the Magistrate’s permission, and bail cannot be granted without hearing the woman upon whom talaq was pronounced;
  • Applicable also to J&K and Ladakh since 30 October 2019; a husband so convicted is also liable to maintenance payments ordered by the court.
Principle evolved / significance
  1. Personal laws are not beyond constitutional scrutiny: when statutory force is given to a personal-law rule (e.g., via s.2, Shariat Act 1937, read with Art.13), and in the “essential religious practice” analysis under Art.25, the Supreme Court will test it against Part III.
  2. Manifest arbitrariness is a ground of Art.14 violation: an instantly operative, unilateral, reason-less and reconciliation-free mode of divorce is manifestly arbitrary.
  3. Talaq-e-biddat is not an essential religious practice of Islam; the Quranic talaq is revocable and requires arbitration — hence triple talaq is void; Parliament has criminalised it (2019 Act).
  4. With this, the classical “bad in theology but valid in law” doctrine (Rashid Ahmad PC) is dead; the Shamim Ara–Masroor procedural checks have culminated in outright prohibition.
How to answer Shayara Bano in the DU exam: (i) one-line facts; (ii) the constitutional questions; (iii) majority vs minority logic in a table (3–2); (iv) the 2019 Act provisions; (v) its link with Shamim Ara (pronouncement/communication), Masroor (one revocable talaq) and Art.25 jurisprudence; (vi) note the Court refused to strike down polygamy and nikah-halala (left to Parliament) — a frequent follow-up in viva/essays.

6.4 Talaq under Muslim law — checklist of validity (modern Indian law)

  1. Capacity: husband must be of sound mind and have attained puberty (Mulla § 308); an insane or minor husband cannot divorce.

7.2 Model Answer 1 (20 marks) — “Discuss the validity of talaq under Muslim law with reference to case laws and statutory provisions.” (capsule grid shows this topic in 6 of the 7 papers 2017–2023 — Q6/Q7/Q8(a)/Q5/Q5/Q7 across successive years — making it the most repeated 20-marker)

Plan of answer: 1. Meaning & nature → 2. Conditions/capacity → 3. Forms → 4. Statutory framework → 5. Case-law evolution → 6. Triple talaq & constitutional invalidation → 7. Conclusion.

1. Meaning and nature. Under Muslim law marriage (nikah) is a civil contract, and talaq is the unilateral repudiation of that contract by the husband — a mode peculiar to Islamic law (Zohara Khatoon v. Mohd. Ibrahim, (1981) 2 SCC 509). Islam regards divorce as “the most disliked of lawful things” (Prophet), permissible only when the spouses cannot live “within the limits of Allah.”

2. Capacity and formal validity. A talaq can be pronounced by a Muslim husband of sound mind who has attained puberty (Mulla, §308); it may be oral or by a written talaknama (§310); words may be express (saheeh) or ambiguous (kinayat, requiring proof of intention). Under Hanafi law no witnesses or particular formula are required; under Ithna Ashari law it must be oral in Arabic before two competent witnesses, and divorce in jest, anger or intoxication is invalid.

3. Forms. (i) Talaq-e-ahsan — a single pronouncement in a tuhr followed by abstinence through the iddat; revocable during iddat, irrevocable on its expiry. (ii) Talaq-e-hasan — one pronouncement in each of three successive tuhrs; the first two revocable, the third irrevocable. (iii) Talaq-e-biddat — three pronouncements at one sitting (triple talaq) or a single pronouncement showing intent to divorce irrevocably; classical Hanafi law called it “bad in theology but valid in law”, effective instantly — a view traceable to the Privy Council in Saiyid Rashid Ahmad v. Mt. Anisa Khatun, AIR 1932 PC 25 (a DU Unit-VII case, casebook pp. 233–236).

4. Statutory framework. s.2, Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937 (quoted in full in Masroor Ahmed) recognises dissolution “including talaq, ila, zihar, lian, khula and mubaraat”; the DMMA 1939 gives the wife judicial remedies; the 2019 Act later criminalised triple talaq.

5. Judicial evolution — the modern conditions of validity. The Gauhati High Court in Jiauddin Ahmed v. Anwara Begum and Rukia Khatun v. Abdul Khalique Laskar (1981) held the Quranic law to be: (i) talaq must be for a reasonable cause; (ii) it must be preceded by an attempt at reconciliation through two arbiters, one from each family. In Shamim Ara v. State of U.P. (2002), the Supreme Court approved these rulings and held that a talaq, to be effective, must be pronounced; a mere plea of prior divorce in a written statement or a self-serving affidavit in non-inter-partes proceedings cannot operate as divorce; the husband must prove pronouncement by evidence. In Masroor Ahmed v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2007), the Delhi High Court added: (i) a triple talaq, even for a Sunni, operates as one revocable talaq; (ii) talaq in extreme anger is invalid; (iii) communication to the wife is an essential ingredient — the talaq is effective from communication and takes no effect if never communicated; (iv) reconciliation may follow pronouncement in a revocable talaq but must precede an irrevocable one.

6. Triple talaq — from valid to void. In Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017), a five-judge Constitution Bench by a 3:2 majority (Nariman & Lalit, JJ., with Kurian Joseph, J. concurring in the result; Khehar CJI & Nazeer, J. dissenting) held talaq-e-biddat unconstitutional: it is not an essential religious practice under Art.25; being arbitrary and unguided it is manifestly arbitrary and violates Art.14 (read with s.2, Shariat Act 1937 as “law” under Art.13). The Court gave Parliament six months (status quo). Parliament responded with the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019: any pronouncement of talaq by words, spoken/written/electronic, is void and illegal (s.3); the husband is punishable with up to 3 years’ imprisonment and fine (s.4); the wife gets subsistence allowance and custody of minor children (ss.5–6); the offence is cognizable (on the wife’s or relatives’ information), compoundable at her instance with the magistrate’s permission, and bail requires hearing her first (s.7).

7. Conclusion. The law of talaq has travelled from arbitrary unilateralism to a regulated, Quran-informed and constitutionalised regime: pronouncement, reasonable cause, reconciliation, communication and the revocability window now guard against caprice, while instant triple talaq is altogether void and penal. The trend reflects the vision of Krishna Iyer, J. in Yousuf Rawther that “the whimsical and capricious divorce by the husband is good in law though bad in theology” is a fallacy founded on an un-Islamic concept of women as chattel.

7.3 Model Answer 2 (10/20 marks) — “State the grounds on which a Muslim wife can obtain a judicial divorce under the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939.” (10-marker in the DU papers — capsule row 22, listed as Q8(b); often paired in the same paper with the 20-mark “validity of talaq” question)

1. Introduction. The DMMA 1939 (Act VIII of 1939) was enacted to consolidate and clarify the law and to remove doubts as to the effect of renunciation of Islam by a married Muslim woman. Its SOR records that Hanafi law gave no relief to a wife whose husband neglected to maintain her, maltreated her, absconded, or was imprisoned, while the Hanafi jurists themselves permitted application of Maliki (or Shafi‘i/Hanbali) law in hardship cases. The Act made such beneficial provisions available to women of all schools.

2. Section 2 — the grounds. A woman married under Muslim law is entitled to a decree of dissolution on any one or more of the following grounds — (i) whereabouts of the husband unknown for 4 years; (ii) neglect or failure to maintain her for 2 years (need not be wilful; the Kerala HC in Yousuf Rawther v. Sowramma held even a wife living apart may sue); (iii) husband sentenced to imprisonment for 7 years or upwards (final sentence); (iv) failure without reasonable cause to perform marital obligations for 3 years; (v) impotence at marriage continuing so (one-year potency test); (vi) insanity for 2 years or virulent venereal disease (leprosy deleted in 2019); (vii) option of puberty — marriage by father or other guardian before 15, repudiated before 18, marriage not consummated (Ghulam Sakina v. Falak Sher, AIR 1950 Lah 45); (viii) cruelty — defined inclusively: habitual assault or conduct making life miserable even without physical hurt; associating with women of evil repute; forcing an immoral life; dealing with her property illegally; obstructing religious observance; or, where the husband has more wives than one, failing to treat her equitably per the Quran; (ix) any other ground recognised by Muslim law (residuary — e.g., judicial khula, lian).

3. Procedural protections (provisos). decree on (iii) only after the sentence is final; decree on (i) inoperative for six months and set aside if the husband appears and undertakes conjugal duties; on (v) the husband gets one year to prove cessation of impotence; s.3 requires notice to the husband’s prospective heirs in (i)-suits.

4. Related provisions. s.4 — apostasy of the wife no longer automatically dissolves the marriage (she may sue on s.2 grounds); s.5 — the wife’s right to dower is unaffected. Judicial khula via s.2(ix) is the modern instrument for a wife whose husband withholds consent to khula (Yousuf Rawther).

5. Conclusion. The Act transformed the wife’s position from near-total dependence on the husband’s whim to a statutory bundle of no-fault and fault grounds, and its liberal construction (as directed in Yousuf Rawther) makes it the principal gateway of judicial divorce for Muslim women in India.

7.4 Model Answer 3 (10 marks) — “Option of puberty under Hindu and Muslim law.” (10-marker — capsule row 20, listed as Q3(a))

Meaning. “Option of puberty” (khyar-ul-bulugh in Muslim law) is the right of a minor whose marriage was contracted by a guardian to repudiate the marriage upon attaining puberty — a recognition that a marriage requires the free consent of the parties and that a child cannot give it.

Muslim law. Before the DMMA 1939, a minor girl given in marriage by her father or father’s father had no option to repudiate — the father’s contract was binding; but a marriage by other guardians could be repudiated on puberty. The 1939 Act changed this. Under s.2(vii) a woman married under Muslim law may obtain a decree of dissolution where “she, having been given in marriage by her father or other guardian before she attained the age of fifteen years, repudiated the marriage before attaining the age of eighteen years: Provided that the marriage has not been consummated.” In Ghulam Sakina v. Falak Sher Allah Bakhsh (AIR 1950 Lah 45), the girl was about one year old at her nikah (1932) performed by her father; she sued in 1945 before turning 18. The Lahore HC held: (i) the father’s contract now stands on no higher footing than any other guardian’s; (ii) puberty is presumed at 15 in the absence of evidence; (iii) cohabitation during minority does not destroy the option — assent or ratification must come after puberty, and even consummation before 15 does not bar repudiation; (iv) refusal of medical examination is no proof of consummation; (v) a suit is a valid mode of repudiation. Decree for dissolution was restored.

Hindu law. Section 13(2)(iv), Hindu Marriage Act 1955: a wife may petition for divorce “where the marriage was solemnised before she attained the age of fifteen years and she has repudiated the marriage after attaining that age but before attaining the age of eighteen years”, provided the marriage was not consummated after she attained fifteen. The concept entered Hindu law as a statutory reform to emancipate child-brides; PCMA 2006 and the HMA amendments also make child marriages voidable on similar lines (Unit-I reading).

Comparison. Both systems protect the child-bride and require (a) marriage in minority (below 15 in both statutes), (b) repudiation within a window (Muslim: repudiation before 18, marriage not consummated at all; Hindu: repudiation after 15 and before 18, no consummation after 15), and (c) court intervention (decree of dissolution / divorce). The Muslim rule of s.2(vii) is available regardless of who the guardian was — a post-1939 equality the classical Hanafi law did not allow.

7.5 Model Answer 4 (20 marks, variant) — “Khula and Mubaraat as modes of extra-judicial divorce. Can a Muslim wife obtain a divorce against the wishes of her husband?”

Khula. A dissolution of marriage with the consent and at the instance of the wife in which she gives or agrees to give a consideration to the husband for her release (Mulla §319). It traces to the celebrated case of Jameela, wife of Thabit-ibn-Quais, decided by the Prophet himself (Bukhari): she returned the garden given as her settlement and the Prophet told Thabit to “take your garden and divorce her at once.” Khula requires an offer by the wife (typically to surrender her mahr) and acceptance by the husband; once accepted it operates as a single irrevocable (bain) divorce; iddat is obligatory; subject to contrary terms the wife's dower stands released, but the husband must still maintain her during iddat and maintain the children (Mulla §320). Under modern Indian law, as stated in Masroor Ahmed, khula is “a divorce which proceeds from the wife which the husband cannot refuse subject only to reasonable negotiation” about her offer — it is a no-fault, no-reason mode, quite common in India.

Can the wife obtain divorce against the husband’s will? (i) Where the husband unreasonably refuses khula, the wife may sue under s.2(ix), DMMA 1939 (“any other ground recognised as valid for the dissolution of marriages under Muslim law”) read with the classical khula doctrine — judicial khula. In A. Yousuf Rawther v. Sowramma, Krishna Iyer, J. described khula as “the last gateway for a Muslim woman out of an irreparably embittered co-existence” and held that s.2(ix) is a statutory preservation of prior Islamic rights (though on the facts he rested the decree on s.2(ii)). (ii) She may also exercise a delegated power — talaq-i-tafwiz — where the nikah-nama so provides (recognised in Masroor; enforced in Badu Mia; and per Ayatunnessa the option survives as the second marriage is a “continuing wrong”). (iii) She can always invoke the statutory grounds of s.2 (maintenance failure, cruelty, impotence, option of puberty etc.), which do not require the husband’s consent. Thus the husband’s consent is not indispensable — his unreasonable refusal only shifts the forum from extra-judicial khula to the court.

Mubaraat. Where aversion is mutual, either spouse may offer and the other accept a release — no consideration is required because both desire separation (Masroor; Mulla §319). It operates as bain divorce; iddat follows. Distinguish: khula originates from the wife with consideration; mubaraat is mutual without consideration; talaq is the husband's unilateral act with no consideration (comparison chart in §6.1 above).

9.2 Glossary of Arabic terms

  • Nikah — Muslim marriage, a civil contract.
  • Talaq — repudiation of marriage by the husband.
  • Tuhr — period of purity between two menstruations.
  • Iddat — waiting period of the wife after divorce/widowhood.
  • Mahr (dower) — sum payable by husband to wife.
  • Rajai / Bain — revocable / irrevocable divorce.
  • Khula — divorce at the wife’s instance with consideration (typically dower).
  • Mubaraat — divorce by mutual consent, no consideration.
  • Ila / Zihar — divorce by vow of abstinence / by injurious comparison (virtually extinct).
  • Lian — dissolution on unproved accusation of adultery, with oaths.
  • Faskh — judicial annulment/dissolution (as under DMMA).
  • Halala — intervening marriage enabling remarriage after third divorce (must occur naturally).
  • Talaq-i-tafwiz — husband’s delegated power to the wife to divorce herself.
  • Talaknama — deed/document of divorce.
  • Khyar-ul-bulugh — option of puberty.
  • Saheeh / Kinayat — express / ambiguous words of divorce.
  • Ijma / Qiyas / Ijtihad / Fiqh — consensus / analogy / juristic reasoning / jurisprudence.
  • Fatwa / Qaza (qazi) — advisory opinion / binding judgment.
  • Zina — unlawful sexual intercourse (adultery).
  • Shariat Act — MPL(S) Application Act, 1937 (s.2 lists modes).

9.3 Ten exam-day tips (Unit VIII)

  1. Begin any talaq answer with “marriage in Islam is a civil contract”.
  2. Quote s.2, Shariat Act 1937 — it lists all six extra-judicial modes.
  3. Never write “triple talaq is valid” — after 2017 it is void; after 2019, criminal.
  4. Know the difference between proviso-less s.2(ii) and “without reasonable cause” s.2(iv) of DMMA — a favourite exam nuance (Yousuf Rawther).
  5. For cruelty (s.2(viii)), reproduce all six sub-clauses (a)–(f), especially (f) — unequal treatment of co-wives.
  6. For option of puberty quote the three conditions + Ghulam Sakina’s “minority cohabitation does not destroy the option”.
  7. Mention s.4 and s.5 of the DMMA — apostasy and dower — they often get ignored.
  8. In RCR answers use Itwari: “right to polygamy is tolerated, not encouraged; equity refuses to compel”.
  9. Link case to case: Rashid Ahmad (PC, old law) → Shamim Ara → Masroor → Shayara Bano — the examiner rewards trajectory answers.
  10. Always end with the position “today” (2019 Act; Art.14 scrutiny) and a one-sentence social-justice note (Art.15(3); Bai Tahira).
