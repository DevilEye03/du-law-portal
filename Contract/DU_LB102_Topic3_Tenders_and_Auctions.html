2 The Law of Tenders — invitation, offer, acceptance & the standing offer

The classic DU framework: a tender is an offer; its “acceptance” may or may not create an immediate contract.

2.1 The four-stage structure of a tender

  • Stage 1 — Invitation to tender: the advertisement/notice inviting tenders is only an invitation to treat (it invites offers), not an offer. A person cannot be bound merely because he “accepts” an invitation.
  • Stage 2 — Submission of the tender: each tender is an offer (S.2(a)) made by the tenderer, capable of acceptance and revocable before acceptance (S.5).
  • Stage 3 — Acceptance of the tender: the effect depends on the invitation’s terms — this is the crux of Union of India v. Maddala Thathiah (see the two cases below).
  • Stage 4 — Formal order / contract: where a formal order is contemplated (as in Government contracts), the real contract may arise only when an order is placed; the “acceptance” of a tender for a maximum quantity creates only a standing offer.

2.2 Tender accepted for a definite quantity — immediate contract

Cheshire & Fifoot, Law of Contract (5th ed., p. 36), quoted in Maddala Thathiah:
Case 1: “the corporation may have stated that it will definitely require a specific quantity… here the ‘acceptance’ of the tender is an acceptance in the legal sense, and it creates an obligation. The trader is bound to deliver, the corporation is bound to accept [the full quantity], and the fact that delivery is to be by instalments as and when demanded does not disturb the existence of the obligation.”

2.3 Tender for a maximum quantity (“if and when required”) — the standing offer

Cheshire & Fifoot, ibid., in Maddala Thathiah:
Case 2: “the corporation advertises that it may require articles… up to a maximum amount… The trader has made what is called a standing offer. Until revocation he stands ready and willing to deliver… The ‘acceptance’ of the tender, however, does not convert the offer into a binding contract… the corporation has not agreed to take… any quantity… It has merely stated that it may require supplies up to a maximum limit.”
“In this latter case the standing offer may be revoked at any time provided that it has not been accepted in the legal sense, and acceptance… is complete as soon as a requisition for a definite quantity of goods is made. Each requisition… is an individual act of acceptance which creates a separate contract.”
Standing offer — the examination point. A “standing offer” (a tender to supply up to a maximum if and when required) is not a binding contract in itself. It is an offer that the offeree (the buyer) may accept from time to time by placing orders; each order creates a separate contract. Until an order is placed, the tenderer may revoke the standing offer (subject to contractual terms to keep it open, which require consideration). This doctrine explains Maddala Thathiah.

2.4 Government tenders & the right to cancel

Government and statutory bodies frequently reserve, in the tender, a right to cancel an outstanding contract or to reject any tender. The validity and effect of such a clause must be construed in the light of the whole tender and the stage reached. If a formal order for a definite quantity has been placed, the administration is generally bound to accept the goods ordered; the cancellation clause covers only such contemplated supplies for which no order has yet been placed (Maddala Thathiah). Courts are normally reluctant to interfere in the award of Government contracts but will set aside an award that is unreasonable, arbitrary, mala fide, or in disregard of mandatory procedure (Kanhaiya Lal Aggarwal; also Tata Cellular v. Union of India, AIR 1996 SC 11).

2.5 Essentials of a valid tender (per the note in the DU material)

Requisites of a valid tender — as noted after Maddala Thathiah (from Tata Cellular v. Union of India) 1. It must be unconditional. · 2. Made at the proper place. · 3. Must conform to the terms of the obligation. · 4. Made at the proper time. · 5. Made in the proper form. · 6. The tenderer must be able and willing to perform. · 7. There must be a reasonable opportunity for inspection. · 8. Made to the proper person. · 9. It must be of the full amount.

2.6 Essential v. collateral conditions & withdrawal of tender

  • Essential vs collateral conditions: whether a tender condition is essential or collateral is judged by the consequence of non-compliance — if non-fulfilment leads to rejection, it is essential; otherwise it is only collateral (G.J. Fernandez v. State of Karnataka, AIR 1990 SC 958, applied in Kanhaiya Lal).
  • Withdrawal of a tender before opening/acceptance: a tenderer may withdraw his tender before it is accepted, and no term in the tender notice can take away that legal right; once withdrawn there is no offer and hence no contract (Rajendra Kumar Verma v. State of M.P.).

3 The Law of Auctions

When do bids and the fall of the hammer create a binding contract?

3.1 The ordinary (English & Indian) rules of auction sale

  • Notice of auction / putting up lots: only an invitation to treat, not an offer.
  • Each bid by a bidder is an offer (S.2(a)); it may be withdrawn before acceptance.
  • Fall of the hammer is the acceptance, ordinarily creating a concluded contract at once.
  • With a reserve price: the auctioneer may refuse the highest (or any) bid below the reserve; the sale is not complete until the hammer falls on an acceptable bid.
  • By the highest bidder: if the auction is expressly “without reserve” or “to the highest bidder,” special rules may apply, but generally the auctioneer is not bound to accept any bid.

3.2 Auction “subject to confirmation / approval” — provisional acceptance

In Government and forest auctions the acceptance of a bid is frequently subject to confirmation by a higher authority. The legal effect is crucial (this is the heart of Haridwar Singh v. Bagun Sumbrui):

  • A bid accepted “subject to confirmation” is only a conditional/provisional acceptance — it does not, by itself, conclude a contract.
  • Whether a binding contract arises on later confirmation depends on whether the confirmation was given and communicated; and on whether the offer was actually accepted on the same terms.
  • A person who makes an offer may withdraw it before acceptance (in the absence of a supported condition to keep it open); a provisional/conditional acceptance cannot itself create a binding bargain (Somasundaram Pillai v. Provincial Govt. of Madras).
  • Following Williston on Contracts (as quoted in the DU material), distinguish: (1) an “acceptance” that introduces a new condition — this is a counter-offer; from (2) an acceptance that unequivocally adopts the offer but states it will only become effective on a future contingency — an “acceptance in escrow” — neither party is bound, and each may withdraw until the contingency occurs.

3.3 When is the contract concluded in a confirmation-auction?

A concluded contract requires (i) a definite offer, (ii) an acceptance of that same offer by the competent authority, and (iii) communication (or dispatch in course of transmission) of that acceptance to the offeror. If the “confirmation” merely accepts a different, later offer (e.g., an offer to take the property at the reserve price rather than the original bid), and that acceptance is never communicated, then no contract is concluded — and the bidder may also have revoked his later offer (Haridwar Singh).

Key takeaway: an auction “subject to confirmation” gives the bidder no enforceable contract merely by being the highest bidder; the Government must confirm and communicate the acceptance of a subsisting offer. This protects the public authority but means the bidder is not locked in until then.

CASE 8 · Union of India v. Maddala Thathiah
AIR 1966 SC 1724 (Supreme Court of India)
Tender = offerStanding offerRight to cancel clauseEach order = separate contract
Hypothetical scene — the railway jaggery tender
Fig C8 — The tender/standing-offer doctrine: an order for a definite quantity creates a binding contract.
Facts

The Dominion of India, as owner of the Madras & Southern Mahratta Railway (acting through the General Manager), invited tenders for the supply of jaggery to railway grain shops. The respondent (Thathiah) tendered to supply 14,000 imperial maunds of cane jaggery for February–March 1948. The tender form’s para 2 contained a note reserving to the administration the right “to cancel the contract at any stage during the tenure of the contract without calling up the outstandings on the unexpired portion.” By a letter of 29 Jan 1948 the Deputy General Manager “accepted” the tender and asked for a security deposit of ₹7,900, adding that on receipt of the remittance a formal order would be placed. By a letter of 16 Feb 1948 the DGM reiterated acceptance, subject to the respondent’s acceptance of terms printed on the reverse — the supply was divided into four instalments of 3,500 maunds on stated dates (later slightly changed by a letter of 28 Feb). On 8 Mar 1948 the DGM wrote that the balance of jaggery outstanding under the order of 16 Feb was “treated as cancelled and the contract closed.” Despite protests, the railway relied on the reserved right to cancel. The respondent sued for damages for breach. The trial court dismissed the suit; the High Court held the cancellation clause void and remanded on damages. The Union appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issue(s)
  • Whether the arrangement was a binding contract for the supply of the whole 14,000 maunds, or merely an agreement to supply such quantity as the railway might order (a standing offer).
  • Whether the reserved right to cancel allowed the railway to escape liability for instalments already the subject of a formal order.
Arguments for the Union (appellant)
  • The railway agreed to buy only such jaggery as it required, up to 14,000 maunds — hence no enforceable obligation to take the whole.
  • The respondent had expressly agreed to the cancellation clause, which was a valid stipulation providing for discharge/determination of the contract.
Arguments for the respondent (Thathiah)
  • The contract was a complete one for a definite 14,000 maunds deliverable on the dates in the order of 16 Feb / 28 Feb.
  • The cancellation clause was repugnant to the contract; even if valid, the railway could rescind only for good and reasonable grounds, not arbitrarily.
Decision

Appeal dismissed — for the respondent (Raghubar Dayal J.). The High Court’s order stood.

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • A tender is an offer. But its “acceptance” creates a binding contract only if it relates to a definite quantity the buyer is bound to take; where the buyer merely states it may require up to a maximum, the “acceptance” creates only a standing offer, not a contract of sale.
  • Applying the Cheshire & Fifoot two-case test: the instant arrangement was of the second (standing-offer) type — a formal order was contemplated (paras 8–9 of the tender).
  • The cancellation clause governed only contemplated supplies for which no formal order had yet been placed; once a formal order for a definite quantity on definite dates was placed, it constituted a binding contract — the supplier must deliver and the railway must accept that supply.
  • Hence the railway could not arbitrarily cancel the deliveries covered by the placed orders.
Per the Court (adopting Cheshire & Fifoot): “each requisition by the offeree is an individual act of acceptance which creates a separate contract.” The standing offer may be revoked only until so accepted.
CASE 9 · Rajendra Kumar Verma v. State of Madhya Pradesh
AIR 1972 MP 131 (Madhya Pradesh High Court, Bishambhar Dayal CJ)
Tender revocable before acceptanceWithdrawal of tenderS.5 ICA
Hypothetical scene — withdrawing the tendu-leaf tender
Fig C9 — A tender (offer) may be withdrawn before acceptance; a tender-notice clause cannot deprive the tenderer of this right.
Facts

The State of M.P. advertised for tenders for the sale of Tendu-patta (leaves) from unit No. 7, Budni. The petitioner gave a tender at ₹38.25 per standard bag and deposited security. The tenders were to be opened on 9 April 1969, but before they were opened the petitioner applied to withdraw/ resile from his tender, asking that it not be opened at all. The tender was nevertheless opened — it was the only tender for that unit — and the Government accepted it. When the petitioner refused to execute the purchaser’s agreement, recovery proceedings were taken for ₹24,846.12 on the ground that the leaves had later been sold to someone else at a loss and the balance was recoverable from him. He filed a writ petition under Art. 226 challenging the recovery.

Issue

Whether the petitioner could validly withdraw his tender before it was opened/ accepted, and whether the tender condition restricting withdrawal took away that right, so that the recovery was unsustainable.

Arguments for the petitioner
  • He withdrew his tender before it was opened and accepted — hence there was no subsisting offer by him and no contract.
Arguments for the State (respondent)
  • Under tender condition 10(b)(i) a tenderer may withdraw only if, after withdrawal, at least one valid tender remains for the unit; since his was the only tender, withdrawal was not permitted.
Decision

Writ allowed — recovery quashed; security refunded.

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • A person who makes an offer (a tender is an offer) is entitled to withdraw it before its acceptance is intimated to him (S.5 ICA).
  • The Government, by merely providing a clause in the tender notice, could not take away that legal right of the tenderer.
  • Since the petitioner had validly withdrawn, when the tenders were opened there was in truth no offer by him; accordingly there could be no contract, express or implied, between the parties, and no liability for the alleged loss.
Bishambhar Dayal CJ: “A person who makes an offer is entitled to withdraw his offer or tender before its acceptance is intimated to him. The Government, by merely providing such a clause in [the] tender notice, could not take away that legal right.”
CASE 10 · Kanhaiya Lal Aggarwal v. Union of India
AIR 2002 SC 2766 (Supreme Court of India, S. Rajendra Babu J.)
Government contractsRebate/conditional offer in tenderEssential vs collateral conditions
Hypothetical scene — the rail-ballast tender & the rebate offer
Fig C10 — An offer (with a rebate for early acceptance) made with the tender is valid; essential vs collateral conditions and fairness govern Government contract awards.
Facts

The Railway invited tenders for five items of work, including supply, delivery and stacking of 75,000 m³ of machine-crushed track ballast at its Naurozabad depot over 24 months. The tender notice required rates to be stated in words and figures, rejected tenders with alterations, permitted only signed corrections, required the offer to remain open for a minimum 90 days from opening, and provided for forfeiture of security and rejection for non-compliance. The appellant (Kanhaiya Lal Aggarwal) submitted his tender on 27-2-2001 with a covering letter offering a rebate if his offer was accepted quickly — 5% within 45 days, 3% within 60 days, 2% within 75 days. Respondent No. 5 also offered a rebate but only after the tenders had been opened (1.25% if accepted in 30 days, 1% in 45 days). The Railway accepted the appellant’s tender subject to the rebate and an agreement was executed on 19-4-2001. Respondent No. 5 filed a writ claiming his tender should have been accepted as his base rates were the lowest. The single judge and the Division Bench of the High Court, holding that the rebate amounted to an impermissible alteration of the tender and that all tenderers must be treated equally, preferred respondent No. 5 and ordered fresh/alternative sourcing. The appellant appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issue(s)
  • Whether the Railway could accept the appellant’s tender accompanied by a rebate offer, consistent with the tender conditions.
  • Whether the essential-v-collateral test and the duty to treat tenderers fairly and equally governed the award.
Arguments for the appellant
  • The rebate was part of the tender submitted on time; rates were clear and the acceptance time clear; it did not militate against the tender terms.
  • Respondent No. 5’s reduction came after opening — an offer of no consequence.
Arguments for respondent No. 5
  • His rates were the lowest; the appellant’s rebate was a condition altering the tender documents (impermissible); all tenderers must be treated equally.
Decision

Appeals allowed — order of the High Court set aside; the writ petition dismissed.

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • Courts are normally reluctant to interfere in the award of Government contracts, but will set aside awards that are unreasonable, arbitrary, mala fide or in disregard of mandatory procedure.
  • Essential v. collateral condition: when a tender condition is not complied with, the inviter may reject; whether the condition is essential or collateral depends on the consequence of non-compliance — if it leads to rejection it is essential, else collateral (G.J. Fernandez).
  • The rebate was offered with the tender itself, gave clear reduced rates for clear early-acceptance windows, and did not militate against the (90-day keep-open) tender terms; it was merely commercial prudence and an inducement to accept expeditiously. It was therefore within scope.
  • The appellant complied with the 90-day condition. Respondent No. 5’s rebate, made only after opening, was of no consequence. Accepting the appellant’s offer was neither illegal nor arbitrary.
S. Rajendra Babu J.: an offer of concessional rates for quick finalisation is “part of commercial prudence”; as long as it does not militate against the terms of inviting the tender, “it cannot be said that such offer is not within its scope.”
CASE 11 · Haridwar Singh v. Bagun Sumbrui
(1973) 3 SCC 889 (Supreme Court of India, K.K. Mathew J.)
Auction subject to confirmationProvisional acceptance ≠ contractCommunication of acceptanceWithdrawal of offer
Hypothetical scene — the bamboo coup auction (a chronology)
Fig C11 — An auction subject to confirmation gives no binding contract until the competent authority accepts a subsisting offer AND communicates that acceptance.
Facts

The Bihar Forest Department advertised for settlement of the right to exploit the “Bantha Bamboo coup” (Chatra North Division, Hazaribagh) by public auction held on 7 August 1970. Five persons, including the appellant, bid. Though the reserve price was ₹95,000, the appellant’s bid of ₹92,001 (the highest) was accepted by the Divisional Forest Officer (DFO). The appellant deposited ₹23,800 security and executed an agreement. Because the provisional settlement exceeded ₹5,000, the papers were forwarded for Government confirmation; and because it was below the reserve, the matter also went to the Finance Department. The DFO explained the lower settlement. On 26 October 1970 the appellant expressed willingness to take the settlement at the reserve price of ₹95,000; on 3 November 1970 he applied for settlement on his highest bid. On 27 November the Minister of Forests directed settlement with the highest bidder at the reserve price; a telegram was sent on 28 November to the Conservator confirming the sale — but the DFO did not receive/communicate it to the appellant. Meanwhile Md. Yakub (respondent No. 6) on 4 December offered ₹1,01,125. The Government cancelled its earlier telegram and, by proceedings of 13 December 1970, cancelled the settlement with the appellant and settled with Md. Yakub. The appellant filed a writ.

Issue

Whether there was a concluded contract between the appellant and the Government (so that the later settlement with Md. Yakub was illegal), given that the DFO’s acceptance was subject to confirmation by the Government.

Arguments for the appellant
  • The DFO’s acceptance was a conditional acceptance, which became unconditional on Government confirmation (27 Nov) — so a concluded contract arose even though confirmation was not communicated to him.
  • Alternatively, the settlement with Md. Yakub violated statutory rules and was invalid.
Arguments for the Government/respondents
  • No concluded contract: the DFO’s acceptance was only provisional/subject to confirmation; the Minister’s 27 Nov proceedings did not confirm the ₹92,001 bid but accepted a different (26 Oct) offer at ₹95,000; that acceptance was never communicated to the appellant; and the appellant himself revoked that offer on 3 Nov.
Decision

No concluded contract between the appellant and the Government; the appeal was disposed of without disturbing the settlement with Md. Yakub (no order as to costs).

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • A conditional/provisional acceptance (subject to a higher authority’s confirmation) does not by itself create a binding contract; there must be an unconditional acceptance of the offer.
  • The Court considered Rajanagaram Village Co-op. Society v. Veerasami Mudaly and Somasundaram Pillai: a person who makes an offer may withdraw it before acceptance in the absence of a supported contrary condition; a provisional acceptance cannot make a binding bargain.
  • Following Williston, where an acceptance is not to take effect until a future contingency, neither party is bound and either may withdraw; if the contingency occurs, a contract arises.
  • Here the impediment was want of confirmation by the Government, not merely want of communication: the Minister accepted the different offer of 26 Oct (₹95,000), not the bid of ₹92,001; and that acceptance was not communicated to the appellant (the telegram went to the Conservator, and was not even put into course of transmission to the appellant). Further, the appellant revoked his offer on 3 Nov.
  • Principle: an auction “subject to confirmation” yields no concluded contract until the competent authority confirms a subsisting offer and that acceptance is communicated (or at least put in course of transmission) to the bidder.
The Court: the want of confirmation by the Government — not the want of communication — was decisive; and “the appellant cannot contend that there was a concluded contract… as the acceptance of that offer was not put in the course of transmission [to him].”
CASE 12 · Indian Airlines Corporation v. Sm. Madhuri Chowdhuri
AIR 1965 Cal. 252 (Calcutta High Court, P.B. Mukharji J.)
Standard-form / ticket contractExemption clauseCommon carrierS.23 ICA does not apply to carriers
Hypothetical scene — the ill-fated flight & the ticket’s fine print
Fig C12 — A standard-form ticket is a contract on the carrier’s printed terms; its exemption clause (if duly brought to notice and not barred by statute) is valid.
Facts

On 12 December 1953 a Dakota aircraft (VT-CHF) of the Indian Airlines Corporation crashed at Nagpur shortly after taking off for Madras; all aboard were killed except the pilot, Captain Cartner. One passenger, Sunil Baran Chowdhuri, a young Calcutta businessman, died. His widow and two minor children sued the Corporation for damages, relying both on breach of contract (failure safely to carry) and on negligence (defective supervision/check-up; the pilots’ handling of the engine failure), claiming roughly ₹20,00,000 for loss of dependency plus ₹5,000 lost in cash/kind. The Corporation relied on the exemption clause in the passenger’s air ticket, which purported to exclude all liability — even for the carrier’s or its servants’ negligence — for death, injury or delay. The trial judge held the exemption clause illegal, invalid and void (inter alia under S.23 ICA) and found the pilot negligent, so the Corporation was liable. The Corporation appealed.

Issue(s)
  • Whether the exemption clause in the air ticket (excluding liability even for negligence) is valid, or void as being opposed to S.23 ICA / public policy.
  • Whether the Indian Contract Act applies at all to a common carrier’s contract of carriage.
  • Whether the Corporation or the pilot was negligent on the facts.
Arguments for the plaintiffs (respondents)
  • The clause is void under S.23 ICA (opposed to public policy / unjust).
  • It cannot bind the heirs/legal representatives, who were not parties to the contract (action under the Fatal Accidents Act).
  • The broad principles of the Warsaw Convention (applied as justice, equity and good conscience) should strike it down.
  • Even if goods could be so exempted, a clause excluding liability for loss of life is bad.
Arguments for the Corporation (appellant)
  • The ICA does not govern common carriers at all (Privy Council in Irrawaddy Flotilla Co.); hence S.23 does not apply.
  • Under the common law a carrier may contract out of liability, even for negligence, if the clause is clear and duly brought to the passenger’s notice.
  • There was no negligence; the pilots exercised reasonable judgment in an emergency.
Decision

Appeal allowed — for the Corporation; the suit dismissed.

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • The ICA does not govern common carriers. Relying on the Privy Council in Irrawaddy Flotilla Co. v. Bugwandas (18 IA 121), the liability of a common carrier arises from public employment, not contract, and is outside the Indian Contract Act — so S.23 cannot be invoked to strike down the clause.
  • Following Indian Airlines v. Keshavlal F. Gandhi (Cal.), the Corporation is a common carrier; its relationship with passengers is governed by the common law, which permits carriers to contract out of liability — including for negligence — through a clear, duly-notified clause.
  • No distinction in law between carriage of goods and carriage of passengers: a carrier of passengers “is neither an insurer nor precluded from making a special contract” (Ludditt v. Ginger Coote Airways; Grand Trunk Ry. v. Robinson).
  • Notice/consent: a condition printed on the ticket binds the passenger if the carrier did what was reasonably sufficient to bring it to his notice (Parker v. S.E. Ry.; Hood v. Anchor Line). Here the conditions were duly brought to the passenger’s notice and he had every opportunity to know them.
  • The clause clearly covered negligence and was not barred by any statute (the Carriage by Air Act / Warsaw Convention did not apply to internal carriage; no Indian statute prohibited such exemption). On the merits, there was no negligence by the Corporation or the pilot.
Per the Court: “On this overwhelming mass of authority we are bound to hold that both in respect of Contract Act and Tort the present exemption clause is good and valid and it legally excludes all liability for negligence. We also hold that it cannot be held to be bad under Section 23 of the Contract Act.”
Modern significance & the two-pronged rule on exemption clauses

The case is authority for two things that recur in later consumer/aviation law: (i) a standard-form ticket contract is binding on the terms printed on it, provided the terms were reasonably brought to the passenger’s notice and he accepted the ticket; and (ii) such exemption clauses are construed strictly — following Denning LJ in White v. John Warwick & Co. (quoted in the case), a clause is construed, so far as possible, as excluding only strict liability and not negligence, unless the words clearly exclude negligence. In India today, statutes (e.g., the Carriage by Air Act amendments, consumer-protection law) may restrict such clauses; the common-law freedom to contract out is subject to those statutory interventions.

20 MARKS

Whether acceptance of A’s tender by a Government Department for supply of coal up to 10,000 tons during the calendar year 2012 creates contractual obligations? Discuss with the help of the Indian Contract Act and case law. (DU, Dec 2011)

Model answer —

Setting. In a tender process, the advertisement inviting tenders is only an invitation to treat; each tender submitted is an offer (S.2(a)); and the legal effect of the buyer’s “acceptance” depends on the terms of the invitation.

The controlling principle (Union of India v. Maddala Thathiah, AIR 1966 SC 1724). Adopting the Cheshire & Fifoot analysis, the Court distinguished two situations: (i) where the buyer states it definitely requires a specific quantity — acceptance of the tender is an acceptance “in the legal sense” creating a binding obligation to take the whole quantity; and (ii) where the buyer states it “may require” goods up to a maximum amount, “if and when required” — acceptance of the tender creates only a standing offer. In the second case, the standing offer may be revoked until it is “accepted in the legal sense,” i.e., until a requisition for a definite quantity is made; each such requisition is an individual act of acceptance creating a separate contract.

Application. Here A’s tender is “for supply of coal up to 10,000 tons during the year 2012” — i.e., a maximum-quantity, “if and when required” arrangement. Merely “accepting” this tender does not by itself oblige the Department to take 10,000 tons, nor does it bind A for the whole. It creates a standing offer. Contractual obligations arise only when the Department places orders/requisitions for specific quantities; each order produces a separate binding contract for that quantity. Until such an order, either side is not bound for the whole, and A may revoke the standing offer (subject to any supported term keeping it open).

Conclusion. Acceptance of a maximum-quantity (“up to 10,000 tons … if and when required”) tender does not create immediate contractual obligations for the entire quantity; obligations crystallise only on each actual requisition/order, each being a separate contract. If, however, the Department had committed to take the entire 10,000 tons, acceptance would have created a binding contract for the whole.

20 MARKS

“An advertisement inviting tenders is not an offer; the tender is the offer and a person making it may withdraw it before acceptance.” Examine with reference to the Indian Contract Act and the relevant case law.

Model answer —

Invitation to treat. By S.2(a) an offer is a signified willingness to be bound on assent. An advertisement inviting tenders does not express such willingness; it merely invites others to make offers. It is therefore an invitation to treat, and nobody can “accept” it to form a contract.

The tender is the offer. Each tender submitted in response is an offer made by the tenderer, containing the terms on which he will contract (see Union of India v. Maddala Thathiah). It becomes capable of forming a contract only when accepted by the inviting party in the manner discussed in that case (definite quantity → contract; maximum quantity → standing offer).

Withdrawal before acceptance (S.5; Rajendra Kumar Verma v. State of M.P., AIR 1972 MP 131). Under S.5, a proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer. In Rajendra Kumar Verma, a tenderer withdrew his tendu-patta tender before the tenders were opened; the State nevertheless opened and accepted it (it was the only one) and sought to recover on the tenderer’s refusal to execute. The High Court quashed the recovery: a person who makes an offer is entitled to withdraw it before acceptance is intimated to him, and a clause in the tender notice cannot take away that legal right. Once withdrawn, there was no offer and hence no contract.

Conclusion. The tender notice is only an invitation to treat; the tender is an offer revocable before acceptance; and the tenderer’s statutory right of revocation (S.5) cannot be ousted by a mere condition in the invitation.

20 MARKS

Critically examine the law relating to Government contracts with special reference to the concept of “provisional acceptance” and its consequences. (DU, Topic 3)

Model answer —

Introduction. Government contracts (tenders and auction settlements by the State) are concluded on the same principles as private contracts, but two features complicate them: (i) the State often reserves powers to reject tenders or cancel, and awards are subject to confirmation by superior authorities; and (ii) the courts are reluctant to interfere in the award of contracts except where the decision is arbitrary, unreasonable, mala fide or in disregard of mandatory procedure (see Kanhaiya Lal Aggarwal v. Union of India, AIR 2002 SC 2766; Tata Cellular v. Union of India).

Tender law & standing offers. An invitation to tender is an invitation to treat; a tender is an offer; and its acceptance creates a binding contract only for a definite quantity, otherwise a standing offer (Maddala Thathiah). The State’s reserved right to cancel operates on supplies not yet covered by a formal order.

Provisional / conditional acceptance. In auctions and tenders, the State frequently accepts a bid/tender “subject to confirmation” by a higher authority. The leading authority is Haridwar Singh v. Bagun Sumbrui (1973) 3 SCC 889.

Consequences of provisional acceptance (Haridwar Singh). A provisional or conditional acceptance (subject to confirmation) does not, by itself, conclude a contract. To have an enforceable contract there must be an offer and an unconditional acceptance; a person making an offer may withdraw it before acceptance in the absence of a supported contrary condition. As Williston explains, an acceptance “in escrow” (not to take effect until a contingency) binds neither party, and either may withdraw until the contingency occurs. Applying this, the Court held there was no concluded contract where (a) the confirming authority accepted a different offer (at the reserve price) rather than confirming the original bid, (b) that acceptance was never communicated to the bidder (not even put into course of transmission to him), and (c) the bidder had himself revoked his later offer.

Conclusion. Provisional acceptance in Government contracts is merely a step toward, not the creation of, a contract. A binding contract arises only when the competent authority gives an unconditional acceptance of a subsisting offer and that acceptance is communicated (or at least put into course of transmission) to the tenderer/bidder. Until then the bidder is not bound, and the State may not be compelled to settle with him.

20 MARKS

Explain how far a “bid” at an auction and the “fall of the hammer” are governed by the rules of offer and acceptance under the Indian Contract Act. What is the effect when the auction is held “subject to confirmation”?

Model answer —

Offer & acceptance in an auction. By S.2(a), a proposal is a signified willingness to obtain another’s assent. In an auction: the auctioneer’s request for bids and the putting up of lots are an invitation to treat (not offers); each bid is an offer made by the bidder; and the fall of the hammer is the auctioneer’s acceptance, which, in an ordinary auction, concludes the contract. The bidder may withdraw his bid before the hammer falls; the auctioneer is not bound to accept any bid (and, where there is a reserve price, may refuse bids below it). Once the hammer falls on an acceptable bid, there is a concluded contract on the bidder’s terms.

Auction “subject to confirmation.” Where the acceptance is expressed to be subject to confirmation by a higher authority, it is only a provisional or conditional acceptance and does not by itself conclude the contract. This is illustrated by Haridwar Singh v. Bagun Sumbrui (1973) 3 SCC 889: the bidder’s bid at ₹92,001 was provisionally accepted by the Divisional Forest Officer subject to Government confirmation, but the Court held there was no concluded contract — the Government never confirmed that bid (it instead accepted the bidder’s separate offer at the reserve price), that acceptance was never communicated to the bidder, and the bidder had revoked his offer. Following Williston and Somasundaram Pillai, a provisional acceptance cannot make a binding bargain; an “acceptance in escrow” binds neither party until the contingency (confirmation) occurs.

Conclusion. Bids and the fall of the hammer follow ordinary offer–acceptance rules; but an auction expressly subject to confirmation postpones the contract — there must be an unconditional confirmation of a subsisting offer, communicated to the bidder, before any enforceable contract arises.

20 MARKS

Write a short note on “Standard form contracts” / “Exemption clauses,” with special reference to Indian Airlines Corporation v. Sm. Madhuri Chowdhuri.

Model answer —

What is a standard-form contract? A standard-form contract is one whose terms are fixed in advance by one party (usually a large enterprise or a carrier) and are not open to negotiation by the other — e.g., tickets, insurance policies, rail/air carriage. The weaker party “adheres” to the printed terms (hence “contract of adhesion”). Such contracts are valid if the terms were reasonably brought to the notice of the other party and he accepted them.

Exemption (exclusion) clauses. These are clauses that exclude or limit one party’s liability. The courts construe them strictly: following Denning LJ in White v. John Warwick & Co. (cited in Madhuri), an exemption clause is construed, so far as possible, as excluding only strict liability and not negligence, unless the words clearly exclude negligence. The party relying on it must show the term was a term of the contract (adequate notice) and that the wording clearly covers the liability in question.

Indian Airlines v. Sm. Madhuri Chowdhuri (AIR 1965 Cal. 252). A passenger died in an air crash; his widow sued for damages. The airline relied on an exemption clause in the ticket excluding all liability, even for negligence. The trial judge held the clause void (inter alia under S.23 ICA). The High Court reversed and allowed the appeal. It held: (i) the liability of a common carrier arises from public employment and is outside the Indian Contract Act — following the Privy Council in Irrawaddy Flotilla Co. v. Bugwandas, so S.23 ICA does not apply; (ii) under the common law a carrier may, by a clear and duly-noticed clause, contract out of liability even for negligence; (iii) there is no legal distinction between goods and passengers in this respect (Ludditt v. Ginger Coote Airways); and (iv) the terms were duly brought to the passenger’s notice. On the merits no negligence was established. The suit was dismissed.

Conclusion. A standard-form ticket binds the passenger on its printed terms where adequate notice is given; and — subject to clear wording and any statutory bar (e.g., consumer or carriage-by-air legislation) — an exemption clause may validly exclude a carrier’s liability, including for negligence.

20 MARKS

“Every agreement, the meaning of which is not certain, or capable of being made certain, is void” — with reference to S.29 ICA. Explain how certainty/definiteness is ensured in tenders and reward-style offers, citing the DU cases on this unit.

Model answer —

Section 29. S.29 provides that agreements whose meaning is not certain, or capable of being made certain, are void. An offer must be sufficiently definite to be capable of acceptance; vagueness prevents a binding contract. In tender practice this is why invitations require precise specifications (quantity, quality, price in words and figures, delivery dates, validity period).

Certainty in tenders (Kanhaiya Lal Aggarwal v. Union of India). The Railway’s tender notice required definite specifications and stated that non-compliance could lead to rejection. The appellant’s tender with a rebate scheme was held valid because the rates were clear and the acceptance-time clear — it did not render the offer uncertain. The Court also applied the essential-vs-collateral test (from G.J. Fernandez v. State of Karnataka): whether a condition is essential or collateral is judged by the consequence of non-compliance. A definite tender (for a stated quantity, price and period) is capable of acceptance and is not void for uncertainty.

Definiteness in the tender/quantity sense (Maddala Thathiah). Certainty also goes to quantity: a tender for a definite quantity is enforceable, whereas a maximum-quantity (“up to X, if and when required”) tender creates only a standing offer, each order being a separate, definite contract. The very reason the standing-offer doctrine exists is that there is no definite obligation until a requisition fixes the quantity.

Conclusion. Certainty (S.29) is satisfied where the terms are definite or capable of being made definite. Tenders that specify quantity, price, delivery and duration are enforceable; vague or merely discretionary (“if and when”) arrangements are not immediate contracts but standing offers requiring definite subsequent orders.

Memory kit for Topic 3.

6.1 One-line takeaway from each case

  • Maddala Thathiah — tender = offer; definite-quantity acceptance = contract; maximum-quantity acceptance = standing offer; each order = separate contract; cancellation clause covers only un-ordered supplies.
  • Rajendra Kumar Verma — a tender may be withdrawn before acceptance; a tender clause cannot take away S.5’s right of revocation.
  • Kanhaiya Lal Aggarwal — courts rarely interfere in Government awards unless arbitrary/mala fide; essential-vs-collateral condition test; a rebate offered with the tender is valid.
  • Haridwar Singh — auction “subject to confirmation”: provisional acceptance ≠ contract; needs confirmation of a subsisting offer + communication to the bidder.
  • Indian Airlines v. Madhuri — standard-form ticket binds if terms noticed; ICA (incl. S.23) does not govern common carriers; a clear exemption clause may exclude even negligence.

6.2 Quick formulas

  • Invitation to tender / auction notice → invitation to treat.
  • Tender / bid → offer (S.2(a)), revocable before acceptance (S.5).
  • Acceptance of definite-quantity tender → contract; of maximum-quantity tender → standing offer.
  • Fall of hammer → acceptance (subject to reserve & confirmation).
  • “Subject to confirmation” → provisional acceptance → no contract till confirmed & communicated.
  • Ticket with printed terms → standard-form contract binding if notice given.

6.3 Traps to avoid

  • Do not treat the advertisement inviting tenders, or the auctioneer’s call for bids, as an offer.
  • Do not say “acceptance of a tender always creates a contract” — for a maximum quantity it creates only a standing offer (Maddala).
  • Do not treat provisional acceptance in a confirmation-auction as a concluded contract (Haridwar).
  • Do not forget communication of the acceptance to the bidder — it is essential (Haridwar).
  • Do not apply S.23 ICA to strike down a common carrier’s exemption clause (Madhuri) — the ICA does not govern carriers; construe the clause strictly.
  • Do not say a tenderer can never withdraw — he may withdraw before acceptance, and a tender clause cannot remove that right (Rajendra).

6.4 Glossary

  • Invitation to treat: an inducement inviting offers (tender notice, auction).
  • Tender / bid: an offer to contract on stated terms.
  • Standing offer: an open offer to supply up to a maximum; accepted piecemeal by orders.
  • Provisional/conditional acceptance: acceptance subject to confirmation — does not conclude the contract.
  • Reserve price: the minimum below which an auctioneer may refuse bids.
  • Standard-form / exemption clause: pre-printed terms (often excluding liability) in a ticket/contract.
