2 Intention to Create Legal Relations

A pre-condition of enforceability — not written in S.10 but read into it from English law.

For an agreement to be a contract, the parties must intend that their agreement be attended by legal consequences. If there is no such intention, the arrangement is a mere social, domestic or moral agreement which the law will not enforce — even if it has all the other marks of a contract (offer, acceptance and even consideration). This requirement is not expressly stated in S.10 of the ICA; it is supplied by the common-law element of the statute and is best illustrated by Balfour v. Balfour (full digest in Case 3).

Atkin LJ, Balfour v. Balfour: such arrangements are “not contracts because the parties did not intend that they should be attended by legal consequences… each house is a domain into which the King’s writ does not seek to run.”

Commercial / business agreements

Presumption that parties intend to be legally bound. Clear and strong evidence is needed to rebut it. Example: a business agreement to supply goods is enforceable.

Domestic / social agreements

Presumption of no intention to create legal relations. Rebuttable by clear evidence. Example: a husband–wife allowance arrangement (Balfour) is not a contract.

Rationale

To keep the courts free from trivial disputes and to respect the parties’ own choice to keep an arrangement outside the law — honour, not the King’s writ, governs the family dinner-table.

Rebutting the presumption

Express words (e.g., “this agreement is legally binding”), formal documents, or surrounding circumstances indicating legal intention rebut the domestic presumption; a clear “honour clause” rebuts the commercial one.

How Balfour is examined: the question “X promises his wife £2,000/month; on divorce he refuses; can she sue?” — answer with Balfour: a domestic arrangement while spouses live in amity is not intended to be legally binding and is not a contract. If they are separated/divorcing and intend a binding agreement, the result may differ.

3 Offer (Proposal) & Invitation to Treat

What is an offer, what is merely an invitation, and the cases that draw the line.

S.2(a) — “Proposal”“When one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other to such act or abstinence, he is said to make a proposal.”

3.1 Essentials of a valid offer

  • It must be capable of creating legal relations (see §2).
  • It must be certain/definite in its terms (not vague) — terms must be capable of being accepted as they are.
  • It must be communicated to the offeree (S.3; also Lalman Shukla — no acceptance without knowledge of the offer).
  • It must be made with a view to obtaining the assent of the other party — i.e., a promise that the proposer is willing to be bound by on acceptance.
  • It may be made to a specific person or to the world at large (general offer — Carlill).
  • It may be express or implied (S.9) — from words or from conduct.

3.2 Offer v. Invitation to treat — the conceptual line

An offer is a definite willingness to be bound on acceptance. An invitation to treat (or “offer to receive offers”) is merely an inducement or invitation to the other side to make an offer; it is not itself an offer, so it cannot be “accepted” to form a contract. Everyday situations that are only invitations:

SituationNatureAuthority / example
Goods displayed on a shop shelf with a priceInvitation to treatPharmaceutical Society v. Boots — the customer makes the offer at the cash point; the shop-keeper accepts.
Goods in a shop window / catalogue / advertisementInvitation to treat (general)Fisher v. Bell; Grainger v. Gough; Harvey v. Facey (a price quotation is not an offer).
Auction “notice of sale” & lots put upInvitation to treat; each bid is an offerAuctioneer may refuse a bid; the fall of the hammer is the acceptance.
Invitation to submit tendersInvitation to treat; the tender is an offerStudied under Topic 3 (tenders).

Do not confuse: the shopkeeper displaying goods does not make an offer (Boots); but a person who issues a reward advertisement promising money for an act does make an offer to the whole world (Carlill). The difference is the nature of the promise: a specific reward promise invites acceptance by an act; a display invites negotiation.

4 Kinds of Offer

Classify offers — a favourite short-note / concept question.

Express offer

Made in words — spoken or written (S.9). Example: a written quotation letter.

Implied offer

Inferred from conduct or circumstances (S.9). Example: a bus plying on a route is an implied offer to carry passengers for the fare.

Specific offer

Made to a particular person; only that person may accept.

General offer

Made to the world at large; anyone performing the condition accepts (Carlill).

Standing / continuing offer

Remains open for acceptance over a period (e.g., an open tender). Each requisition/order operates as a separate acceptance.

Counter-offer

A reply which varies or adds to the terms of the offer. It rejects the original offer and amounts to a fresh offer (S.7 — acceptance must be absolute & unqualified). Example: “I’ll pay £30, not 30 guineas.”

Cross-offers

Two identical offers crossing in the post, each made in ignorance of the other. Generally no contract — neither is an acceptance of the other (no communication of acceptance).

Conditional offer

An offer subject to a condition (e.g., reward payable only if the act is done). The condition must be fulfilled to accept.

Fig 4.1 — A ready classification. Cite Carlill (general), S.7 (counter-offer), Felthouse (offeree’s option).

5 Acceptance & its Communication

S.2(b), S.7, S.8, S.9 + S.3–4 rules of communication — the cases of Carlill, Lalman, Bhagwandas & Felthouse.

S.2(b)“When the person to whom the proposal is made signifies his assent thereto, the proposal is said to be accepted. A proposal, when accepted, becomes a promise.”
S.7 — Acceptance must be absoluteIn order to convert a proposal into a promise, the acceptance must — (1) be absolute and unqualified; (2) be expressed in some usual and reasonable manner, unless the proposal prescribes the manner of acceptance.
S.8 — Acceptance by performing conditions“Performance of the conditions of a proposal, or the acceptance of any consideration for a reciprocal promise… is an acceptance of the proposal.”

5.1 Rules of a valid acceptance

  • Acceptance must be absolute & unqualified (S.7): a conditional or variant acceptance is really a counter-offer and destroys the original offer.
  • Acceptance must be by the offeree (and, for a general offer, by anyone with knowledge who performs): only the person to whom the offer is made can accept it.
  • Acceptance must be given with knowledge of the offer: no contract where the act was done in ignorance of the offer — Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Datt.
  • Acceptance must be communicated, unless the offer dispenses with it (S.3): the offeror may waive communication, as in a reward advertisement (Carlill — performance is enough).
  • Acceptance must be within a reasonable time (or within the time prescribed); otherwise the offer lapses (S.6(2)).
  • Acceptance must be in the mode prescribed, or a usual & reasonable mode (S.7(2)).
  • Silence is not acceptance unless the offeree does something — an offeror cannot impose a contract by saying “if I hear nothing, you have accepted” — Felthouse v. Bindley.
  • Acceptance may be express or implied from conduct (S.9).
  • Mental assent alone is insufficient: there must be an external manifestation of assent (per Bhagwandas — “a mere state of mind does not create a contract”).

5.2 Communication of proposal & acceptance — S.3 & S.4

S.3 — Communication by act or omissionCommunication of proposals, acceptances and revocations “are deemed to be made by any act or omission of the party… by which he intends to communicate… or which has the effect of communicating it.”
S.4 — When communication is complete • Communication of a proposal is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the offeree.
• Communication of an acceptance is complete — as against the proposer, when it is put in a course of transmission to him (out of the acceptor’s power); as against the acceptor, when it comes to the knowledge of the proposer.
• Communication of a revocation is complete — as against the maker, when put into course of transmission; as against the other party, when it comes to his knowledge.
Fig 5.1 — Two regimes of acceptance by S.4. Both are essential to answer Bhagwandas and short-notes on “communication of acceptance.”
When is acceptance complete? • Post/telegram: complete as against the offeror on posting/dispatch; as against the acceptor on receipt. • Telephone/instantaneous: complete when heard/received by the offeror (place of contract = where acceptance is received). • Face to face / by conduct: instantaneous — completes when communicated.

6 Revocation of Offer & Acceptance

S.5 & S.6 — when a proposal/acceptance may be revoked and how.

S.5 — Revocation of proposals and acceptances “A proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards.
An acceptance may be revoked at any time before the communication of the acceptance is complete as against the acceptor, but not afterwards.”
S.6 — Revocation how madeA proposal is revoked — (1) by the communication of notice of revocation by the proposer to the other party; (2) by the lapse of the time prescribed for acceptance, or (if no time) by the lapse of a reasonable time without acceptance; (3) by the failure of the acceptor to fulfil a condition precedent to acceptance; or (4) by the death or insanity of the proposer, if the fact comes to the acceptor’s knowledge before acceptance.
Fig 6.1 — Revocation timeline & S.6 modes. Note: an acceptance can be revoked only before its communication is complete as against the acceptor.
Key examination rule (post): B posts acceptance on Monday; A posts revocation on Tuesday which reaches B on Wednesday. Since acceptance was complete as against A on Monday (posting), A’s revocation is ineffective and a contract exists. But A may revoke his offer any time before the acceptance is posted (S.5 first para).

CASE 1 · Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.
[1891–94] All ER 127; (1893) 1 Q.B. 256 (Court of Appeal, England)
General offer to the worldUnilateral contractAcceptance by performanceConsideration
Hypothetical scene — the advertisement
Fig C1 — An advertisement promising a reward is an offer to the world; performing its conditions is the acceptance.
Facts

On 13 Nov 1891 the defendants (Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.) advertised in the Pall Mall Gazette that they would pay £100 reward to any person who contracted influenza (or a cold etc.) after using the Carbolic Smoke Ball three times daily for two weeks according to the printed directions, and stated that £1,000 had been deposited with the Alliance Bank to show their sincerity. The plaintiff, Mrs. Carlill, read the advertisement, bought a smoke ball from a chemist, and used it three times daily for several weeks as directed. She nevertheless contracted influenza on/about 17 Jan 1892 and claimed the £100. The company refused. She sued. Hawkins J. found for the plaintiff; the company appealed.

Issue(s)
  • Whether the advertisement was a binding offer or a mere “puff” with no contractual intent.
  • Whether an offer “to all the world” can be accepted to form a contract with anyone who performs.
  • Whether communication of acceptance was required.
  • Whether there was any consideration for the promise.
Arguments for the Company (defendant/appellant)
  • It was only a puff / advertising proclamation, not intended to create legal relations; no sane advertiser could intend to bind himself.
  • An offer cannot be made to the world — no “contract with all mankind.”
  • There was no notification of acceptance by Mrs. Carlill.
  • The promise was too vague (no time-limit for catching influenza).
  • There was no consideration — using the ball conferred no benefit on the company (a nudum pactum).
Arguments for Mrs. Carlill (plaintiff/respondent)
  • The deposit of £1,000 negatives a mere puff and shows genuine intention.
  • It was a reward offer to the world; whoever performs the condition accepts (Williams v. Carwardine).
  • Notification of acceptance is dispensed with — performance itself is the acceptance.
  • She used the ball and suffered the inconvenience/detriment at the company’s request.
Decision

Appeal dismissed — for the plaintiff (Lindley, Bowen and A.L. Smith LJJ).

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • A true offer: the deposit of £1,000 and the clear language of reward show it was an offer intended to be acted upon, not a puff.
  • Offer to the world: “It is an offer made to all the world… which is to ripen into a contract with anybody who comes forward and performs the conditions” (Bowen LJ). A unilateral offer — the offeror is bound once the condition is performed.
  • Acceptance by performance, no notification needed: where the offer expressly/impliedly indicates performance is a sufficient acceptance, notification is dispensed with (S.8 ICA reflects this: performance of the conditions is acceptance). The offeror gets notice of acceptance contemporaneously with performance.
  • Consideration: the plaintiff’s act of buying and using the ball as directed was a detriment/inconvenience suffered at the defendants’ request; and the company derived a benefit — the public’s increased confidence and sales. Ample consideration.
  • Vagueness resolved by reasonable/business construction — protection during use or within a reasonable time.
Bowen LJ: “It is not a contract made with all the world… the contract is made with that limited portion of the public who come forward and perform the conditions on the faith of the advertisement.”
CASE 2 · Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v. Boots Cash Chemists (Southern) Ltd.
[1952] 2 All ER 456 (QBD, Lord Goddard CJ)
Display of goods = invitation to treatCustomer makes the offerSelf-service shop
Hypothetical scene — the self-service shop
Fig C2 — In a self-service shop the customer, not the shopkeeper, makes the offer; the shopkeeper accepts at the till.
Facts

Boots ran a “self-service” shop. In the chemist’s department, goods containing poisons (within Part I of the Poisons List under s.18(1) of the Pharmacy and Poisons Act, 1933) were displayed on shelves, priced, for customers to pick up and take to a cash desk. The statute made it unlawful to sell such poisons except by or under the supervision of a registered pharmacist. A pharmacist was stationed at the cash desk. The question, by way of a stated case, was whether a sale occurred when a customer picked up the goods (in which case the unsupervised moment would be unlawful) or only when the money was taken at the cash desk under the pharmacist’s supervision.

Issue

Whether the display of goods on shelves is an offer by the shopkeeper (accepted when the customer picks up the goods), or merely an invitation to treat so that the customer makes the offer, completed only when the shopkeeper accepts at the till.

Arguments for the Pharmaceutical Society (plaintiff)
  • Self-service is a new mode of trading: exposing priced goods and inviting customers to “help yourself” is an offer to sell; picking up the item is the acceptance → a sale occurs on the shelf (hence not under supervision).
Arguments for Boots (defendant)
  • There is nothing revolutionary; exposure of goods is only an invitation to treat; the customer’s act is an offer to buy; the sale is complete only when the shopkeeper accepts — at the cash desk under a pharmacist.
Decision

Judgment for the defendants (Boots) — no unlawful sale on the shelf.

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • Exposure of goods with price tags is only an invitation to treat, not an offer to sell. The customer, by selecting the goods, makes an offer to buy; the shopkeeper is free to accept or decline.
  • Lord Goddard CJ: holding otherwise would produce absurd consequences — property would pass the instant an article is picked up, and a customer could neither change his mind nor be refused, nor could the shopkeeper reserve an article for another.
  • A self-service transaction is thus no different from an ordinary shop sale: acceptance occurs at the cash desk, under the pharmacist’s supervision, making the sale lawful.
Lord Goddard CJ: “The mere fact that a customer picks up a bottle of medicine from a shelf does not amount to an acceptance of an offer to sell, but is an offer by the customer to buy.”
CASE 3 · Balfour v. Balfour
(1918–19) All ER 860 (Court of Appeal)
Intention to create legal relationsDomestic agreementHusband & wife
Hypothetical scene — the marital arrangement
Fig C3 — A domestic allowance promise between spouses in amity is not intended to create legal relations → not a contract.
Facts

The Balfours married in 1900 and lived in Ceylon, where the husband was Director of Irrigation under the Government. In Nov 1915 the couple returned together to England on leave. In Aug 1916, when his leave expired, the husband had to return to Ceylon, but on medical advice the wife remained in England. Just before he sailed (8 Aug 1916) the husband orally agreed to pay her £30 a month as an allowance while she stayed in England, and she agreed to support herself without claiming maintenance. Their relationship later broke down; the husband stopped the payments; the wife sued for the allowance. Sargant J. at first instance held there was a binding contract supported by consideration and found for the wife. The husband appealed.

Issue

Whether an arrangement between a husband and wife living in amity — an allowance for the wife during temporary separation — constitutes a contract enforceable in law.

Arguments for the wife (respondent)
  • A definite bargain existed (parol evidence + letters re-affirming the £30).
  • She promised to support herself — there was consideration for the husband’s promise.
  • Contracts between spouses are capable of existing in modern law.
Arguments for the husband (appellant)
  • It was only a domestic arrangement between spouses in amity — no intention to create legal relations.
  • No real bargain by the wife; she never agreed to accept £30 in full satisfaction of his duty to maintain her in all circumstances.
  • To imply such permanent mutual obligations would be absurd.
Decision

Appeal allowed — no contract; judgment for the husband (Warrington, Atkin and Duke LJJ).

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • Intention to create legal relations is an essential element of a contract.
  • Agreements between husband and wife while living in amity, and other purely domestic/social arrangements, are not intended to be attended by legal consequences, and are therefore not contracts — even though consideration in form may exist.
  • Atkin LJ: such promises are governed by the “domestic code” of love and honour, not by the courts — “each house is a domain into which the King’s writ does not seek to run.”
  • Had the parties been separated and intended a binding agreement, the result might differ; here there was no such intention.
Atkin LJ: “They are not contracts because the parties did not intend that they should be attended by legal consequences… The consideration that really obtains for them is that natural love and affection which counts for so little in these cold courts.”
CASE 4 · Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Datt
(1913) XL ALJR 489 (Allahabad High Court, Banerji J.)
Knowledge of offer is essentialReward + pre-existing dutyNo contract
Hypothetical scene — the missing boy & the reward
Fig C4 — Performing the act without knowing of the reward gives no right to it; a pre-existing obligation also negatives consideration.
Facts

In January, the defendant’s nephew absconded. The defendant sent his servants in search, including the plaintiff, who was the munim (manager/servant) of his firm; the plaintiff was sent to Hardwar with money for fare and expenses. After the plaintiff had left, the defendant issued hand-bills offering a reward of Rs. 501 to anyone who found the boy (some hand-bills were later sent to the plaintiff at Hardwar). The plaintiff traced the boy at Rishikesh and informed the defendant, who brought the boy back. The defendant gave the plaintiff two sovereigns and later twenty rupees. On the plaintiff’s claim for the balance of Rs. 499, he alleged the defendant had promised the reward when sending him, but that allegation was found untrue — the hand-bills were issued after his departure. The lower court dismissed the claim; the plaintiff applied in revision.

Issue

Whether the plaintiff, who found the boy without knowledge of the reward offered by advertisement, is entitled to claim the reward on the basis of a contract.

Arguments for the plaintiff
  • Merely tracing the boy entitled him to the advertised reward; privity and even knowledge/motive were not essential.
  • Relied on Williams v. Carwardine and Gibbons v. Proctor — performance of the act alone suffices.
Arguments for the defendant
  • His claim can rest only on a contract, which needs an acceptance and an assent; there was none.
  • The plaintiff was already under an obligation (as servant sent to search) to do what he did, so his act was not consideration.
Decision

Claim dismissed; revision rejected — no contract.

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • An action like this can be founded only on a contract.
  • Knowledge of the offer is essential for acceptance. “There can be no acceptance unless there is a knowledge of the offer. Motive is not essential but knowledge and intention are.”
  • Where a reward advertisement is published and the act performed with knowledge of the offer, performance raises an inference of acceptance (S.8) and a claim arises — but not where the act preceded knowledge.
  • Additionally, the plaintiff was already under a subsisting obligation to search as the defendant’s servant; the performance of the act therefore could not be fresh consideration for the defendant’s promise.
Banerji J.: “In order to constitute a contract, there must be an acceptance of offer and there can be no acceptance unless there is a knowledge of the offer.”
CASE 5 · Bhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas & Co.
AIR 1966 SC 543 (Supreme Court of India)
Contract by telephoneInstantaneous communication rulePlace of contractS.3–S.4
Hypothetical scene — the long-distance telephone contract
Fig C5 — For an instantaneous medium (telephone), acceptance is complete where it is received by the offeror — the postal rule does not apply.
Facts

The plaintiffs (Girdharilal Parshottamdas & Co., Ahmedabad) sued the defendants (Kedia Ginning Factory & Oil Mills, Khamgaon) in the City Civil Court, Ahmedabad for Rs. 31,150, alleging the defendants had failed to supply cotton-seed cake agreed under an oral contract of 22 July 1959 negotiated by long-distance telephone. The plaintiffs claimed part of the cause of action arose at Ahmedabad because they had accepted the defendants’ offer there (the offer to sell having been accepted over the phone at Ahmedabad), and delivery/payment were tied to Ahmedabad. The defendants contended that the plaintiffs had offered to purchase and they (the defendants) had accepted at Khamgaon, so no part of the cause arose within the Ahmedabad court’s jurisdiction. The trial court held part of the cause of action arose at Ahmedabad. The matter reached the Supreme Court.

Issue

Where is a contract made when it is concluded by conversation over the telephone — at the place where the acceptance is spoken (Khamgaon, per the postal rule) or where it is received/heard (Ahmedabad)? This determines territorial jurisdiction.

Arguments for the defendants (appellants)
  • Ss. 3 & 4 apply uniformly to any mode of transmission; under S.4 the acceptance is complete as against the proposer once “put into a course of transmission,” so the contract is made where the acceptance was spoken — Khamgaon.
  • The English rules are inapplicable to our statutory scheme.
Arguments for the plaintiffs (respondents)
  • An offer (or a part of the cause of action) made at Ahmedabad gives jurisdiction.
  • A contract requires intimation of acceptance to the offeror, so the contract is made where that intimation is received — Ahmedabad.
Decision

Majority (per Shah J.): for the respondents — a part of the cause of action arose at Ahmedabad, where acceptance was communicated; jurisdiction of the Ahmedabad court upheld. Hidayatullah J. delivered a separate opinion.

Reasoning & Principle evolved (majority — Shah J.)
  • An agreement needs a real “meeting of minds”; there must be an external manifestation of assent, and acceptance must be communicated to the offeror unless waived or dispensed with by the course of dealing.
  • S.4 deals with the completion of communication, not the place; but it supports that a contract is complete when and where the acceptance is communicated.
  • The postal/expedition rule is an exception based on commercial convenience; it applies to post and telegrams where a third agency intervenes.
  • By telephone the parties are, in a real sense, in the presence of each other; communication is instantaneous, with no independent intervening agency. Hence the ordinary rule applies: acceptance is complete only when received by the offeror, and the contract is made where the acceptance is received.
  • Following Entores Ltd. v. Miles Far East Corpn. [1955] 2 QB 327, the place of contract for instantaneous communication is where acceptance is received.
Shah J. (adopting Entores): a contract made by telephone is complete only when the acceptance is received/heard by the offeror, and is made at the place where the acceptance is received.
Note — Hidayatullah J.’s separate view

Hidayatullah J. agreed in the result but reasoned differently: he read S.4 literally and felt that, once spoken into the telephone, the acceptance is “put into a course of transmission” as against the proposer, so the contract would be complete at Khamgaon. He enumerated four telephonic scenarios (fully heard; failure known to acceptor; failure not known and no repeat asked; failure notified) and treated only genuine effective communication as binding. However, the ratio laid down by the majority is the “reception rule” — accepted by later Indian and common-law authority as the law for instantaneous modes such as telephone and telex/fax/email.

CASE 6 · Harvey v. Facey
[1893] AC 552 (Privy Council)
Mere statement of price ≠ offerInvitation to treat / answer to enquiryNo contract
Hypothetical scene — the three telegrams
Fig C6 — Merely quoting a price in answer to an enquiry is not an offer; it leaves the negotiation open.
Facts

The appellants (Harvey & others, solicitors at Kingston) had been negotiating with the respondent, L. M. Facey, for the purchase of his property, Bumper Hall Pen. On 7 Oct 1891 they telegraphed Facey (travelling on a train): “Will you sell us Bumper Hall Pen? Telegraph lowest cash price — answer paid.” Facey replied: “Lowest price for Bumper Hall Pen £900.” Harvey then telegraphed: “We agree to buy Bumper Hall Pen for the sum of nine hundred pounds asked by you. Please send us your title deed…” Facey did not complete the sale, and Harvey sued for specific performance. Their Lordships had to decide whether these telegrams constituted a concluded contract.

Issue

Whether Facey’s telegram stating the “lowest price” (£900) was an offer capable of acceptance by Harvey’s third telegram, or merely an answer to an enquiry / information (an invitation to treat), so that Harvey’s third telegram was itself an offer not yet accepted.

Arguments for Harvey (appellants)
  • Facey’s telegram should be read as answering “yes” to the first question (willingness to sell) and stating the price — i.e., an offer to sell at £900, which Harvey accepted.
Arguments for Facey (respondent)
  • He had only answered the enquiry as to price; he never offered to sell; no acceptance by him occurred.
Decision

No contract — for the respondent (Facey).

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • The first telegram asked two questions: willingness to sell, and the lowest price. Facey answered only the price question.
  • A statement of the lowest price at which a person is willing to sell, in answer to an enquiry, does not amount to an offer — it does not bind the speaker to sell at that price to the enquirer; everything else is left open.
  • Therefore Harvey’s third telegram was itself an offer which required Facey’s acceptance to complete a contract. Since Facey never accepted, there was no contract.
  • Principle: a mere statement of price or of intention, or an answer to a request for information, is an invitation to treat, not a contractual offer.
Their Lordships: “The mere statement of the lowest price at which the vendor would sell contains no implied contract to sell at that price to the persons making the inquiry.”
CASE 7 · Felthouse v. Bindley
(1862) 11 CB (NS) 869 (Common Pleas)
Silence is not acceptanceNo communication of acceptanceOfferor cannot impose contract
Hypothetical scene — “if I hear nothing, the horse is mine” If I hear no more about him, I consider the horse mine at £30/15s.” Nephew intended uncle to have it, but never told him 25 Feb — auction Nephew sells farming stock by auction; told auctioneer (Bindley) the horse was “already sold” to his uncle, so auctioneer left it out & later sold it by mistake to another Uncle sues the auctioneer in conversion for the horse He must first show the horse was HIS property → fails No acceptance communicated → property never passed to uncle → NO CONTRACT
Fig C7 — An offeror cannot bind the offeree merely by saying “if I hear nothing I shall treat the offer as accepted”; silence is not acceptance.
Facts

The plaintiff (an uncle) had a nephew, John Felthouse, who owned a horse. In December 1860 they discussed its purchase — the uncle thought he had bought it for £30, the nephew that he had sold it for 30 guineas (£31.50). No complete bargain resulted. On 1 Jan 1861 the nephew wrote, clarifying that his price was 30 guineas. On 2 Jan the uncle replied offering to “split the difference” and said: “If I hear no more about him, I consider the horse mine at £30/15s.” The nephew did not reply. On 25 Feb, when the nephew’s farming stock was being sold by auction, the nephew told the auctioneer (the defendant, Bindley) that the horse was already sold to his uncle, so Bindley left it out of the sale — but later mistakenly sold it to a third person. The uncle sued Bindley (the auctioneer) for the horse/conversion, which required the uncle first to establish that the horse had become his property under a concluded contract with the nephew.

Issue

Whether there was a concluded contract between the uncle and the nephew so that property in the horse passed to the uncle before 25 Feb — in particular, whether the nephew’s silence after the uncle’s “if I hear nothing, I consider the horse mine” letter amounted to acceptance.

Arguments for the uncle (plaintiff)
  • The nephew, by telling the auctioneer the horse was “already sold” to his uncle, evidenced that he had accepted and intended the uncle to have it.
Arguments for the auctioneer (defendant)
  • No acceptance of the uncle’s offer was ever communicated by the nephew; the uncle had no right to impose a sale by treating silence as acceptance.
Decision

For the defendant — no contract; the horse never became the uncle’s property.

Reasoning & Principle evolved
  • The uncle had no right to impose upon the nephew a sale of the horse merely by saying that, unless he wrote to repudiate, the uncle would consider the horse his.
  • The nephew’s mere intention in his own mind (even telling the auctioneer the horse was sold) was not communicated to the uncle and could not bind him.
  • Acceptance of an offer must be communicated to the offeror; silence by the offeree is not acceptance (unless the offeree does something, or the offer contemplates acceptance by conduct).
  • Property in the horse therefore never passed to the uncle, so he had no title to sue the auctioneer.
Willes J.: “the uncle had no right to impose upon the nephew a sale of his horse for £30/15s. unless he chose to comply with the condition of writing to repudiate the offer.”

8 Previous-Year Questions (PYQs) + Best Model Answers

DU-semester style 20-mark questions for Topic 2 with complete model answers.

20 MARKS

“Where a person in an offer made by him expressly or impliedly intimates that the performance of a particular condition is a sufficient acceptance, performance is enough without communicating acceptance.” Explain with reference to the facts and decision in Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.

Model answer —

Introductory proposition. As a general rule an acceptance of an offer is effective only when it is communicated to the offeror — this is the requirement of consensus (S.3 ICA: communication by act/omission). But the offeror may waive communication of acceptance, expressly or impliedly. Where the offer intimates that performance of its condition is sufficient, no separate notification is needed; the act itself is the acceptance.

Facts of Carlill. The defendants advertised a £100 reward to anyone who caught influenza after using the Carbolic Smoke Ball as directed, and stated £1,000 was deposited to show sincerity. Mrs. Carlill bought and used the ball as directed, yet caught influenza. When the company refused to pay, she sued.

The company’s contentions were that the advertisement was a mere puff with no contractual intent, that an offer could not be made to the world, that there was no notification of acceptance, and that there was no consideration. The Court of Appeal (Bowen LJ) rejected these.

Reasoning. (i) The advertisement was a unilateral offer to the world — a promise to pay anyone who performed the condition; the £1,000 deposit negatived “puffery.” (ii) Though made to all the world, it ripens into a contract with that limited class who perform the conditions. (iii) The nature of a reward offer impliedly dispenses with notification of acceptance — a reward offer is accepted by performance. As the DU case-material itself records (Bowen LJ in Carlill), where the offeror expressly or impliedly intimates that a particular mode (here, acting on the proposal) is sufficient acceptance, “performance of the condition is a sufficient acceptance without notification.” (iv) Consideration existed — the plaintiff suffered the detriment/inconvenience of buying and using the ball as requested, and the company benefited from enhanced public confidence and sales.

Decision & principle. The company was liable for £100. The principle — in a unilateral reward offer, acceptance is complete on performance of the condition, without any prior communication — is embodied in S.8 of the ICA (“performance of the conditions of a proposal… is an acceptance of the proposal”).

20 MARKS

Explain the difference between an offer and an invitation to offer. Is display of goods with price tags in a self-service store an offer? Discuss with the help of the Indian Contract Act and case law.

Model answer —

Offer. By S.2(a), a person makes a proposal when he “signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other.” It is a definite promise capable of being accepted; once accepted it becomes a contract.

Invitation to treat. This is not an offer but an inducement or invitation to the other party to make an offer. It lacks the definite willingness to be bound. Examples — display of goods, shop-window exhibitions, catalogues, auction notices, price lists.

The distinction. An offer, when accepted, concludes a contract; an “acceptance” of an invitation is itself only an offer. The test is whether the party is willing to be bound at once on assent, or whether he still leaves the negotiation open.

Display in a self-service store. In Pharmaceutical Society v. Boots Cash Chemists [1952], goods containing poisons were displayed on shelves, priced, in a self-service chemist. The Pharmacy Act permitted sale of poisons only under a registered pharmacist. The question was whether a sale occurred on the shelf (customer picking the item up). Lord Goddard CJ held the exposure of goods is only an invitation to treat; the customer, by taking the goods to the cash desk, makes the offer to buy; the shopkeeper accepts at the till, under the pharmacist’s supervision. So no unlawful sale occurred on the shelf. Holding otherwise would have absurd results (property passing the moment an article is picked up, and a customer unable to change his mind).

Answer. Display of goods with price tags in a self-service store is an invitation to offer, not an offer. The customer makes the offer; the shopkeeper accepts. This contrasts with a reward advertisement (Carlill), which is a true offer to the world.

20 MARKS

“A contract by telephone is complete when and where the acceptance is received by the offeror.” Discuss with reference to Bhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas & Co. and the relevant provisions of the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

Model answer —

Relevant provisions. S.3 (communication by act/omission) and S.4 (when communication is complete). For post/telegram, S.4 adopts the expedition rule: acceptance is complete “as against the proposer” once “put into a course of transmission.” But S.4 regulates the completion of communication, not the place of the contract; the ordinary law requires an intimation of acceptance to the offeror unless waived.

Facts of Bhagwandas. The plaintiffs at Ahmedabad sued for Rs. 31,150 alleging an oral contract of 22 July 1959 for cotton-seed cake negotiated over the long-distance telephone; they claimed they had accepted the defendants’ offer at Ahmedabad. The defendants said they had accepted at Khamgaon, so the Ahmedabad court lacked jurisdiction.

Arguments. The defendants invoked Ss. 3–4 uniformly (contract made where acceptance spoken — Khamgaon). The plaintiffs insisted an acceptance must be communicated to the offeror, so the contract is made where the intimation is received — Ahmedabad.

Decision & reasoning. The majority (Shah J.) held that the postal/expedition rule is an exception resting on commercial convenience for cases where a third agency (post office) intervenes. In a telephone conversation the parties are, in effect, in each other’s presence; the communication is instantaneous, with no independent intervening agency. Hence the ordinary rule applies: acceptance is complete only when received by the offeror, and the contract is made where the acceptance is received. This follows Entores Ltd. v. Miles Far East Corpn [1955]. Applying it, part of the cause of action arose at Ahmedabad (where the acceptance was heard), so the Ahmedabad City Civil Court had jurisdiction.

Note. Hidayatullah J. agreed in result but read S.4 literally to complete the contract at Khamgaon. The ratio governing instantaneous communication is the majority’s “reception rule,” now accepted for telephones, telex, fax and analogous instantaneous electronic media.

Conclusion. For a contract made by telephone, acceptance (and hence the contract) is complete where and when the acceptance is received/communicated to the offeror — the postal expedition rule does not govern instantaneous communication.

20 MARKS

Explain the concept of “intention to create legal relations” and discuss the circumstances in which an agreement is not enforceable for want of such intention. Support your answer with Balfour v. Balfour.

Model answer —

Concept. For an agreement to be enforceable as a contract, the parties must intend to create legal relations — that is, they must intend that their promises be enforceable by law. This is not an express limb of S.10 but is a foundational common-law element. Without it an agreement is a mere moral/social arrangement.

Presumptions. In commercial/business agreements the law presumes an intention to be legally bound; in domestic and social agreements it presumes the contrary. Both are rebuttable.

Balfour v. Balfour. A husband, returning to Ceylon on duty while his wife stayed in England on medical advice, agreed to pay her £30 per month, and she agreed to support herself. On the later breakdown of the marriage the husband stopped paying and the wife sued. The Court of Appeal held there was no contract: the parties — spouses living in amity — had never intended the arrangement to be attended by legal consequences. Atkin LJ stressed that such arrangements are outside the realm of contract; even if consideration in form exists, the spouses’ promises rest on love and honour, and “each house is a domain into which the King’s writ does not seek to run.”

Other situations without legal intention include social invitations, family arrangements about allowances or pocket-money, and agreements expressly stated to be binding “in honour only” (honour clauses). Where parties are separated/divorcing or use formal commercial documents, the intention to be bound may exist and the agreement may be a contract.

Conclusion. Intention to create legal relations is an indispensable element of a contract; where it is absent the agreement is not enforceable in law, however real it may be morally.

20 MARKS

“An acceptance, to be valid, must be communicated to the offeror, and silence does not amount to acceptance.” Examine the statement and illustrate with Felthouse v. Bindley and Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Datt.

Model answer —

General rule. An acceptance is a signification of assent by the offeree (S.2(b)); to conclude a contract it must generally be communicated to the offeror (S.3). A mental intention to accept or mere silent assent creates no contract. The offeror may, however, waive notification (as in reward offers).

Felthouse v. Bindley (silence not acceptance). An uncle, after offering to buy his nephew’s horse, wrote “if I hear no more about him, I consider the horse mine at £30/15s.” The nephew did not reply but privately intended the uncle to have it and told the auctioneer so. The Court held no contract: the uncle had no right to impose a sale by stipulating that silence would count as acceptance. Acceptance had never been communicated, so property in the horse never passed to the uncle. Principle: the offeror cannot force a contract on the offeree through his silence.

Lalman Shukla (no knowledge, no acceptance). A servant was already sent to search for his master’s missing nephew; a reward was advertised after he left. He found the boy and later claimed the reward. The court held a claim could rest only on a contract, and there can be no acceptance without knowledge of the offer. Since he acted before knowing of the reward — and was already under a duty to search — there was no contract. Principle: acceptance presupposes knowledge of the offer; mere performance without knowledge gives no right.

Conclusion. Both cases reinforce that a genuine acceptance must be a communicated, knowing assent in response to the offer; a silent or ignorant offeree cannot bind the offeror, nor be bound.

20 MARKS

Distinguish between an offer and a mere statement of intention/price. In what circumstances is a “general offer” made to the world capable of creating a binding contract? Support with Harvey v. Facey and Carlill.

Model answer —

Offer v. statement of price/intention. An offer (S.2(a)) is a definite willingness to be bound on acceptance. A mere statement of price, intention or information is not an offer; it is only an answer to an enquiry or an invitation to treat. In Harvey v. Facey, Harvey asked Facey “will you sell Bumper Hall Pen? Telegraph lowest cash price”; Facey replied “lowest price £900.” The Privy Council held that Facey had merely answered the enquiry about price — the statement did not bind him to sell, and Harvey’s “we agree to buy” was itself an unaccepted offer. Hence no contract.

General offer to the world. An offer may be addressed to the world at large. It becomes a binding contract with any person who, with knowledge of it, performs the stipulated condition. In Carlill, the £100 reward advertisement was held a true unilateral offer to the world; performance by Mrs. Carlill (buying and using the ball) was the acceptance; notification of acceptance was dispensed with; and the inconvenience/detriment she suffered supplied consideration.

Conclusion. A price quotation merely invites a further offer and does not bind; but a genuine reward-style general offer, accepted by performance, creates a valid contract — contrasting Harvey v. Facey (invitation) with Carlill (offer).

9 Rapid-Revision Pack

Memory kit for Topic 2.

9.1 One-line takeaway from each case

  • Carlill — reward advertisement = offer to the world; performance (not notification) is the acceptance; consideration lies in the act & benefit.
  • Boots — display of priced goods = invitation to treat; the customer makes the offer at the till.
  • Balfour — domestic/spousal arrangement lacks intention to create legal relations → not a contract.
  • Lalman — no acceptance without knowledge of the offer; pre-existing duty is not consideration.
  • Bhagwandas — telephone contract complete where the acceptance is received (instantaneous/reception rule).
  • Harvey v Facey — a mere lowest-price statement in answer to enquiry is not an offer.
  • Felthouse — silence is not acceptance; the offeror cannot impose a contract by “unless I hear otherwise.”

9.2 Mnemonic — “O-A-C-C-C-O”

Offer (S.2(a)) → Acceptance absolute (S.7) → Communication (S.3–4) → Consideration (next topic) → Capacity + Object lawful (S.10) — to form a CONTRACT. For Topic 2 specifically, remember the three “I” checks: Intention, Identify the offer, Intimate acceptance.

9.3 Traps to avoid

  • Do not call a shop display or a price quotation an “offer” — it is an invitation to treat (Boots, Harvey).
  • Do not apply the postal rule to telephone — use the reception rule (Bhagwandas).
  • Do not treat silence or a private intention as acceptance — communication is required (Felthouse).
  • Do not forget knowledge of the offer — an act done in ignorance of the reward yields nothing (Lalman).
  • Do not say all social promises are void — they are not contracts for want of legal intention (Balfour).
  • Quote S.2(a), 2(b), 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 exactly where you can.

9.4 Glossary

  • Proposal / offer: S.2(a) — willingness to do/abstain to obtain assent.
  • Invitation to treat: a step that invites the other party to make an offer.
  • Unilateral / general offer: accepted by performance of a condition by anyone (Carlill).
  • Expedition rule: postal acceptance complete on posting (S.4).
  • Reception rule: instantaneous-media acceptance complete on receipt (Bhagwandas).
  • Counter-offer: a variant acceptance that rejects and replaces the original offer (S.7).
