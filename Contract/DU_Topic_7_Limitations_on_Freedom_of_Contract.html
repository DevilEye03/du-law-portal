Freedom is the starting point, not the end point

Parties ordinarily choose whether to contract, with whom and on what terms. Yet private autonomy cannot license evasion of statutes, fraud, injury, immorality, socially harmful bargains, deprivation of access to courts, suppression of lawful livelihood, or bargains too uncertain to administer. Sections 23–30 operate as gatekeeping rules: some attack the object or consideration; others declare specified classes of agreement void.

Examination trap: “void = illegal” is false. Every illegal agreement is void, but every void agreement is not illegal. Gherulal Parakh is decisive: a wager under §30 is ordinarily void, not forbidden by law; therefore a collateral partnership may remain enforceable (subject to a local gambling statute).

The Dhurandhar refinement

The labels are functional, not magical. A complete nullity—e.g. an act wholly without jurisdiction—needs no setting aside and may be ignored collaterally. A voidable act is legally effective until avoided. Some acts loosely called “void” nevertheless require a declaration in practical operation. Ask: does the act have legal effect until a competent challenge succeeds?

3. Section 23 — lawful consideration and object

Rule: consideration or object is lawful unless (1) forbidden by law; (2) if permitted, it would defeat the provisions of any law; (3) fraudulent; (4) involves or implies injury to person or property; (5) the court regards it as immoral; or (6) opposed to public policy. In each case it is unlawful, and every agreement whose object or consideration is unlawful is void.

Object and consideration distinguished

Consideration is the price for a promise—the act, abstinence or counter-promise under §2(d). Object is the purpose/design sought to be achieved. They can overlap, but should be separately tested: an apparently lawful payment may finance an unlawful end.

1. Forbidden by lawExpressly prohibited by legislation, delegated legislation, or a rule carrying legal prohibition; commonly punishable acts. Example: payment to procure public employment (statutory illustration (f)).
2. Defeats a lawNot directly prohibited in words, but permitting it would circumvent the statute. Example: a revenue defaulter uses a nominee to repurchase an estate despite statutory disqualification (illustration (i)).
3. FraudulentThe agreement’s design is fraud. Example: agent secretly takes money to secure a lease from his principal—concealment defrauds the principal (illustration (g)).
4. InjuryAgreement involves or implies injury to another’s person or property, including civil wrong. Courts will not enforce a bargain whose promised performance is injurious.
5. ImmoralIn the DU-prescribed Gherulal analysis, precedents confine this limb principally to sexual immorality: future illicit cohabitation, brothel-facilitating bargains, concubinage.
6. Public policyPublic good/public interest—not the temporary preference of a government. It is cautious but capable of principled growth: Central Inland Water Transport.

Statutory illustrations — what each teaches

Consequences and restitution. Voidness does not automatically answer recovery. Consider §65 (“agreement discovered to be void” / contract becomes void), §64 (rescission of voidable contract), and the maxim in pari delicto. If a claimant must found the cause of action upon a serious illegality, relief is ordinarily denied. Central Inland, quoting Kedar Nath Motani, notes that trivial/venial illegality not required to be pleaded, or timely repentance before the unlawful purpose, may alter the result.

4. Public policy — disciplined evolution

Public policy is the bridge between freedom of contract and the community’s minimum standards. Because it can become an “unruly horse,” a court must identify demonstrable harm, constitutional values, precedent and proportionality—not personal morality.

Narrow view

No new head should be invented without established authority. Values certainty and judicial restraint.

Broad/evolutionary view

Existing heads can expand and a new head may arise where changed circumstances disclose incontestable public harm. Central Inland adopts principled evolution, guided by the Preamble, Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

Recognised or recurring heads

Trading with enemyStifling prosecutionMarriage brokerageTrafficking in public officeInterference with justiceCorruptionUnconscionable standard terms

Central Inland test (exam formulation). Where a standard-form term is imposed by a party with overwhelming bargaining power upon persons with little real choice, and the term is so unfair or unreasonable that it shocks the conscience and harms public interest, the term may be void under §23 as opposed to public policy. Inequality alone is not enough; context, real choice and substantive harshness matter.
POWER IMBALANCE + ABSENCE OF MEANINGFUL CHOICE + SHOCKING TERM + PUBLIC HARM → §23 VOIDNESS

5. Section 24 — partial unlawfulness and severability

Textual rule: if any part of a single consideration for one or more objects, or any one or any part of any one of several considerations for a single object, is unlawful, the agreement is void.

The section targets an indivisible bargain. Its illustration combines supervision of lawful indigo manufacture with an illegal traffic for one annual salary; because the consideration/object package is mixed and single, the whole agreement fails.

InseverableOne salary for lawful and illegal services; no principled allocation. Whole bargain void.
SeverableIndependent promises and apportioned consideration may permit the lawful part to survive, provided severance does not rewrite the bargain.

Blue-pencil analysis

  1. Can unlawful words/promises be removed mechanically?
  2. Does the lawful remainder remain grammatically and commercially complete?
  3. Is separate consideration attributable?
  4. Would enforcement preserve, rather than create, the parties’ bargain?
  5. Does the statute demand total invalidity despite textual severability?

6. Section 25 — agreement without consideration

General rule: agreement without consideration is void. Three statutory exceptions: (1) written and registered promise from natural love and affection between near relations; (2) promise to compensate for something voluntarily done for the promisor, or something the promisor was legally compellable to do; (3) written and signed promise to pay wholly or partly a debt barred by limitation.
ExceptionCumulative elementsTrap
§25(1)Writing + registration + natural love and affection + near relationshipNear relation alone is insufficient; absence of actual affection may defeat it.
§25(2)Prior act; voluntary for promisor (or legally compellable act); subsequent promise to compensate wholly/partlyDo not confuse with an act done at promisor’s desire, which may already be consideration under §2(d).
§25(3)Debt once enforceable but time-barred; express written promise; signed by debtor/authorised agent; whole or part specifiedOral promise is not saved. New promise supplies enforceability without fresh consideration.
Explanation 1Gift actually made remains valid between donor and doneeIt saves completed gifts, not unexecuted gratuitous promises.
Explanation 2Free consent + some consideration; inadequacy alone does not voidGross inadequacy is evidence relevant to free consent.

Donation/subscription problem

A bare charitable promise is normally unenforceable. If, at the promisor’s request and on the faith of the subscription, the promisee incurs liability or materially alters position, that act may constitute consideration. In an exam, do not merely say “promissory estoppel”: connect request, reliance and incurred liability to §2(d) and the DU consideration cases.

7. Section 26 — restraint of marriage

Rule: every agreement in restraint of the marriage of any person, other than a minor, is void.

Both total and partial restraints can fall within the section. The inquiry is whether the contractual term restrains the freedom of an adult to marry—not whether marriage itself is promised. A genuine condition in a transfer may raise distinct personal-law/transfer issues, but a contractual price for remaining unmarried is suspect.

Minor exception. The statutory words “other than a minor” mean §26 does not itself declare a restraint on a minor’s marriage void. That does not automatically make such an agreement valid: capacity, child-marriage legislation and public policy remain independently applicable.

8. Section 27 — restraint of lawful profession, trade or business

Rule: every agreement by which anyone is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind is, to that extent, void. Express exception: seller of goodwill may agree not to carry on similar business within specified local limits while buyer/successor carries on like business there, if the limits appear reasonable having regard to the nature of business.

Indian statutory wording is stricter than the broad English reasonableness doctrine. Reasonableness is expressly attached to the goodwill exception. Nevertheless, courts distinguish a restraint during the subsistence of an employment contract—often a legitimate negative covenant—from a post-termination non-compete, which generally collides with §27.

ClausePresumptive treatmentReason
Exclusive service during agreed termGenerally valid if not harsh/one-sidedProtects performance of the positive promise; Golikari.

Injunction checklist from Golikari

  1. Is the negative covenant confined to the contractual period?
  2. Does it protect confidential know-how or faithful exclusive service?
  3. Is it limited by time, geography, industry, role and duties?
  4. Would enforcement compel idleness or indirectly force specific performance of personal service?
  5. Is the contract unconscionable, excessively harsh or one-sided?
  6. Can the court grant a narrower injunction because the covenant itself is lawful?

9. Section 28 — restraint of legal proceedings

Void to the extent: (a) absolute restriction on enforcing contractual rights through usual legal proceedings in ordinary tribunals, or contractual limitation of the time to enforce; (b) extinction of rights/discharge from liability on expiry of a specified period so as to restrict enforcement.
Exception 1Future disputes may be referred to arbitration; only the award amount is recoverable.
Exception 2A written agreement may refer an already-arisen dispute to arbitration; arbitration law is unaffected.
Exception 3Written bank/financial-institution guarantee clauses may provide extinguishment/discharge after a period not less than one year from specified event/non-event.

The section distinguishes invalid ouster of courts from valid selection of one among otherwise competent courts. A clause cannot confer jurisdiction where none exists, but where two courts are competent, parties may ordinarily choose one. Arbitration does not unlawfully “oust” courts; it substitutes the agreed adjudicatory route subject to arbitration legislation.

Limitation v extinguishment. The amended section attacks both clauses shortening the statutory time to sue and clauses ingeniously extinguishing rights/liability after a short period where the practical effect restricts enforcement. The guarantee exception is specific and should not be generalised.

10. Section 29 — ambiguous and uncertain agreements

Rule: agreements whose meaning is not certain, or capable of being made certain, are void.

Courts prefer construction to destruction: commercial context, prior dealings, trade usage, objective standards and a named third-party mechanism can make terms certain. But a court cannot make a contract for the parties.

11. Section 30 — wagering agreements

Rule: agreements by way of wager are void; no suit lies to recover winnings or money entrusted to abide the result of an uncertain event. Horse-race proviso: subscription/contribution toward a plate, prize or sum of ₹500 or upwards for winner(s) is not rendered unlawful. The provision does not legalise transactions hit by the penal provision referred to in §30.

Essential elements

Uncertain eventFuture event, or an event whose result is unknown to parties.
Mutual chanceEach side stands to win or lose depending on outcome.
Money’s worthPromise transfers value on outcome.
No controlNeither side should control the event in the relevant sense.
No interestParties lack genuine interest other than the stake.
Common intentionIn commodity contracts, both intend no delivery—only price differences.

Wager distinguished

TransactionWhy not (ordinarily) a wager
InsuranceInsurable interest, indemnity/risk distribution and genuine protective interest; not mere stake.
Contingent contract (§31)Uncertain event is collateral to a real transaction; parties have genuine interest in performance.
Commercial forward contractReal intention to give/take delivery. Speculation alone does not prove wager.
Skill competitionPredominance of skill can remove pure chance character; exact regulatory statute still matters.
Stakeholder/agent arrangementCollateral character must be separately tested; under general Contract Act rule, wager is void not illegal—Gherulal.
Local-law qualification. Gherulal itself notes that local legislation may make wagering/gaming transactions illegal (the judgment referred to the then Bombay position). Always test the governing State gaming law; §30 alone supplies the general void-but-not-illegal rule.

Material facts

Managers of two joint families agreed to partner in wagering contracts with two Hapur firms, transactions being made in the respondent’s name with profits and losses shared equally. The respondent entered 32 contracts with Mulchand Gulzarimull and 49 with Baldeosahay Surajmull. The net result was a loss paid by him. After an earlier arbitral recovery concerning part of the dealings, he sued for the remaining share/settled accounts of the dissolved adventure. The appellant resisted, principally asserting that a partnership formed to wager was unlawful under §23.

Procedural history

The Subordinate Judge treated the object as forbidden/opposed to public policy and dismissed. The Calcutta High Court held the partnership was between the managers, was a single-season adventure dissolved on completion, and that wagers, though void under §30, were not unlawful under §23; it decreed half the proved Baldeosahay loss (₹3,807-8-0 plus a small item). The appellant reached the Supreme Court.

Issues

  1. Was there in fact a common intention to settle price differences without delivery?
  2. Does §30 voidness mean the wager is “forbidden by law” under §23?
  3. Is wagering opposed to public policy or “immoral” within §23?
  4. Can the collateral partnership/accounting claim be enforced?

Appellant’s arguments

  • The partnership’s very object was wagering; since wagers are void under §30, the object was forbidden by law under §23.
  • Gambling’s social harm and condemnation in Hindu texts made it opposed to public policy and immoral.
  • Loss actually paid on wagers should not enter partnership accounts; the business was extra commercium.

Respondent’s arguments

  • §30 deliberately says “void,” not “illegal” or “forbidden”; the Act distinguishes unenforceability from prohibition.
  • Historically, collateral transactions connected with wagers remained enforceable unless a specific statute declared gaming illegal.
  • No settled public-policy head or contractual meaning of immorality covered wagers; courts should not legislate.
  • The completed adventure had dissolved under partnership law; accounting between partners was collateral.

Decision and reasoning

The Court accepted the concurrent factual finding that parties intended differences only. It nevertheless dismissed the appeal. A thing merely declared void is not necessarily forbidden. §30 prevents direct enforcement of the wager but, under the general Indian rule, does not attach illegality to collateral dealings. Prior Indian cases allowed agents/partners/lenders to recover in collateral arrangements.

Public policy must be invoked with caution. No English or Indian common-law rule had treated wagers as against public policy; State practice had long tolerated/regulate them. Even if a new head may exceptionally be evolved where incontestable social harm exists, wagering’s prohibition was for the legislature. “Immoral,” read in its statutory and precedential setting, had been confined to sexual immorality; it could not be expanded ad hoc to wagering.

Ratio:
A wagering agreement is void and directly unenforceable under §30, but is not, merely for that reason, “forbidden by law,” immoral, or opposed to public policy under §23. Therefore, absent a special local prohibition, a collateral agreement such as the partnership may be enforceable.

Principles evolved / exam use

  • Void and illegal are not synonyms.
  • Collateral transactions survive a merely void wager; illegality may taint them.
  • Public policy can evolve only cautiously and on compelling social grounds.
  • For forward trades, prove common intention that delivery would never be demanded.

Facts

Century acquired exclusive tyre-cord know-how from Dutch and German collaborators for 140,000 Deutsche Marks and promised secrecy, including employee undertakings. Golikari accepted a five-year Shift Supervisor appointment (joined 16 March 1963). Clauses required exclusive service, confidentiality during and after employment, and, if he left in breach before five years, no competing business/service for the remainder, six months’ salary as liquidated damages, and training reimbursement. He received specialised training March–December 1963. In October–November 1964 he left and accepted higher-paid work with Rajasthan Rayon, a tyre-cord competitor.

Claims and defence

Century sought damages, a time-limited injunction against service in competing tyre-cord work until 15 March 1968, and perpetual secrecy relief. Golikari said he was merely a labour supervisor; Clauses 9 and 17 were overwide, oppressive, unconscionable and contrary to §§27/public policy. At High Court stage, coercion/undue-influence pleas were abandoned.

Arguments

EmployeeClause 17 prevented livelihood in any capacity; Clause 9 lasted indefinitely and covered all information; restraint exceeded legitimate protection and an injunction would indirectly compel service/idleness.
CompanyHe received costly confidential technical knowledge, left during the agreed five-year term solely for a rival’s higher pay, and posed a real disclosure risk. The negative covenant supported a valid subsisting exclusive-service promise and the requested restraint was narrow.

Decision

Trial Court and Bombay High Court found specialised training and breach, and issued a tailored injunction limited to work as Shift Supervisor/substantially similar duties in continuous-spinning tyre-cord manufacture in India for the unexpired term, plus secrecy. The Supreme Court affirmed and dismissed the appeal with costs.

Reasoning

Restraints operative during employment differ from post-termination restraints. A covenant to serve one employer exclusively for a definite term, and not perform similar duties for a rival during that term, is generally not a restraint of trade under §27. It becomes objectionable if unconscionable, excessively harsh, unreasonable, one-sided, or if an injunction effectively forces idleness or personal service. Here time, field, role and area were limited; genuine trade secrets required protection. Where the covenant is not void, the court may grant only the limited injunction necessary; the rule against severing a legally bad covenant did not prevent calibrated relief.

Ratio:
A reasonable negative covenant operating during a subsisting employment term, protecting exclusive service and confidential technical know-how, is generally outside §27 and may be enforced by a suitably limited injunction.

Limits of the precedent

  • It is not a blanket approval of post-employment non-competes.
  • General skill and experience cannot be monopolised as “secret.”
  • Personal service is not specifically enforced; only a valid negative promise may be restrained.
  • Unconscionable duration or arbitrary employer power can defeat relief.

Central Inland Water Transport Corp. v. Brojo Nath Ganguly

Facts

Brojo Nath Ganguly and Tarun Kanti Sengupta were employees of the predecessor company whose services were taken over by the Corporation in 1967. The 1979 Discipline and Appeal Rules governed managerial employees. Rule 9(i) allowed either side to terminate permanent employment on three months’ notice or pay in lieu, without stated cause. Although detailed disciplinary machinery existed, the Corporation served allegations, received/solicited explanations, held no inquiry, and terminated both men on 26 February 1983 under Rule 9(i).

Issues

  1. Could a permanent employee’s service be ended under an unrestricted notice clause despite disciplinary safeguards?
  2. Was Rule 9(i) unconscionable because of gross inequality of bargaining power?
  3. Should such a clause be merely voidable, or void under §23 as opposed to public policy?

Corporation’s arguments

  • Mutuality saved the rule: employee and employer each had a three-month termination right.
  • Unconscionability was not an independent ground stated in the Contract Act; the employees accepted service rules.
  • Notice termination was distinct from punitive dismissal; managerial efficiency required flexibility.

Employees’ arguments

  • The rules were imposed, not negotiated; permanent officers had no meaningful option and vastly weaker bargaining power.
  • The rule conferred naked “hire and fire” power, bypassed inquiry, gave no reason or hearing, and created insecurity.
  • As an instrumentality of the State, the Corporation was bound by constitutional non-arbitrariness; the clause was unfair, unreasonable and against public policy.

Decision and reasoning

Rule 9(i) was void under §23 and constitutionally impermissible. Formal mutuality was unreal: a corporation could readily replace an officer; the officer could not comparably replace livelihood. Standard-form service rules affected a large class lacking negotiation or union protection. A clause allowing termination of permanent employment without reason or procedure was against right and reason, shocked the conscience, harmed public interest and generated insecurity.

The Court rejected a frozen public-policy doctrine. Public policy concerns public good, not government policy, and can develop with social conditions. Constitutional values provide a beacon where precedent is incomplete. If such mass standard terms were merely voidable, every weaker party would need separate litigation; declaring the offensive term void better protected public interest.

Ratio:
An unfair and unreasonable standard-form clause imposed where bargaining power is grossly unequal, leaving no meaningful choice and affecting public interest, can be declared void under §23 as opposed to public policy. Rule 9(i)’s arbitrary termination power met that test.

Doctrinal cautions

  • The judgment does not invalidate every adhesion contract or every unequal bargain.
  • The principle is especially strong for necessities, employment and public-sector/instrumentality contexts.
  • Substantive harshness, absence of real choice and public impact must be demonstrated.
  • §23 and Article 14 reasoning reinforce one another where “State” action is involved.

Facts and procedural setting

During pending litigation, the college was taken over as a constituent unit and its assets/liabilities devolved upon the University. The successor was not brought on record under Order XXII Rule 10 CPC. A decree was later sought to be executed against the successor University. The High Court accepted an objection under §47 CPC that the decree was inexecutable because the University had not been impleaded—treating it as akin to a decree against a dead/non-existent person. The decree-holder appealed.

Arguments

Decree-holderRule 10 devolution does not cause automatic abatement; proceedings can continue through the original party unless leave is obtained. The University, as successor to assets/liabilities, was bound. At most omission was procedural error, not inherent lack of jurisdiction.
UniversityIt was a necessary party after takeover; a decree against the ceased management, without successor substitution, was a nullity and could be resisted in execution under §47.

Decision

The Supreme Court allowed the appeal, set aside the High Court order and restored the executing court. Unlike Rules 3 and 4 (death), Rule 10 prescribes no automatic abatement for failure to seek leave after devolution of interest. The original proceeding could continue. A court with jurisdiction over subject matter and parties may decide wrongly; an erroneous decree remains binding until set aside and is not a nullity merely because of legal error.

Void/voidable taxonomy in the DU extract

  • True nullity: wholly without jurisdiction, void ab initio; no declaration needed; can be ignored in collateral proceedings.
  • Error within jurisdiction: authority had power but exercised it wrongly; decision is effective until reversed/quashed.
  • Some “void” acts needing action: practical legal effect may persist until an interested/protected person obtains a declaration.
  • Voidable act: a good act unless and until avoided; the temporal effect of avoidance depends on the governing doctrine.
Ratio:
Only a decree suffering inherent jurisdictional nullity may ordinarily be disregarded in execution. An error committed within jurisdiction makes the decree erroneous/voidable, not non-existent; it must be challenged through the prescribed direct remedy.

Exam use

Use this case to resist simplistic definitions. First classify the defect, then state the procedural consequence: direct challenge required, or collateral disregard permitted? The case’s contract-law relevance is conceptual; its actual setting is civil procedure.

13. Companion authorities named in the DU material

AIR 1991 SC 101 • Constitution Bench

D.T.C. v. D.T.C. Mazdoor Congress

Facts: Regulation 9(b) enabled DTC to terminate permanent employment on one month’s notice/pay, in addition to disciplinary provisions. Employees/unions attacked the unguided “hire and fire” power.

Rival positions: DTC invoked managerial flexibility and attempted to sustain/read down the regulation; employees argued that livelihood and public employment could not depend on arbitrary, reasonless discretion without hearing.

Holding/principle: By majority the provision was struck down as arbitrary, uncanalised and contrary to natural justice/Article 14. It reinforces Central Inland: a public employer cannot use a broad notice clause to bypass fairness. Where language plainly grants draconian power, “reading down” cannot rewrite it.

AIR 2003 SC 858

Bank of India v. O.P. Swarnakar

Facts: Nationalised banks introduced Voluntary Retirement Schemes. Employees applied, some attempted withdrawal before acceptance, while scheme terms purported to restrict withdrawal.

Arguments: Banks treated options as final/binding under scheme terms; employees invoked ordinary contract rules and the continued employer–employee relationship until competent acceptance.

Holding/principle: The scheme was generally an invitation to offer; an employee’s application was the offer and could ordinarily be revoked under §5 before acceptance. A term barring pre-acceptance withdrawal, in the relevant contractual structure, lacked independent support; employees who accepted benefits could not approbate and reprobate. The case demonstrates that even employment schemes are analysed through offer, acceptance, consideration and waiver rather than labels.

20-mark model
Dec. 2011: “Explain the difference between void agreement and void contract. What agreements have been expressly declared void by the Indian Contract Act?”

Model answer

Issue and definitions. Under §2(g), an agreement not enforceable by law is void. Under §2(j), a contract which ceases to be enforceable becomes void when it ceases to be enforceable. Thus a void agreement never matures into an enforceable contract, while a void contract was validly enforceable at formation but is discharged by a supervening event.

Distinctions. (i) inception versus subsequent failure; (ii) no contractual rights ever arise versus accrued rights may have arisen before discharge; (iii) restitution: §65 expressly addresses an agreement discovered void and a contract becoming void; (iv) examples: bilateral essential mistake/minority/unlawful object versus later impossibility or illegality. A voidable contract under §2(i) is different again: valid until the protected party avoids it.

Express void agreements. The Act includes: bilateral mistake as to essential fact (§20); unlawful object/consideration (§23); inseparable partial illegality (§24); no consideration subject to exceptions (§25); restraints of marriage (§26), trade (§27), and legal proceedings (§28); uncertainty (§29); wager (§30); and agreements to do impossible acts (§56, first paragraph). Other sections create voidness in particular contingent-contract settings (§§32, 35, 36).

Qualification. “Void” does not necessarily mean “illegal.” Gherulal Parakh holds a §30 wager ordinarily void but not forbidden under §23, so collateral arrangements may survive. Dhurandhar further teaches that a true nullity can be ignored, whereas an error within jurisdiction remains effective until set aside.

Conclusion. The correct answer classifies by source, timing and consequence rather than using “invalid” indiscriminately.

Short note
July 2010: “Distinguish between void and voidable agreements. Give two illustrations of each.”

Void: unenforceable by either party; usually no affirmation; third-party/collateral effects depend on illegality. Illustrations: (1) “100 tons of oil” with no means to identify type (§29); (2) adult paid to remain unmarried (§26). Voidable: enforceable unless protected party rescinds; affirmation is possible after vitiating factor ends; restoration follows §64. Illustrations: (1) consent caused by fraud (§19); (2) unconscionable transaction induced by undue influence (§19A). Dhurandhar: a voidable act stands until avoided; a jurisdictional nullity does not.

Short note
July 2010: “Essentials of a wagering agreement.”

A wager is a promise to give money/money’s worth upon determination of an uncertain event. Establish: (i) uncertain event; (ii) reciprocal chances of gain/loss; (iii) stake of value; (iv) neither has genuine interest apart from stake; (v) event is outside relevant control; (vi) in forward trades, common intention that no delivery occur and only differences be paid. Under §30 it is void and no action lies for winnings/stakes. Horse-race prize contribution proviso applies at the statutory threshold. Insurance differs by insurable interest; contingent contracts contain a genuine collateral interest. Under Gherulal, wager is generally void, not illegal, unless local law supplies prohibition.

20-mark model
2017 archival: “Discuss the exceptions to the rule that agreement without consideration is void.”

Rule. Consideration is the requested act, abstinence or promise under §2(d); §25 declares a no-consideration agreement void. First exception: a promise based on natural love and affection must be written, registered, and between near relations; all elements are cumulative. Second: a promise to compensate wholly/partly a person who voluntarily did something for the promisor, or did what the promisor was legally compellable to do. Third: written and signed promise by debtor/authorised agent to pay wholly/partly a debt formerly enforceable but now time-barred.

Explanations. Completed gifts remain valid; an executory gift does not. Inadequacy is not absence: a freely agreed sale for a low price remains a contract, though gross inadequacy may evidence want of free consent. Related doctrines: agency needs no consideration (§185); remission under §63 needs no fresh consideration; charitable subscriptions become enforceable where, at promisor’s request and on faith of promise, liability is incurred. Conclude by applying exact formal requirements, not merely fairness.

Problem answer
Dec. 2010: Murari owes ₹20,000, time-barred; signs a written promise to pay ₹15,000. Decide.

Issue: whether absence of fresh consideration defeats the promise. Rule: §25(3) validates a written, signed promise by the person charged/authorised agent to pay wholly or partly a debt which the creditor could have enforced but for limitation. Application: the old debt was once enforceable, is barred only by limitation, and Murari signed a writing specifying ₹15,000. This precisely satisfies §25(3). Conclusion: enforceable for ₹15,000; the remaining ₹5,000 is not revived absent a promise covering it.

Problem answer
2010/archival donation variant: subscribers promise donations; later refuse. Does purchase of building materials on faith of promises change the answer?

A mere gratuitous subscription is generally void under §25. If the promoter, at subscribers’ request or within the contemplated purpose, incurs a definite liability on the faith of their promises, that requested detrimental act supplies consideration under §2(d). Therefore, without action/liability the bare promises fail; after purchase/contractual commitment reasonably undertaken on faith of subscriptions, they may be enforced to the extent supported by the incurred liability. State facts showing request, reliance, causal connection and legal liability; moral reliance alone is insufficient.

Integrated 20-mark model
Likely doctrinal question: “Critically discuss public policy and unequal bargaining power with reference to Central Inland.”

Freedom of contract presumes meaningful choice. Section 23 withdraws enforcement where object/consideration is opposed to public policy. Public policy means public good and is not identical to a government’s current policy. The narrow school fears the “unruly horse”; the evolutionary school permits principled growth. Central Inland reconciles certainty and justice: courts should not invalidate merely because values differ or parties are economically unequal, but may strike down a standard term where gross power disparity, no meaningful choice, shocking unfairness and broad public injury converge.

Rule 9(i) let a State-controlled corporation terminate permanent officers with three months’ notice despite elaborate disciplinary safeguards. Formal reciprocity was illusory. Employees had no voice in the rules and loss of livelihood was not symmetrical with the corporation’s replacement power. The clause generated insecurity across a large class. It was therefore void—not merely voidable—under §23 and incompatible with Article 14. DTC Mazdoor Congress later reinforced this against an analogous unguided notice power. The doctrine should remain contextual: negotiated commercial bargains between sophisticated equals, reasonable termination structures and genuinely optional terms are not automatically unconscionable.

Integrated 20-mark model
Likely case/problem: Employee leaves mid-term for a competitor. Is the negative covenant void under §27?

Identify whether restraint operates during the promised term or after termination. Section 27 voids restraints of lawful trade, subject to statutory exceptions. Under Golikari, an exclusive-service covenant during a definite subsisting term is generally not restraint of trade where it protects performance and confidential know-how. Examine role, training, true secrets, duration, territory and scope; distinguish general skill. Ask whether the agreement is unconscionable or enforcement would compel idleness/personal service. A narrow injunction against substantially similar rival duties for the unexpired term may issue; a broad post-employment ban is ordinarily void to that extent. Confidentiality may continue for genuine secrets, but cannot sterilise experience.

Common mistakes

  • Writing five limbs for §23—the text has six distinct grounds.
  • Calling every §30 wager illegal, or assuming all stock/forward transactions are wagers.
  • Applying an English “reasonable restraint” exception generally to §27.
  • Forgetting writing and registration under §25(1), or writing and signature under §25(3).
  • Saying all access-to-court clauses are void; arbitration and exclusive competent-forum clauses require nuance.
  • Calling price incompleteness uncertain where an objective mechanism exists.
  • Treating inequality alone as unconscionability under Central Inland.
