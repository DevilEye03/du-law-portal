1 The Nature of Contractual Obligation

What law of contract is about — promises, obligations and why the law enforces them.

1.1 What is the “law of contract”?

As the DU course-material frames it, the law of contracts is one of the basic laws every law student studies — it touches equally the lives of ordinary persons and the activities of business. In substance it is the branch of law that deals with promises, their formation, performance and enforceability. The DU course captures this in a single sentence — it is the study of:

DU Course-objective formulation“This branch of law deals with law relating to promises, their formation, performance and enforceability. … this paper will include a study of general principles of contracts spelt out in Sections 1–75 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872.”

The field creates and regulates voluntarily-created civil obligations — a hallmark emphasized in the DU “Course Outcomes” (“the concept of voluntarily created civil obligations”). Because most commercial relations rest on contract, a sound grasp of its underlying principles is indispensable for every lawyer.

1.2 “Obligation” — the idea at the core

A legal obligation is a tie of law binding one person (the obligor / promisor) to do or forbear from doing something for another (the obligee / promisee). Every obligation is a jural relationship of right and duty: the promisee holds a right; correspondingly the promisor is under a duty. Salmond taught that a contract is precisely “an agreement creating and defining obligations between the parties.” A contractual obligation is thus a right in personam (available against a specific person) — unlike a right in rem which avails against the world.

Fig 1.1 — Every contract creates a legal obligation: correlative right (promisee) and duty (promisor).

1.3 Kinds of obligation — where contract sits

Not all obligations arise from agreement. Classically, obligations are divided by their source into four kinds. The law of contract governs only one of them — but the boundaries matter for a precise answer.

① Contractual (voluntary)

Created by the agreement of the parties. The promisor is bound because he freely chose to bind himself. — ICA 1872 governs.

② Quasi-contractual / Restitution

Imposed by law to prevent unjust enrichment — “obligations resembling those created by contract.” ICA §§ 68–72 (Ch. V). Not based on agreement.

③ Tortious (delictual)

Imposed by law for breach of a duty fixed by law (e.g., negligence, defamation) — not by promise.

④ Criminal / status obligations

Imposed on citizens generally by the State (offences, and duties arising from status such as parent–child).

1.4 Why does the law enforce promises? (theories)

Several theories explain why a bare promise should be binding. No single one is exhaustive; the Indian statute is a blend. Be able to name them:

  1. Will / Autonomy theory: A contract is enforced because it expresses the free will of the parties; the law gives effect to self-imposed obligation. (Links to ICA emphasis on free consent, S.14.)
  2. Promise / “Promise principle” theory: A promise binds because making a serious promise to another raises an expectation the other relies on; the promisor has voluntarily undertaken the duty.
  3. Bargain / consideration theory: Only promises given in exchange for something (consideration) are enforced; gratuitous promises are not generally enforced (S.25: agreement without consideration is void).
  4. Reliance / injurious-reliance (equity) theory: Promises are enforced where the other party has reasonably relied to his detriment (doctrine of promissory estoppel — an equitable supplement).
  5. Economic / utilitarian theory: Enforcing promises facilitates exchange, credit and planning, serving social and commercial welfare — which is why contract law is called the foundation of modern commerce.
Exam tip: Indian law gives primacy to the bargain theory (consideration, Ss. 2(d), 25) but qualifies the will theory through the doctrine of free consent (Ss. 13–22) and public policy (S.23). Always mention that “all agreements are not contracts” because enforceability by law is the deciding test (S.2(h), S.10).

2 From Proposal → Promise → Agreement → Contract

The conceptual “ladder” that links every definitional clause in Section 2.

The entire architecture of Section 2 is a staircase of concepts. Learn this sequence — it answers the classic DU question “All contracts are agreements, but all agreements are not contracts — comment.”

Fig 2.1 — Proposal + Acceptance = Promise; Promise(s) + Consideration = Agreement; enforceable Agreement = Contract. Not every agreement is enforceable → hence “all contracts are agreements, but not all agreements are contracts.”

2.1 The key “closed” statements to memorise

  • Every promise and set of promises forming consideration for each other = agreement [S.2(e)].
  • An agreement not enforceable by law is void [S.2(g)].
  • An agreement enforceable by law is a contract [S.2(h)].
  • An agreement enforceable at the option of one party only is a voidable contract [S.2(i)].
  • A contract which ceases to be enforceable becomes void when it so ceases [S.2(j)].
Classic result: Proposal + Acceptance = Promise; Promise + Consideration = Agreement; Agreement + Enforceability by law = Contract. Therefore a contract is an enforceable agreement; every contract is an agreement but the converse is not true. A social arrangement (e.g., a dinner invitation) is an agreement in common parlance but not a contract because the parties never intended legal consequences.

3 Essential Elements of a Valid Contract — Section 10

The statutory heart of “enforceability.” Section 10 lists the conditions that turn an agreement into a contract.

Section 10, Indian Contract Act, 1872 “All agreements are contracts if they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object, and are not hereby expressly declared to be void.
Proviso: Nothing herein shall affect any law requiring a contract to be made in writing, in the presence of witnesses, or any law relating to registration of documents.”

Section 10 itself names only the core conditions (free consent, capacity, lawful consideration & object, not expressly void). But a complete 20-mark answer should integrate the non-express essentials read into S.10 from the other provisions — two parties, offer & acceptance, intention to create legal relations, certainty, and possibility of performance.

Fig 3.1 — Ten essentials of a valid contract read from Ss. 2, 7, 10–14, 23, 25–30, 56.

3.1 The essentials explained one-by-one (with authority)

① Parties / Offer & Acceptance

There must be at least two parties — one making a proposal (S.2(a)) and another accepting it (S.2(b)). A man cannot contract with himself. Acceptance must be absolute & unqualified (S.7) and communicated.

② Intention to create legal relations

Parties must intend that their agreement create a legal obligation. Purely social/domestic agreements (husband–wife allowances) are not contracts — Balfour v. Balfour (see §8).

③ Consideration

At the desire of the promisor the promisee (or another) has done/abstains/promises something — S.2(d). S.25: an agreement without consideration is void (subject to 3 exceptions).

④ Capacity

S.11: competent = age of majority (Indian Majority Act, 1875), of sound mind (S.12), and not disqualified by law. Minors’ agreements are void — Mohori Bibee v. Dhurmodas (Topic 5).

⑤ Free consent

S.13 consent = agreeing on the same thing in the same sense; S.14 it is free unless caused by coercion (S.15), undue influence (S.16), fraud (S.17), misrepresentation (S.18) or mistake (Ss.20–22).

⑥ Lawful object & consideration

S.23: consideration/object is lawful unless forbidden by law, defeats a law, is fraudulent, involves injury, or is immoral / opposed to public policy — then the agreement is void.

⑦ Not expressly void

The agreement must not be one the Act expressly declares void — e.g., S.24 (partly unlawful), S.26 (restraint of marriage), S.27 (restraint of trade), S.28 (restraint of legal proceedings), S.29 (uncertainty), S.30 (wager).

⑧ Certainty & possibility

S.29: meaning must be certain or capable of being made certain; S.56: an agreement to do an impossible act is void.

⑨ Legal formalities (Proviso to S.10)

Where a special law requires writing, registration or attestation, these formalities must be complied with — e.g., S.25 contracts in writing & registered, transfer of immovable property, negotiable instruments.

Examiner’s favourite formula (S.10): “All agreements are contracts if made by (i) the free consent of (ii) parties competent to contract, (iii) for a lawful consideration and (iv) with a lawful object, and (v) are not hereby expressly declared to be void.” Quote it verbatim, then expand each limb.

4 History & Evolution of the Law of Contract

From Roman stipulation and the English action of assumpsit to the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

4.1 Why study the history?

Understanding history explains why modern Indian contract law looks the way it does: why consideration is central (England), why some promises were enforced without it (the sealed deed/covenant), why equitable remedies and “justice, equity and good conscience” matter, and why we have one unified Indian statute rather than many community laws.

4.2 The ancient foundations

No single civilization invented “the contract.” Each legal system recognised certain categories of binding promises long before a general law of contract existed.

  • Roman law: Early Roman law enforced only specific, formalised transactions. Later, the stipulatio — a formal question-and-answer promise — became enforceable. Roman jurists developed the idea of obligations (obligatio) and classified them by their source (contract, quasi-contract, delict, quasi-delict) — a scheme that later shaped common-law and Indian thought.
  • Hindu (ancient Indian) law: Before the Act, Indian contractual relations were governed largely by personal/community laws — Hindus by Dharmaśāstra-derived rules (the Vyavahāramayukha and other smṛti texts dealing with loans, deposits, sales, pledges), Muslims by Muslim law, merchants by trade custom and usage (protected even today by the saving clause in S.1). Ancient Indian texts emphasised free consent and consensus on terms; certain formalities were laid down.
  • Medieval English law: For a long time the common law enforced few promises. A promise under seal (covenant/deed) was enforceable simply because of its form. But simple or parol promises — today’s everyday contracts — were not enforceable until the writ of assumpsit developed.

4.3 The rise of the enforceable simple contract in England

  1. The old actions (debt & covenant): An action of covenant lay for breach of a promise under seal; the action of debt lay for a fixed sum owed, but required the actual giving of value, not merely a promise to pay in future.
  2. Slade’s Case (1602): The decisive turning point. A mere promise to pay for goods (an “executory” contract) became actionable in indebitatus assumpsit. Henceforth, reciprocal promises — even where nothing had yet been performed — were enforceable. This is the birth of the modern simple contract.
  3. The doctrine of consideration: As assumpsit grew, the courts required that a promise be bought — supported by consideration (something given, done or promised in exchange). Gratuitous promises came to be generally unenforceable, except deeds under seal.
  4. Form & evidence legislation: The Statute of Frauds, 1677 required certain contracts (e.g., guarantees, land, marriage) to be in writing — the origin of the “writing / registration” proviso in S.10.
  5. Equity: The Chancellor’s courts supplied equitable remedies (specific performance, injunction, rescission) where common-law damages were inadequate, and doctrines of good conscience (fraud, undue influence, restitution) which later reached India through English law.
  6. 19th-century rationalisation: English judges and writers (notably Anson, Pollock, Holdsworth as historians) systematised contract law around agreement, consideration and breach — the model India codified.
Sir William Holdsworth, History of English Law, Vol. III (p. 55), on how the common law & equity evolved through changing principle: a body of law that grows with the nation necessarily acquires fixed principles, yet must remain able to suppress practices that, “under ever new disguises, seek to weaken or negative them.” (Quoted in the DU casebook in the context of the expansion of doctrine.)

4.4 The road to the Indian Contract Act, 1872

  • Pre-1872 India: In the Presidency towns (Calcutta, Madras, Bombay) English common law applied; in the mofussil (interior), courts applied personal laws and, in gaps, “justice, equity and good conscience” (largely meaning English rules of equity & good conscience adapted to Indian conditions). There was no single, uniform law of contract — different communities were governed by different rules, and trade depended on local usage and custom.
  • Charter Act 1833 & the Law Commissions: The Charter Act 1833 set up the first Indian Law Commission to consolidate and codify the law. The First Law Commission (1834–35) and later Commissions worked toward codification.
  • The 1866 Commissioners’ draft: The Bill was based on “a report of Her Majesty’s Commissioners appointed to prepare a body of substantive law for India, dated 6 July 1866.” (See the footnote to the Act’s title.) The draft was discussed in Council (1867–1872) and vetted by a Select Committee (report dated 28 March 1872).
  • Enactment: The Indian Contract Act was enacted as Act IX of 1872 on 25 April 1872 and came into force on 1 September 1872 across the whole of British India. It was a codification of the English common law of contract, suitably Indianised.
Preamble, Indian Contract Act, 1872 (Act No. 9 of 1872) “Whereas it is expedient to define and amend certain parts of the law relating to contracts; It is hereby enacted as follows….”
Section 1 — Short title, extent & commencement; Saving “…It extends to the whole of India and it shall come into force on the first day of September, 1872.
Saving — Nothing herein shall affect the provisions of any Statute, Act or Regulation not hereby expressly repealed, nor any usage or custom of trade, nor any incident of any contract, not inconsistent with this Act.”

4.5 Original structure of the 1872 Act & later amendments

The original Act also contained chapters later hived off into separate statutes as commerce grew more specialised:

  • Chs. I–VI (retained in the parent Act): Communication/acceptance/revocation (Ss.3–9); Contracts, voidable contracts & void agreements (Ss.10–30); Contingent contracts (Ss.31–36); Performance (Ss.37–67); Relations resembling those created by contract / quasi-contracts (Ss.68–72); Consequences of breach (Ss.73–75).
  • Ch. VII (Sale of Goods) — repealed & replaced by the Indian Sale of Goods Act, 1930 (from 1 July 1930).
  • Ch. XI (Partnership) — repealed & replaced by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 (from 1 October 1932).
  • Chapters on Indemnity & Guarantee (Ch. VIII, Ss.124–147), Bailment & Pledge (Ch. IX, Ss.148–181) and Agency (Ch. X, Ss.182–238) remain within the Act.
Post-1872 supplementation (the statutes the DU syllabus lists as “Prescribed”): the Specific Relief Act, 1963 (equitable remedies — specific performance & injunction); the Indian Majority Act, 1875 (fixes the age of majority for capacity, S.11); and the Information Technology Act, 2000 (legal recognition of e-contracts and electronic records) — reflecting how the law of contract has evolved into the digital age (studied as E-Contracts, Topic 11).

6 Sources of Indian Contract Law & the Scheme of the Act

Where the rules come from, and how the statute is organised.

6.1 Sources

  1. The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — the primary, codified source for general principles (Ss.1–75 of present structure).
  2. Special statutes governing particular contracts — e.g., Sale of Goods Act, 1930; Indian Partnership Act, 1932; Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881; Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (contracts relating to immovable property are to be read as part of the Act in places where that Act is in force, per the note to the ICA title).
  3. Statutes supplementing capacity & remedies — Indian Majority Act, 1875 (S.11 capacity); Specific Relief Act, 1963 (equitable remedies); Limitation Act, 1963; Evidence Act, 1872.
  4. E-contracts law — Information Technology Act, 2000 (Ss.3–5, 10–17 etc.), which legally recognises electronic records & signatures.
  5. English common law & equity — the ICA is drafted on English lines; courts may look to English authorities “so far as they are applicable to Indian conditions” where the statute is silent (a principle echoed throughout the DU casebook).
  6. Usage & custom of trade — expressly saved by S.1 (e.g., the damdupat custom limiting interest, long recognised in Calcutta & Bombay).
  7. Judicial precedent — case-law (including the DU case-material cases) interprets and develops the statute.

6.2 Scheme of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (as it stands)

Preliminary — Ss.1–2

Short title, extent, commencement, saving (S.1); the master interpretation-clause defining proposal, promise, agreement, void, contract, voidable (S.2).

Ch. I — Ss.3–9

Communication, acceptance & revocation of proposals; when communication is complete; modes of revocation; acceptance must be absolute; express & implied promises.

Ch. II — Ss.10–30

Contracts, voidable contracts & void agreements: capacity, free consent, coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, mistake; what consideration/object is unlawful; void agreements (no consideration, restraint of marriage/trade/legal proceedings, uncertainty, wager).

Ch. III — Ss.31–36

Contingent contracts.

Ch. IV — Ss.37–67

Performance of contracts (obligation, by whom performed, time & place, reciprocal promises, frustration S.56, novation S.62, restitution S.65).

Ch. V — Ss.68–72

Certain relations resembling those created by contract (quasi-contracts): necessaries, reimbursement, non-gratuitous acts, finder of goods, money paid by mistake/coercion.

Ch. VI — Ss.73–75

Consequences of breach — compensation for loss/damage (S.73), penalty/liquidated damages (S.74), party rightfully rescinding entitled to compensation (S.75).

Chs. VIII–X

Indemnity & guarantee (Ss.124–147); bailment & pledge (Ss.148–181); agency (Ss.182–238).

7 Master Definitions — Section 2, Interpretation Clause

“Unless a contrary intention appears from the context, the following words are used in the following senses.”

S.2(a) Proposal

One person signifies to another his willingness to do/abstain from doing anything with a view to obtaining that other’s assent. (≈ an “offer”.)

S.2(b) Acceptance

When the offeree signifies assent to the proposal. A proposal when accepted becomes a promise.

S.2(c) Promisor / Promisee

The person making the proposal = promisor; the person accepting it = promisee.

S.2(d) Consideration

When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done/abstained, or does/abstains, or promises to do/abstain from something — that act/abstinence/promise is consideration.

S.2(e) Agreement

Every promise and every set of promises forming consideration for each other.

S.2(f) Reciprocal promises

Promises which form the consideration or part of the consideration for each other.

S.2(g) Void agreement

An agreement not enforceable by law — it is void from the start.

S.2(h) Contract

An agreement enforceable by law.

S.2(i) Voidable contract

Enforceable by law at the option of one or more parties, but not at the option of the other(s).

S.2(j) Void contract

A contract which ceases to be enforceable by law becomes void when it so ceases.

Memory hook for S.2(d) consideration: it must be at the desire of the promisor, may move from the promisee or any other person, may be past / present / future, and may be an act, abstinence or a promise. Every limb is examinable — e.g., “consideration must move at the desire of the promisor” (see Abdul Aziz, Topic 4) and “may move from a stranger” (Chinnaya v. Ramayya, Topic 4).

1 · Balfour v. Balfour (1918–19) All ER 860 (CA) · Topic 2 DU material · Intention to create legal relations
Facts

Mr. and Mrs. Balfour married in 1900 and lived in Ceylon, where the husband was Director of Irrigation under the Government. In November 1915 the couple returned together to England on leave. In August 1916 the husband’s leave ended and he had to return to Ceylon, but on medical advice the wife (suffering from rheumatoid arthritis) was to remain in England. Just before he sailed (8 August 1916) the husband orally agreed to pay his wife £30 a month as an allowance while she stayed in England, and she agreed to support herself without claiming maintenance from him. Later their relationship broke down, the husband stopped paying, and the wife sued him for the monthly allowance as money due under the agreement. The trial judge (Sargant J.) held there was a binding contract supported by consideration and gave judgment for the wife. The husband appealed.

Issue(s)

Whether an arrangement between a husband and wife living in amity — for an allowance while temporarily separated — constitutes a contract enforceable in law.

Arguments for the wife (respondent)
  • A definite bargain existed (letters and parol evidence); she promised to maintain herself in exchange for the £30/month — i.e., sufficient consideration.
  • Such agreements are capable in modern law of being contracts between spouses.
Arguments for the husband (appellant)
  • No contract in law was ever intended; it was merely a domestic/friendly arrangement.
  • No real bargain by the wife — no evidence she agreed to accept £30 in satisfaction of his duty to maintain her in all circumstances.
  • It would be absurd to imply such permanent mutual obligations.
Decision

Court of Appeal (Warrington, Atkin, Duke LJJ) allowed the husband’s appeal; judgment was entered for the husband. No enforceable contract existed.

Reasoning / Principle evolved
  • Intention to create legal relations is essential. Agreements that are purely domestic or social, or made between spouses living in amity, do not result in contracts even though consideration in form may exist.
  • Atkin LJ: “They are not contracts because the parties did not intend that they should be attended by legal consequences… such arrangements are outside the realm of contracts altogether.”
  • Spousal agreements are enforced by the domestic code of honour, not by the King’s writ — “each house is a domain into which the King’s writ does not seek to run.”
2 · Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (bridge case) [1891–4] All ER 127 (CA) · Topic 2 DU material · Offer to the world / unilateral contract
Why it is cited in “Nature of Obligation”

It shows that a promise addressed to the whole world can nonetheless create a binding obligation once a person performs the stipulated condition — illustrating that the essence of contract is a promise capable of enforcement, not a face-to-face negotiation. (Its full offer–acceptance analysis is examined under Topic 2.)

Facts

The defendants advertised that they would pay £100 reward to anyone who used their Carbolic Smoke Ball as directed and still contracted influenza, and stated £1,000 had been deposited at the Alliance Bank to show sincerity. Mrs. Carlill bought and used the ball as directed, yet caught influenza. When she claimed the £100, the company refused, arguing the advertisement was only a “puff”, not an offer to any particular person, and if an offer it was not binding for want of acceptance/consideration.

Issue

Whether the advertisement was a binding offer which could be accepted by performance, and whether there was valid consideration.

Decision & principle (CA — Lindley, Bowen, A.L. Smith LJJ)
  • The advertisement was an offer to the world, not a mere puff — the deposit of £1,000 negatived “puffery” and showed a real intention to pay.
  • It was a unilateral offer; performance of the condition = acceptance, and no communication of acceptance was needed.
  • Consideration existed in the inconvenience/act of using the ball as requested, and the company gained the benefit of the public buying & using the ball.
  • The company was liable for the £100.
Remember: Balfour is the key case proving that a valid consideration alone is not enough — there must also be an intention to create legal relations. This is why a “contractual obligation” is narrower than a mere moral or social promise. For a one-line classic on offer, quote Carlill: a general advertisement promising a reward is a binding offer accepted by performing its conditions.

20 MARKS

State briefly the purpose, evolution and nature of the law of contract (or: describe the history and nature of contractual obligation).

Model answer —

Introduction / purpose. The law of contract is the law of binding promises. Its purpose is to give legal effect to deliberately created obligations, so that a man is compelled to perform what he has led another to expect. As the DU course notes, it “deals with law relating to promises, their formation, performance and enforceability,” and protects voluntarily created civil obligations. It is foundational because ordinary life and nearly all commerce rest on enforceable bargains.

Evolution (history). Ancient systems enforced only selected promises: Roman law enforced the formal stipulatio; ancient Indian law applied personal/community rules (Dharmaśāstra & Muslim law) plus trade custom. In England a promise under seal was enforced by the action of covenant, and a fixed debt by the action of debt; but ordinary (“simple”) promises were enforceable only after Slade’s Case (1602) recognised assumpsit for executory promises. Consideration became the test of enforceability, the Statute of Frauds (1677) imposed writing for some contracts, and equity supplied specific performance and like remedies. In India, before 1872 there was no uniform law: the Presidency towns applied English law and the mofussil applied personal laws and “justice, equity and good conscience.” Codification came through the Law Commissions and the 1866 draft of Her Majesty’s Commissioners; the result was the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (Act IX), in force 1 September 1872, based on the English common law. Specialised parts were later split into the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 and the Partnership Act, 1932, while the Specific Relief Act, 1963 and IT Act, 2000 modernised remedies and e-contracts.

Nature of contractual obligation. A contract is “an agreement enforceable by law” [S.2(h)]. Not every agreement is enforceable: only an agreement that creates a legal obligation is a contract. A contractual obligation is a right–duty relationship in personam, voluntarily created, and enforceable by the courts. It is distinguished from obligations in tort (imposed by law), quasi-contract (imposed to prevent unjust enrichment, Ss.68–72) and criminal/status obligations. The essential conditions of enforceability are collected in S.10: free consent, capacity, lawful consideration & object, and not being an agreement expressly declared void.

Conclusion. The law of contract is thus the enforceable-promise law of a commercial society — historical in origin, codified in 1872, and ever-evolving — whose central idea is the voluntarily created legal obligation.

20 MARKS

“An agreement enforceable by law is a contract.” Define contract and explain the essential elements of a valid contract under the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

Model answer —

Definition. S.2(h) of the ICA defines a contract as “an agreement enforceable by law.” Salmond adds that a contract is “an agreement creating and defining obligations between the parties,” and Pollock stresses the element of enforceability. Hence Contract = Agreement + Enforceability by law. Every contract is an agreement, but not every agreement is a contract — a purely social arrangement creates no legal obligation.

Steps of formation. Proposal [S.2(a)] + Acceptance [S.2(b)] = Promise; promises + consideration [S.2(d)–(e)] = Agreement; Agreement + enforceability = Contract [S.2(h)].

Essential elements (S.10 read with allied sections). An agreement is a contract if made — (i) by the free consent of parties (S.14; consent not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake); (ii) by parties competent to contract (S.11 — majority, sound mind, not disqualified); (iii) for a lawful consideration (S.2(d)); (iv) with a lawful object (S.23); and (v) the agreement is not expressly declared void (Ss.24–30, 56). To these the courts add the non-express essentials: two or more parties; a valid offer and an absolute acceptance (Ss.7–8); an intention to create legal relations (Balfour v. Balfour); certainty of terms (S.29); possibility of performance (S.56); and compliance with any required legal formalities — writing, registration or attestation (Proviso to S.10). If any essential is absent, the agreement is void, voidable, illegal or unenforceable accordingly.

Illustration of the operation of S.10: A agrees to sell his house to B for ₹10,000 — there is offer, acceptance, lawful consideration and object; it is a valid contract (S.23 illus. (a)). But an agreement to discover treasure by magic is void for impossibility (S.56), and a wagering agreement is void (S.30).

Conclusion. The essentials of S.10 together determine enforceability; a contract is simply an enforceable agreement that satisfies them.

20 MARKS

“All contracts are agreements, but all agreements are not contracts.” Explain with reference to the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

Model answer —

Propositions. Under S.2(e) an agreement is “every promise and every set of promises forming the consideration for each other.” Under S.2(h) “an agreement enforceable by law is a contract.” Therefore a contract is that subset of agreements which the law will enforce.

Why all contracts are agreements: a contract cannot exist without an agreement — it is an agreement that has become legally enforceable. So every contract necessarily contains the element of agreement.

Why not all agreements are contracts: an agreement is a contract only if it is enforceable. An agreement fails to become a contract where — (i) it is made without consideration (S.25, with three exceptions); (ii) by an incompetent person, e.g., a minor (S.11); (iii) with an unlawful object or consideration (S.23); (iv) it is expressly declared void (e.g., restraint of marriage S.26, restraint of trade S.27, restraint of legal proceedings S.28, uncertainty S.29, wager S.30); or (v) the parties lacked an intention to create legal relations — e.g., a social/domestic arrangement (Balfour v. Balfour). Such an agreement is merely a void agreement [S.2(g)] and is no contract at all.

Illustration: A invites B to dinner and B accepts — there is an agreement in daily speech but no legal obligation and hence no contract. By contrast, A agreeing to sell his car to B for ₹2,00,000 is a contract because it is enforceable.

Conclusion. The test that separates an agreement from a contract is enforceability by law; hence while every contract is an agreement, the converse does not hold.

20 MARKS

Distinguish between (a) an agreement and a contract; and (b) a void agreement, a void contract and a voidable contract, with illustrations.

Model answer —

(a) Agreement v. Contract. An agreement (S.2(e)) is every promise or set of promises forming consideration for each other; it may or may not be enforceable. A contract (S.2(h)) is an agreement enforceable by law. Basis of distinction: (i) Definition — agreement is the broader genus; contract is the narrower enforceable species; (ii) Enforceability — every agreement is not enforceable, but every contract is; (iii) Legal effect — an unenforceable agreement is a void agreement (S.2(g)) creating no rights, whereas a contract creates rights and duties; (iv) Test — S.10 essentials must be satisfied for enforceability. Hence, all contracts are agreements but not all agreements are contracts.

20 MARKS

Explain the historical background and development of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, and state why it is described as a codification of the law of contracts. What has been the contribution of common law and equity to it?

Model answer —

Background. Before 1872 there was no uniform law of contract in India. Different communities (Hindu, Muslim) and different regions followed divergent personal and customary rules; in the Presidency towns (Calcutta, Madras, Bombay) English common law applied, while the mofussil courts administered personal laws and, in the gaps, “justice, equity and good conscience.” Trade and commerce therefore lacked a single, predictable legal framework.

Codification movement. The Charter Act, 1833 established the first Indian Law Commission to codify the law. After several Commissions and drafts — the bill rested on the report of Her Majesty’s Commissioners (6 July 1866) to prepare a body of substantive law for India, revised by a Select Committee (report 28 March 1872) — the Indian Contract Act, 1872 was enacted as Act IX of 1872 (25 April 1872) and brought into force on 1 September 1872 over the whole of India. Its preamble states that it was expedient “to define and amend certain parts of the law relating to contracts.”

Contribution of English common law and equity. The Act is a codification of the English common law of contract, which developed through the action of covenant (sealed promises), debt, and — after Slade’s Case (1602) — assumpsit for simple executory promises. Central English doctrines (the requirement of consideration, offer and acceptance, breach and damages under the rule in Hadley v. Baxendale) were enacted in the Indian statute (Ss.2(d), 3–9, 73). Equity contributed the ideas of good conscience — relief from fraud, undue influence, coercion (Ss.15–17), the void/voidable distinction, and equitable remedies later consolidated in the Specific Relief Act, 1963. The saving clause in S.1 preserves trade usage and custom not inconsistent with the Act.

Development since 1872. The Act’s specialised chapters were hived off: Sale of Goods Act, 1930 (Ch. VII) and the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 (Ch. XI). Capacity was aligned with the Indian Majority Act, 1875, and modern electronic transactions were recognised through the Information Technology Act, 2000. Because it restates the general principles of contract in one comprehensive code, the ICA 1872 is described as the foundation statute of Indian contract law.

Conclusion. The 1872 Act unified India’s fragmented contract law by codifying, in adapted form, the English common law of contract enriched by equitable principles — and it continues to govern general principles of contract to this day.

10.1 The three golden formulas

  • Proposal (S.2(a)) + Acceptance (S.2(b)) = Promise
  • Promise(s) + Consideration (S.2(d)–(e)) = Agreement
  • Agreement + Enforceability by law = Contract (S.2(h), S.10)

10.2 Mnemonic for the essentials of a valid contract

“O A I C C · F C L O P” — Offer, Acceptance (absolute), Intention to create legal relations, Consideration, Capacity; then Free consent, Lawful object & consideration, Otherwise not expressly void, Possibility & certainty (+ formalities).

Alternative — “2CC · F3L”: 2 parties, Consent (free), Capacity, then legal — Formality, consideration lawfulness, object lawfulness, and law-not-expressly-void. Use whichever sticks.

10.4 Common traps to avoid in the exam

  • Do not call a void agreement and a void contract the same thing — void agreement is void from the start (S.2(g)); void contract was valid but became void (S.2(j)).
  • Do not say consideration must move only from the promisee — S.2(d) allows it to move “from any other person” (used in privity exceptions, Topic 4).
  • Do not forget the saving of trade usage/custom in S.1 — a good extra point in a history answer.
  • Quote the exact words of S.2(h) and S.10 — examiners reward statutory accuracy.
  • Always tie the “nature” discussion back to enforceability and the difference between legal and merely moral/social obligations.

10.5 Glossary of the unit

  • Obligation: legal tie binding a person to do/forbear for another; a right–duty relationship.
  • In personam: right against a specific person (contractual rights are in personam).
  • Assumpsit / indebitatus assumpsit: the historical common-law action that made simple promises enforceable.
  • Covenant: promise under seal, enforceable at common law.
  • Consideration: the price (act/abstinence/promise) for which a promise is bought.
  • Codification: restating scattered/customary law into one enacted statute (the 1872 Act).
  • Void ab initio: void from the very beginning, of no legal effect.
